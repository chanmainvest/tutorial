## Part 2: YouTube Script

---

**VIDEO TITLE:** Private Markets — Where the Premium Goes (and Why It Isn't to You)
**RUNTIME TARGET:** ~12 minutes
**HOSTS:** Horace, Stella

---

**[INTRO — 0:00]**

[VISUAL: Title card "Private Markets: VC, PE, Private Credit, and the Illiquidity Mirage"]

**Stella:** Horace, every wirehouse pitch deck I've seen in the last
three years has the same line: "alternatives." Private equity,
private credit, real assets, infrastructure. Five percent here, ten
percent there. The implication is always: this is what the
endowments do, you should do it too.

**Horace:** And if you ask the wirehouse what *exactly* the
endowments do, they'll show you a chart of average PE returns. Then
they'll sell you something called BCRED or BREIT or an interval
fund and tell you it's the same exposure.

**Stella:** Which it isn't.

**Horace:** Which it isn't. The endowments commit to funds that are
closed to retail, run by managers who are closed to retail, in
vintages that retail can't pace properly. What's sold to retail is
the *spillover* product — and the spillover product has a fee
structure that eats the premium before it reaches you.

**Stella:** Today we're going to walk through the four pillars,
the J-curve, the 2 and 20, the smoothing trick, and what — if
anything — actually works at the retail level.

---

**[SECTION 1 — THE FOUR PILLARS — 1:30]**

**Horace:** "Private markets" is not one thing. It's four things
stacked together for marketing convenience. Venture capital — equity
in pre-IPO startups. Growth equity — minority stakes in profitable
private companies. Buyouts — leveraged acquisitions of mature
companies. Private credit — direct loans to middle-market borrowers.

**Stella:** Different return profiles, different risk profiles,
different fee structures. The only thing they share is they're
illiquid and they charge a lot.

**Horace:** Globally about thirteen trillion in assets across the
four. Up from one trillion in 2000. The growth has been spectacular,
which is exactly why every GP is now trying to push the product
into retail wallets — they've saturated the institutional market.

**Stella:** Quick numbers on each. VC: top-quartile funds compound
at twenty-five-plus percent, median is eleven-thirteen, bottom
quartile loses money. Growth: top quartile eighteen-twenty-two,
narrower distribution. Buyouts: top quartile fifteen-eighteen,
median around eleven. Private credit: ten-twelve at the BDC level,
loosely.

**Horace:** And those are *gross* numbers, before fees. The fees
are punishing.

---

**[SECTION 2 — THE J-CURVE — 3:00]**

[VISUAL: image/side14_pe_jcurve.png]

**Stella:** This chart shows the cash-flow profile of a typical
ten-year PE fund. Red bars are capital calls — money flowing *out*
of the LP into the fund. Green bars are distributions — money
flowing *back*. The black line is the cumulative net cash flow.

**Horace:** Year one through five — the fund deploys capital. You
get capital calls on ten-day notice. Years four through ten — the
fund harvests, exits portfolio companies, sends distributions back.

**Stella:** And the cumulative line traces a J. It troughs at about
sixty-five percent of committed capital underwater in year four.
You break even on cumulative cash around year six. The fund finishes
at plus seventy on a 12% IRR.

**Horace:** Two consequences. One: don't judge a fund by its
year-three return. The IRR is computed across the whole stream, and
the early years are noise. Two: institutional LPs commit to a new
fund every single year — that's how you build a "self-funding"
portfolio where last year's distributions roughly fund this year's
calls.

**Stella:** Retail can't do that. The minimum to commit to a real PE
fund is two-fifty-K per fund, you'd need ten years of two-fifty-K
commitments to build a proper vintage ladder, that's two-and-a-half
million in total. Most retail investors don't have that.

**Horace:** And so retail buys the wrapper that says "private
equity" on the box. We'll get to the wrappers.

---

**[SECTION 3 — THE TWO AND TWENTY — 5:00]**

**Stella:** Standard PE fee structure. Two percent of committed
capital each year as management fee. Twenty percent of profits
above an eight-percent hurdle as carried interest. Catch-up
provision usually gives the GP a hundred percent of profits between
eight and ten so they "catch up" to their twenty before the
eighty-twenty kicks in.

**Horace:** Apply that to a fund with a fifteen-percent gross IRR
over ten years. Roughly thirteen-fifteen percent of commitments
goes to management fees. The carry on the seven percent above the
hurdle eats another two-and-change. LP's net IRR: eleven-twelve.

**Stella:** Three or four percentage points of return per year, paid
to the GP, every year, in good times and bad.

**Horace:** And on a fund that produces eight gross — which is the
median these days — the LP nets five-six. Below the leveraged
small-cap-value benchmark. Below SPY. Paid for with a ten-year
lockup.

**Stella:** That's the deal. The premium isn't the LP's. It's the
GP's.

---

**[SECTION 4 — THE ILLIQUIDITY PREMIUM — 6:30]**

**Horace:** The marketing pitch is always: "you get an extra three
percent for the illiquidity." That number, 3% per year, comes from
Cambridge Associates and Burgiss comparing private equity IRRs to
the S&P 500 over thirty years.

**Stella:** And the academic critics — Phalippou, Stafford, L'Her —
have spent the last decade dismantling that comparison. Three issues.

**Horace:** One: leverage. PE portfolio companies run at one-and-a-half
to two-times debt-to-equity. The S&P 500 doesn't. If you delever PE
returns to match an unlevered benchmark, one-and-a-half points of
premium disappear.

**Stella:** Two: benchmark. PE funds invest in smaller-cap, value-tilted
companies. The fair comparison isn't the S&P — it's the Russell 2000
Value or a Fama-French replication. That benchmark compounded at
ten-eleven, eating another point or so of the premium.

**Horace:** Three: smoothing. Private NAVs are marked quarterly,
based on stale comp transactions, lagging the public market by two
to four quarters. Reported PE volatility nine-eleven percent. True
look-through volatility twenty-two-twenty-five. Once you de-smooth,
the risk-adjusted premium falls to roughly zero.

**Stella:** So the answer to "is there an illiquidity premium" is:
in theory yes, on paper somewhat, after adjustment essentially no.
For top-quartile managers there's still a real premium. For the
median fund — and especially for the retail wrapper of the median
fund — there isn't.

---

**[SECTION 5 — PSP, BX, KKR, AND THE LISTED PROXIES — 8:00]**

[VISUAL: image/side14_psp_vs_spy.png]

**Horace:** This is PSP — the Invesco Global Listed Private Equity
ETF. Holds about sixty listed private-equity-related stocks. Closest
thing to a PE index fund retail can buy.

**Stella:** Versus SPY since 2007. PSP launches into the GFC, draws
down seventy percent into 2009, recovers slowly. Compounds at about
six percent per year over the full window. SPY compounds at nine.

**Horace:** Three points behind. The premium that the marketing decks
promised? It's nowhere in this chart.

**Stella:** Where did it go?

**Horace:** It went to the GPs. Look at Blackstone's stock since
2007 — up roughly twelve-fold. KKR up nine-fold since its 2010 IPO.
Apollo, Carlyle, Ares — all multi-baggers. The fee stream has been
the trade.

**Stella:** Which is the actually defensible private-markets
position for retail. You don't own the underlying private deals —
you own the asset manager that charges the two-and-twenty. As global
AUM has grown from one to thirteen trillion, those fee streams have
compounded at twelve-eighteen percent per year.

**Horace:** Tranche-1 growth allocation. Small overweight. One-three
percent in the BX-KKR-APO-CG basket, or PSP itself if you want
diversification across the universe at the cost of one-and-a-half
percent expense ratio.

**Stella:** And the listed BDCs — ARCC, MAIN — go in tranche two
income. Eight-ten percent yields, monthly distributions, fully
liquid. Sized small because of recession tail risk.

---

**[SECTION 6 — THE WRAPPERS THAT DON'T WORK — 9:30]**

**Horace:** Last topic. The interval funds. BREIT. BCRED. The
non-traded BDCs and REITs that dominated retail private-markets
distribution after 2018.

**Stella:** Why don't they work? Three reasons. One: fees. One-and-a-
quarter management plus twelve-and-a-half percent incentive plus
financing costs plus operating expenses — typically four-five
percent of NAV per year before any LP return.

**Horace:** Two: gates. Quarterly redemption windows capped at five
percent of NAV. In late 2022, BREIT redemption requests blew through
the cap and the vehicle gated for fourteen months.

**Stella:** Imagine telling someone "this is liquid quarterly" and
then locking them out for fourteen months because you didn't want
to sell assets at the price they were actually worth.

**Horace:** Three: NAV opacity. The GP marks the book. There's no
market price. When the listed REIT index fell twenty-five percent in
2022, BREIT marked itself down seven percent. The smoothing finally
caught up with a ten-percent catch-up writedown a year later.

**Stella:** The listed equivalents — VNQ for real estate, ARCC for
private credit, BX for private equity fee streams — give you the
same exposure with daily pricing, lower fees, and no gates. Use
those.

---

**[OUTRO — 11:00]**

**Horace:** Bottom line. Private markets are a real thing. The
premium is real for top-quartile institutional access. That access
is closed to retail. The retail wrappers — interval funds, non-
traded BDCs and REITs — extract fees in exchange for restricted
liquidity and opaque NAVs.

**Stella:** What works for retail: listed alternative managers as
a small Tranche 1 growth allocation, listed BDCs as a small Tranche
2 income allocation, zero in non-traded products. Alpha is rare.
The investable universe is US-listed. Both
apply with extra force here.

load, a holding period. Watch what 2 and 20 does to your net
return. Then come back and ask yourself whether the ten-year
lockup is worth it.
