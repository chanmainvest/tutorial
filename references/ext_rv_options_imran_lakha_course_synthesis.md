## Part I — How the Options Market Really Works

### 1.1 What options are for

An **option** is the right, but not the obligation, to buy (**call**) or sell (**put**) an underlying asset at a fixed **strike** price, up to a fixed **expiry** date, paid for with a **premium**. Every plain-vanilla option must specify a strike and a maturity. Lakha's orienting metaphors:

- **Insurance.** A put is like house insurance: you pay a premium, and no one gives you protection for free. Index puts used to hedge a portfolio are genuinely insurance. And like insurance, "you don't wish that your house burned down" — a hedge expiring worthless can be a *success*.
- **Limited-time contract.** Options expire; if you still want the protection or exposure, you buy a new one (renew).

Why options instead of just stocks? Lakha: trading stocks (or "delta one" products) is "trading a bunch of 45-degree lines" — one-dimensional. Options have curved payoffs that change shape through time and with proximity to the strike, which lets you express views on **direction, timing, ranges, and market fear** — not just direction. The concrete uses he enumerates:

1. **Leverage without unlimited risk ("safe leverage").** Short on capital? "You can put $1,000 down. You can have a three-month view... and with that $1,000, you can really participate in the upside if you actually get it right." Futures give leverage too, but options tell you exactly how much you can lose and exactly how long you're in the trade — "almost fire and forget."
2. **Hedging/protection** without selling the underlying (avoids tax events, keeps fundamental positions).
3. **Income** (selling options against holdings or cash — overwriting/underwriting).
4. **Expressing volatility views** (long or short vol independent of direction).
5. **Multi-dimensional expression:** "You might be super bullish on a stock, look at the payoff diagram, and it says you'll make money if it goes up — but because of the Greeks, you might make no money if it goes up tomorrow. You'll only make money if it goes up *and stays up* in four weeks' time." Understanding the mechanics lets you match the structure to your strongest conviction.

Roger's summary of the flexibility: with a stock "you're either long or you're short, or you're not even in the game. With an option, you can be a little bit long, a little bit short. You can be taking some income in. You could be supercharged leverage. You could do your nuts if you get it wrong, admittedly."

**Terminology anchor set** (from the intro lesson): **strike** (the price at which you have the right to buy/sell), **maturity/expiry**, **premium** (what you pay), **open interest** (contracts that have changed hands and remain open — "for every buyer there has to be a seller"; high open interest at a strike means the dealer who provided liquidity "has got stuff to do there"), **in the money** (stock beyond strike), **at the money** (stock at strike), **out of the money** (stock short of strike), **skew** and **term structure** (defined in Part II).

### 1.2 The market makers — who they are and what they actually do

Market makers "are the beating heart of the options world. Without these guys, there would be no options market." Key points as taught:

- **They are volatility traders, not directional traders.** They provide liquidity to everyone else, hedge the options, and "turn them into a volatility position." They "can make money even if you are also making money, because this is not a zero-sum game."
- **Business model:** small margins on a large number of trades. They decompose the option price into component parts — **the Greeks: delta, gamma, theta, vega** — and try to neutralize those exposures across all strikes and maturities. "Ideally, a market maker wants to stay flat and just capture a bit of the spread by providing liquidity."
- **Who they are:** the biggest US listed-options names are **Citadel Securities, Jump Trading, and Susquehanna** — automated, high-frequency operations that lay off risk quickly across venues, shift prices in response to flows, and use advanced volatility modeling to price and hedge. Their holding periods are much shorter than an investor's because "they don't enter trades out of desire. They get put into positions, and then they try to manage that risk, or get the other side as quickly as possible."
- **Delta hedging mechanics** (worked through in the Lesson 4 discussion): you buy an Apple $100-strike call, one contract = the right to buy **100 shares** (the US standard multiplier). The market maker who sold it does nothing, he loses exactly what you make. So he neutralizes: an at-the-money option has roughly a 50 delta, so he buys **50 shares**. If Apple rallies and your option becomes a 60 delta, he buys another 10 shares; at 70 delta, another 10; if it goes to 100 delta (deep in the money) he holds 100 shares and "won't care what happens next." If instead you bought the **110 strike** and its delta is 20%, he buys only 20 shares — and if the stock goes nowhere, the delta decays and he gradually sells those shares down to zero by expiry: "So you lost your money, and I managed my risk." **Maturity matters enormously:** a 10%-out-of-the-money call with one day to go has almost no delta; the same strike with a year to go might carry a 20–25 delta (or higher), because delta is a probability of finishing in the money.
- **Once delta is neutralized, they are direction-agnostic** and their edge is in the other Greeks. Lakha: gamma and theta "go hand in hand, two sides of the same coin" — together they are your **realized-volatility exposure**. Delta is the Greek that matters *least* to a market maker ("that's not your edge"). The edge is "providing liquidity, looking at the surface, seeing where the relative value on the surface lies, and how that shape is potentially going to change," plus deciding where to be long/short gamma versus expected realized vol, and which part of the curve to own vega on.
- **They are not villains.** Roger raises the common suspicion; Lakha pushes back: "It's a fallacy to think they're up to something and always screwing the little man over... most of the time they're getting put into positions they don't even want because they just want to be flat by the end of the day." When a market maker keeps getting run over by the same flow, he **raises prices** to protect himself — that is the correct business response, not predation.
- **The meme-stock lesson (Jan 2021).** In the GameStop episode retail all decided simultaneously to buy calls, market makers got run over, "and market makers aren't stupid. Once they get run over once, they learn very quickly how to see the patterns." Since then dealers track Reddit chatter and squeeze candidates and charge a persistent **implied-vol premium** (Roger's illustration: realized might be 40 but they price 70, ready to jack it up further) "because how toxic that gamma can get if every man and his dog is buying call options at the same time."

### 1.3 The institutions — hedging demand and income demand

Institutions (asset managers, pension funds, insurance companies, mutual funds, absolute-return funds) are "one of the big drivers of all of the flows in the market." Their motivations:

- **Mandates and reporting.** Many are required to stay fully invested but have cautious short-term views; many mark to market monthly, so "a major macro event could create significant underperformance versus their peers." Some hedge for **capital-adequacy / regulatory requirements** — protection against a surprise 30% crash so the firm "can survive market shocks and live to fight another day."
- **Why hedge instead of sell?** The recurring question. Answers taught: (a) the stocks are hand-picked on a 5-year fundamental view — dumping them risks "being right about the macro and making no money" because the stocks outperform; (b) reducing exposure has **tax implications**; (c) institutions have monthly performance pressure and retail investors have portfolio stop-losses — hedging lets both keep their positions through macro risk. "Investing is all about time horizons. You may have a three-month bearish view on the market but a five-year bullish view on a handful of stocks. Options let you reflect both sides of this view at the same time."
- **Tenor by player type.** Insurers/pension funds play long-dated — 2-year, even 5-year options — for asset-liability and capital hedging. Run-of-the-mill asset managers hedge around events, some even using **weekly puts** when weeklies look cheap ("we're a bit worried about this number coming out next week").
- **Income generation.** "Systematic selling of options has a statistical edge that many of these firms are looking to capture so they can outperform their peers." In a world where bonds paid nothing, yield-hungry institutions sold short-dated options on both sides — the single biggest flow in the market (see Part V.3).
- **How these flows shape the surface** (the crucial structural point, repeated across lessons):
  - Persistent **demand for downside puts** (hedging) bids up low-strike implied vols.
  - Persistent **supply of upside calls** (overwriting for yield) presses down high-strike implied vols.
  - Together these create the **equity downside skew**, and Lakha calls the put-financed-by-call structure (the **risk reversal / collar**) "one of the most heavily traded option structures in the market and a big reason why an equity downside skew exists."
  - Long-dated buyers (pensions, asset managers) bid the back end of the term structure, while short-dated sellers (yield enhancers, overwriters, structured products supplying 5-year vols) press the front — so **the shape of the term structure is "massively impacted by the dominant players in the different parts of the curve."**

### 1.4 Hedge funds and retail — the speculative end

The course groups hedge funds and retail together because both mostly use options for **tactical directional views** with leverage, "and that's where some of the most exciting returns can be found."

- **Hedge funds** use directional options to supercharge highest-conviction ideas; "when fundamentals of a trade also line up with an attractive entry point via the options market, some of the potential returns can be spectacular." Historically (Imran's bank years) hedge funds were "toxic" counterparties for a different reason: they quoted 10 banks, traded with the one that gave the worst price, and the other nine then traded against the position — leaking the information. Over time institutions became options-savvy, "started to behave exactly like the hedge funds... in 10 times the size," and became, in his words, "probably an even more toxic client."
- **What retail actually do most:** "take a punt... they want the bang for their buck. They want to buy the option that's going to do 10x or 100x, and that tends to be the super short-dated, way out-of-the-money option... the probability of that is pretty low, and chances are you're just going to lose all the premium." Roger's addendum: don't bet on raw statistical odds — marry your own fundamental/technical view, your time horizon, and your budget to the expiry and strike, and "you might actually beat those odds."
- **"Retail is brave money, not smart money."** They coordinate (Reddit's WallStreetBets), take risks hedge funds can't (no investors to answer to), and became "a whale" in 2020–21: thousands of sub-$1,000 lottery-ticket call buys in the same strike/expiry aggregated into massive size.
- **How retail behavior changed after 2021:** much of the volume in meme-type names is now **intraday** — bought and sold back the same day, so volume explodes but **open interest barely moves**. And in the 2022 bear market, retail still needed educating on playing the downside (puts, put spreads) rather than only being bullish.
- **Lakha's two underused retail trades** (flagged in Lesson 2, demonstrated later): buying enough **time to be right** ("I'm not arrogant enough to believe my view happens next week... I buy myself some time... usually around four weeks" — he buys 4–12-week options), and **hedging with risk reversals** against existing winners (sell a call ~10% out of the money, use the premium to buy a put; "as long as you're happy to get rid of that position higher up, the risk reversal is a bit of a lay-up trade — you get the best of both worlds").

### 1.5 Market plumbing: contracts, expiries, liquidity, margin

- **Contract specifications differ.** US listed options are fairly standardized: **100-share multiplier**, monthly expiries on the **third Friday**, and the most liquid quarterly expiries (**March, June, September, December**). Europe varies by multiplier, expiry dates, and times. "You've got to make sure you know the specifications of the listed contracts you're trading."
- **US markets are "crazy liquid"** — billions of notional in some names — and have ETFs for all the macro instruments: **USO** (oil), **GLD** (gold), sector ETFs, index ETFs. Liquidity is best in the macro instruments; single stocks suit traders with micro views. Lakha is macro: "I focus on indices and ETFs."
- **Know what your option settles into** — cash, physical shares, a futures contract ("or a truckload of oil"). Settlement mechanics differ by exchange and product.
- **Margin** is money posted to cover potential losses on short positions. Selling outright (naked) options requires substantial margin and an account approved for it; selling **spreads (credit spreads)** caps the maximum loss and reduces margin. Retail accounts without **portfolio margin** (which nets Greeks across the whole portfolio) get no offsetting benefit between long and short legs — so a 1x2 ratio with a naked extra leg can require posting the whole account balance. Small accounts are effectively forced into safer structures (e.g., adding a far wing, turning a 1x2 into a 1x2x1 "call fly").
- **Broker tiering is real.** With ~$10k in an account, brokers impose trading-frequency restrictions and limits; more capital = more flexibility ("it's pretty unfair... but they're saying, we're trying to keep you safe"). Good retail platforms (Lakha names **Interactive Brokers**, **Tastyworks**, **TD Ameritrade**; UK traders often use US brokers for access) provide Greeks breakdowns and vol analytics — "if you haven't got Bloomberg, which most retail can't afford... you can analyze implied vol, term structure, skew, history, realized" in-platform.
- **Transaction costs matter.** High-frequency option churning builds up commissions and exchange fees; "you need a lot of edge and a pretty damn high hit rate to justify constantly churning your book." Lakha does not day-trade; he trades strategically on longer horizons.
- **Keep the book simple.** Even on the bank desk he always kept the book so "I could do five to ten trades and be out." You want to be able to answer: *what have I got on, why have I got it on, does it reflect my view on spot, on vol, and on time?* And when a position causes trouble, **the best hedge is usually closing part of it, not adding a new leg**: "If this position is giving you a problem, cut it in half, and it will give you less of a problem." A **margin buffer rule** (see Part VII) protects against requirement spikes — including market-wide ones, where after GME brokers doubled margin on shorts across *all* stocks and investors got carried out "not because the position went against you but because margin was increased."

### 1.6 Dealer positioning and how options move the underlying market

This is the material "you'll never see in books," and the heart of the course's market-dynamics claim: **options positioning can dampen, pin, or accelerate the underlying market.**

- **The volatility asset class.** Options are a market in volatility itself: "the decision to buy or sell options is determined by one's perception about the future volatility of an asset." Flows concentrate around hedging and income, creating structural supply/demand imbalances that show up as skew and term structure.
- **Pinning.** If, into a large expiry, dealers are net **long** options (long gamma) with strikes near the market, their delta hedging is *stabilizing*: "when the market rises, they must sell stock, and when the market falls, they must buy stock." If open interest is high, these flows "can overwhelm any other flow in the market and hold the market at certain levels until the options expire." Roger's framing: the stock "just oscillates gently around that place."
- **Magnitude matters.** Dealer gamma only matters relative to market volume: hedging that is 2% of daily volume is irrelevant; a monstrous single-stock position where dealers hedge "50% of daily volume... that stock's going nowhere."
- **Calibration is in gamma, not open interest.** A huge position 20% out of the money with a week left has essentially no gamma. Positioning analytics (e.g., **SpotGamma**, which Lakha recommends) convert open interest into **gamma exposure** to find stickiness/acceleration levels. The caveat taught: we only see open interest, never who is long or short; the working assumption is that **dealers sell puts to hedgers and buy calls from overwriters**, and it's often wrong around unusual flows or large OTC deals. Lakha cross-checks the models with implied-vs-realized vol: implied trading well above realized confirms the street is short vol; a very tight spread confirms long vol.
- **Street long gamma = pinning; street short gamma = acceleration.** When dealers are structurally **short downside options and long upside** (the normal state), an adverse move makes them shorter gamma/vega, forcing them to **sell futures into falling markets** (the only liquid hedge — buying options back is expensive when everyone is short). Into a big expiry loaded with those shorts, this can exaggerate the down-move; when the positions expire, "there's a relief... the market has a bit of a recovery and a bounce." Observed examples: **market lows on the first trading day after the December 2018 expiry and the March 2020 expiry**, where "the unwinding of the dealer inventory exacerbated the move through the expiry." Roger's paraphrase: "Options don't define the direction, but they accentuate the move."
- **Short-gamma pain in chop.** For a dealer (or anyone) short options — especially short-dated near-the-money — violent back-and-forth moves are "the worst nightmare": every rehedge costs money. If the market "moves violently, and then you hedge it all and then it rips back the other way, you have to buy it all back — arguably more painful than if it just goes down and stays down." Investors who can tolerate drawdowns can run short-gamma longer and let markets mean-revert.
- **The expiry-week cycle.** Monthly expiries (third Friday) and quarterlies (Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec) are calendar anchors. Lakha's 2022 statistic: **if you owned the S&P but closed it before expiry week and re-bought at the end of expiry week all year, you'd have been flat; buy-and-hold was down 20%** — "all the weakness in 2022 came from expiry week." Practical uses: hedgers can hold a longer-dated put and monetize into the expiry-week weakness (you do not need to own a one-week put to express a one-week view); cash holders can time bids around expiry; and everyone should watch whether the pattern persists — "these things can stop happening."

### 1.7 Crypto options — a new market with its own plumbing

- **Lakha entered crypto in 2020 in meaningful size**, as "a call option on the future of money" and a hedge against fiat debasement, analogous to gold. As a vol trader he was drawn in because "there's nothing better than a market that moves" — realized vol is high, and **vol-of-vol** is super high, with big swings in vol, skew, and curve shape driven by sentiment.
- **Venue:** **Deribit** has the lion's share of crypto options volume and the best liquidity; bid-offer is only **1–2 vol points**, tighter than many European single stocks. US retail can't use Deribit; US alternatives are **LedgerX** (regulated but poor liquidity), the **BITO ETF** (US-listed Bitcoin ETF options, launched late 2021, growing — same idea as USO/GLD), and **CME** micro Bitcoin/Ethereum futures and options-on-futures.
- **The "hockey stick from hell"** (Roger's coinage, endorsed by Lakha): on Deribit you post margin and receive premium **in crypto**, but a short option is a **dollar liability**. Worked example: Bitcoin at $20,000; you sell the at-the-money $20,000 put and receive Bitcoin premium. Bitcoin halves to $10,000. Your put is $10,000 in the money — but $10,000 was **half a Bitcoin** when Bitcoin was $20,000 and is **one full Bitcoin** at $10,000. An account funded with one Bitcoin is gone, whereas a dollar-margin account would have lost only half. Lesson: crypto margin embeds crypto exposure; hedge with futures and use the exchange's position-builder tools, or simply understand what you're shorting.
- **Crypto-specific phenomena:** crashes come with skew "bid like crazy" (max fear — see Part IV), and the **Ethereum Merge** was the most anticipated crypto vol event (worked example in Part IV.4).

## Part II — Pricing Fundamentals: Implied Volatility, Skew, Term Structure

### 2.1 Implied vs realized volatility, and the rule of 16

- **Realized volatility** is backward-looking — how much the asset *has been* moving, measurable over any window (5-day, 10-day, 100-day).
- **Implied volatility** is the market's expectation of future movement, embedded in option prices. "Implied vol impacts what you pay for the option. Realized volatility is the actual volatility of the underlying." The two are related but routinely diverge — and the divergence is where both the risk premium (Part IV) and the trade opportunities live.
- **The rule of 16.** Annualized vol converts to a daily move by dividing by ~16 (square-root-of-time scaling over ~252 trading days): "If we expect a 1% per day move in the asset, that equates to a **16% annualized vol**... if it was 32, it would mean 2% a day." (Roger checks: "48 is 3%?" — yes.) So a 40-vol stock implies ~2.5% daily moves; a 100-vol stock implies ~6% daily moves. This rule is used throughout the course to translate vol numbers into daily expectations.
- **Cheap vs expensive is relative, not the dollar price.** A low-premium option can still be expensive: "cheap or expensiveness is all relative to the volatility of the underlying asset, not the cost of the option in dollars." The practical screen taught: take the **percentile of the current at-the-money implied vol against its own trailing history** (they use a trailing one-year window) because "volatility is typically mean-reverting." Example: ATM vol at 25 when it has been 15 for three months is historically expensive — even if the option "costs" little.

### 2.2 Skew

**Definition:** skew is the difference in implied volatility between downside puts and upside calls at the same expiry. With zero skew, every strike on one expiry would plot as a flat line; real equities plot with puts at a premium and calls at a discount.

- **Why it exists (flow explanation):** hedging demand bids downside puts; overwriting supplies upside calls. "There's more demand in the equity space for downside protection than there is for strategies that profit on big rallies. An option struck 5% below is often — though not always — more expensive than an option struck 5% above, even though they're equidistant."
- **Why it persists (empirical explanation):** equity markets "crush down and grind up." Downturns are fast and panicky; recoveries are slow grinds. Realized vol is structurally higher on the way down, so downside options rationally cost more.
- **What the skew is telling you:** "the volatility market's perception of where the tail risk lies" — which direction the market thinks big moves could come from; those wings trade at a premium.
- **It is asset-class specific:**
  - **Equities/indices:** persistent downside skew.
  - **Commodities (e.g., gold, silver) and often crypto:** **upside skew** — calls can trade richer than puts. (Crypto flips around: "sometimes it's for puts, sometimes it's calls.")
  - **FX:** roughly **symmetric smile** — either wing can go bid; FX "does nothing for ages and then pretends it's a stock for a few days," so both tails carry premium.
- **Cross-strike comparisons within one side also reflect the skew's slope:** a lower-strike call generally trades on higher implied vol than a higher-strike call — which is why a call spread (buy low strike, sell high strike) buys the expensive vol and sells the cheap vol, and why its payoff ratios are systematically worse than the same-width put spread (Part V).

### 2.3 Term structure and forward volatility

**Definition:** the term structure maps implied vol (usually at-the-money) across expiries. The S&P has an expiry every week "for as far as you can see"; retail mostly trades sub-six-month, as does Lakha himself.

- **Contango (upward slope) is the normal state** — a **term premium**: the front end is priced fairly for current conditions, while longer expiries charge "extra juice for uncertainty... anything could happen in a year."
- **Inversion (backwardation) = stress.** When the market is moving now, everyone wants optionality and gamma, short-vol players are forced to cover, and the front end spikes above the back end, which prices in mean reversion to normal. Stress inversions "don't typically last that long."
- **Kinks mark events.** A distortion at a specific expiry usually means a known event (earnings, election, central-bank meeting) sits inside that window; the kink's size indicates the **implied move** being priced (worked example in Part IV.4).
- **Forward volatility** can be extracted from the term structure: compare the expiry *before* an event with the expiry *containing* it, convert the difference into the forward vol for the event window (a straightforward variance calculation), and compare it to the asset's base vol to gauge how big a move is being priced.
- **The shape is flow-driven, not just expectation-driven:** heavy short-dated selling can push the front end to "very, very cheap" levels (steep contango); long-dated hedging demand can steepen the back; in 2022-style stress the whole front lifts.

### 2.4 The volatility surface, put-call parity, and the forward

- **The surface** is the combination of strike, expiry, and implied vol into one 3D object — "a fancy way of saying, how do I observe what is going on in the whole space of strikes and expiries." Lakha's practical note: in 20 years he never stared at the 3D chart; he watches the **term structure** and the **skew for each expiry**, expiry by expiry. The 3D view is "an elegant encapsulation."
- **Put-call parity:** the call and put at the same strike and maturity must trade at the **same implied vol** (apparent differences on a broker chain come from wide markets or calculation errors).
- **Implied vol is conditional.** The vol attached to a strike 20% below spot is the market's expectation of movement *if the market gets down there* — "there is conditionality." That's why tail strikes carry their own vol levels.
- **Options price off the forward, not spot.** Because options expire in the future, models anchor on the **forward price**, which moves with **interest rates** and **dividends**. The 2022 rate cycle moved forwards materially — but "in anything six months and shorter, the dividends are known" and rates are stable, so the impact is small for the tenors this course focuses on. (A COVID-era sudden dividend cut is cited as the rare exception that shocked the market.)
- **The information program:** by monitoring spot, implied vol, skew, and term structure together, "we glean a wealth of information about what sophisticated participants are thinking" — and "the option market often leads the spot market." Out-of-the-money calls or puts going unusually well bid frequently precede moves in that direction. Even an investor who never trades an option can use the options market as an information source about risk, positioning, and smart-money behavior.

## Part III — The Greeks, as Taught (the Car Analogy)

The Greeks decompose an option's price sensitivity into its drivers: spot, time, and implied vol. "They are the building blocks of the option price... if they sell you an option, it's not because they disagree with your view — it's because they think they can make money from some or all of these Greeks by the way they hedge and risk-manage them." To de-scarify them, Lakha teaches the whole set through **driving a car**:

| Greek | Car analogy | Definition | Key teaching points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Delta** | Speed | Change in option value per change in underlying price | Long stock = 100 delta. OTM call starts near 0 delta; ~50 delta at the money; →100 delta deep ITM. Works in reverse on the way down. Puts have negative delta. |
| **Gamma** | Acceleration | Change in delta per change in underlying | Low far from the strike; **maximum at the strike**; declines once ITM (speed still rising, acceleration falling). Creates options' asymmetry. |
| **Theta** | Daily fuel cost | Value lost per day of time passing | "You are buying time." Every option bleeds theta; the majority of options expire worthless. |
| **Implied vol** | Unit price of fuel | Market's expected future vol | Sets how expensive the gamma is to carry: tractor (low vol, cheap decay) vs rocket ship (high vol, expensive decay). |
| **Vega** | The vehicle morphing | Change in option value per change in implied vol | "You bought something priced as boring that is now priced as exciting." |

**Delta, expanded.** The canonical example: stock at $100, you buy the $110 call expiring in a week. The stock needs +10% in a week — unlikely, so the option barely moves when the stock ticks to $101 (near-zero delta; "the car is not moving yet"). As the stock approaches $110 the delta accelerates to ~50%; at $150 the option is deep ITM, captures ~100% of further moves, and behaves like the stock ("we have hit our max speed"). Falling prices run the film backwards: delta decays from 100 toward 50 toward 0 — "like hitting the brakes" — so a long option's losses decelerate and are capped at the premium, while long stock keeps losing the whole way down. **The asymmetry — limited loss (premium) vs theoretically unlimited gain — is perhaps the most important concept for the option user.**

**Gamma, expanded.** Gamma is what makes the delta accelerate when the stock moves your way and decelerate when it doesn't — "an option can often be dead in the water, and then boom! The underlying heads to and through the strike, and the value explodes higher." For market makers, gamma into expiry is the central risk: short gamma (short options, especially short-dated near the money) pushes their delta the wrong way and forces costly hedging in choppy markets.

**Theta and implied vol, expanded.** Theta is the daily rent on gamma: "the more gamma you have, the more theta you will pay, all other things equal." Implied vol is the unit price of that theta. Sewage-utility options (low vol) give you gamma cheaply; high-tech-growth options charge rocket-ship fuel prices. If a "tractor" stock starts moving like a rocket ship, its options get re-priced upward — that repricing **is** vega P&L.

**Vega, expanded.** Your exposure to a change in the market's perception of future vol. Examples taught:
- **AMC:** "a boring cinema company... the meme craze happens and it goes to the moon." Implied vol went from ~40 (≈2.5%/day expectation) into the hundreds (≈6%+/day). Option buyers who owned a few months before the repricing made fortunes on vega alone — "that's when a tractor turns into a rocket ship."
- **Banks 2007–08:** banks were a boring sector trading at ~20 vol; after subprime, Bear Stearns, and Lehman they moved on ~100 vol. Anyone long vega on banks — single stocks or the bank indices (**SX7E** in Europe, **XLF** in the US) — with even one-year options "made a fortune" on the 20→100 repricing.
- The market maker's mirror-image fear: "selling an option on a tractor and seeing it turn into a rocket ship before expiry," because makers price off past performance, not fundamental foresight.

**Second-order Greek: vanna.** Introduced during the GameStop discussion: **vanna is the change in delta for a change in implied volatility.** In a gamma squeeze, deltas grow not only through gamma (spot moving) but through vanna (implied vol rising as call demand surges) — both force dealers to buy more stock, feeding the loop (Part IV.4).

**What matters most to whom:**
- *Market maker:* delta least; the edge is the **gamma/theta versus realized-vol judgment**, **vega positioning along the curve**, and relative value across the surface.
- *Retail/investor:* you must understand **theta** (the constant bleed) and the **gamma/theta tradeoff near expiry**: "if you let your option get sub-three-weeks and it's anywhere near the money, the theta bill is growing — but so is the gamma. It makes your position binary: you either lose all your money really fast or it multiplies quickly." Investors wanting a smooth P&L path should **roll before options get that short-dated**, keeping gamma/theta moderate and giving views time to play out.

## Part IV — Volatility as an Asset Class

### 4.1 The variance/volatility risk premium and "expensive vs cheap" vol

- **Implied usually trades above realized** — that spread is the **volatility risk premium** (also called the variance premium): the price nervous hedgers persistently pay and option sellers persistently harvest. Watching the **implied-minus-realized spread** is a core barometer of fear: if the VIX rises while realized stays low, "that's telling you there's fear in the market"; if it rises because realized is rising, it's just tracking reality.
- **The mean-reversion psychology trap.** Vol is a mean-reverting asset, and fear of the mean reversion stops people doing the right trade. Examples drilled in class:
  - *High vol can be cheap:* stock implied at 80 (down from 40 — scary!) but realizing 120: "owning it at 80 is a great trade because it's moving."
  - *Low vol can be expensive:* 2017 — VIX ~10, market realizing 4–5. Everyone said "vol is low, protection is cheap." "Being short vol was the best trade you could do" almost all year. There's a floor to how low implied goes because nobody wants to *sell* at 10 — but if realized is 4, selling is still right.
  - **Rule of thumb from the desk:** "High vol is often the cheapest vol" — elevated implied is usually elevated *for a reason* (something is happening), and the sectors with the highest implied (miners, 2008 banks) were exactly the ones worth owning. And you don't have to catch the bottom in vol: "catching falling knives is dangerous. It's the same with volatility. When everyone's long and there's no catalyst, vol grinds lower... once the street flips short and starts paying up for vol, that's when owning options makes sense."

### 4.2 The VIX and VIX futures

- **What the VIX is:** the 30-day implied volatility of the S&P 500 computed across **all listed strikes** (a strike every 5 ticks) as a variance-swap-style weighted average, weighted toward the downside. Because it integrates the skew, **the VIX typically sits 3–5 vol points above the 30-day at-the-money S&P implied** (Roger's numbers: ATM 15, VIX 20).
- **You cannot trade spot VIX.** You trade **VIX futures**, which are *forward* vols: a one-month future expires into the VIX spot a month from now — which itself represents 30-day S&P vol starting then. In a normal contango world those forwards trade at a premium: VIX spot 20, one-month future 22, next 24. **If nothing happens, each future decays down toward spot** — buy at 22, settle at 20, lose 2 vol points. "Buying and holding VIX futures and rolling them monthly has typically got a negative expected value" — a carry cost, institutionalized in Barclays' **VXX** ETN, whose long-term chart is "not pretty."
- **Consequences for hedgers:**
  - Long VIX is **not buy-and-hold**; it is a high-conviction, imminent-selloff trade. Always account for the roll cost as the expected loss if vol doesn't come.
  - **When it works, monetize immediately.** March 2020: VIX 20→80 in under a month; **VXX +500%** — and it gives it back if you don't exit.
  - **Shorting VIX made people a lot of money** — systematically rolling short front-month futures harvests the term premium — "but that can also blow you up spectacularly" (Volmageddon, Part IV.5). Safer variants taught: buy **puts on the VIX** (fixed premium, profits if VIX collapses) rather than naked short futures; in options, use **credit spreads** to cap the tail.
  - **The one long-VIX setup that isn't timing:** when VIX is at its floor and the market is transitioning to *upside* realized vol — a rally that breaks levels on 15–20 realized instead of 10 — the VIX doesn't need to fall further. Then long VIX behaves "like a put on the S&P that restrikes itself higher if the market rallies" — an asymmetric profile. It requires screen time to spot the inflection.

### 4.3 Reading the surface for signals

The practical toolkit Lakha uses daily (and builds into his Volatility Dashboard):

- **Two lenses, not the 3D blob:** term structure, and skew per expiry, watched across expiries.
- **Skew extremes as a panic bottoms counter-indicator.** In crashes, skew goes to "astronomic levels" (crypto crashes are the purest case). Extreme skew + implied at a big premium to realized = everyone has paid up for protection, "there are no sellers left... we're at peak panic." **Peak panic marks a short-term low — rarely the ultimate low.** The signature of the *real* bottom: on the retest (or break) of the low, **vol and skew don't pick up** — exhaustion. Cited: 2003, 2009, and 2022 (VIX peaked at the Ukraine invasion; subsequent market lows came on *lower* VIX).
- **Quiet skew-bid in a grinding rally = early warning.** Market calmly rising while puts get steadily better bid: "some smart money is putting hedges on because they think the rally is running out of steam." Not foolproof — "another part of the puzzle."
- **Event kinks in an otherwise flat curve** (see 4.4).
- **Cross-asset relative value.** Screen vol and 25-delta skew across oil, gold, copper, TLT (20-year Treasuries), major indices, FX. Two taught uses:
  - *Same driver, cheaper expression:* dollar-yen is extremely correlated to US rates; TLT at 21 vol realizing 13 looks expensive, while dollar-yen at 12 vol realizing 14 is good value for the **same rates trade**. "Pick your spots... in a place where it's better value."
  - *Leading indicators:* copper skew going bid (fear in "the economy's call") without a filter-through to equities can flag cheap equity downside.
- **Implied-vs-realized as a positioning check** for the dealer-positioning models (Part I.6).
- **Bottoms, tops, and options:** "a low in the market does not mean higher volatility." In 2002–03 and 2022, investors got the direction right buying puts but lost to falling vol — "you get the trade direction right but the instrument is wrong."

### 4.4 Volatility events

**Definition:** a vol event is either a **known event** expected to move the market (earnings, elections, central-bank meetings, macro data) or an **unexpected shock** (9/11, COVID). In the zero-rates decade, "natural" market vol was suppressed and "all the volatility was centered around certain events" — pricing those events became a market maker's bread and butter.

**Known events — how they're priced and traded:**
- **Earnings example (Google, as taught):** establish the **base vol** from realized (~40 = ~2.5%/day); observe the term structure — the expiry containing earnings trades **5–6 vol points above** adjacent weeklies and **~10–11 points above base**; convert the event-window forward vol into an **implied move** of ~**5.1%** on the day — consistent with Google's historical earnings moves. (Options Insight publishes a free implied-move calculator.) The market is "more often quite well priced than not," but surprises happen — Target/Walmart and Netflix/Facebook-style **20–30%** post-earnings moves against 5% implieds.
- **Macro events — the VIX kink:** into the November 2020 US election, the VIX curve showed a kink at the **October '20 expiry** *nine months ahead* — the market pricing election-window premium "how forward-looking they are." As the event nears and hits every research note, "the kink normally gets amplified."
- **Event-date/definition risk (the tail risk of calendar trades):** in 2020 the election date didn't move, but a contested-result/recount fear moved the *expected resolution* a month later — **the entire event premium shifted from the October bucket to the November bucket**. Anyone long October got whacked while short November spiked: "that's a tail risk for people who play these calendars. If the date moves on you, it could take some pain."
- **The dreaded vol reset.** After a known event passes, event premium collapses. Post-2020-election: the inverted curve reverted to contango within a week, with the **front end collapsing ~12 vol points** — a huge move for front-month VIX contracts. Because "the majority of the market are too scared to sell volatility for the event," it trades at excess premium; Lakha's playbook: if the event looks expensive, **get out before the event or go short the vol** to capture the reset. He was a public advocate of short VIX into the 2020 election, reasoning the result wouldn't move the market much either way and any move would mean-revert.
- **Hold through or exit?** His rule: hold long event vol into the event only if it was cheap-to-fairly-priced; if it's now rich in the final week, exit or flip short. Directional buyers around events must budget for the reset: estimate where VIX/implied mean-reverts absent the event, translate that into premium damage on your option, and work out how big a spot move you need just to overcome the vega loss. Lakha himself prefers event trades that are about vol, not direction ("sell delta-neutral strategies to trade the reset"), and warns retail that outright vol-selling into events is "for the pros — the cleanest retail version is VIX futures, or safer still, VIX puts / credit spreads."
- **The strange convex-to-concave flip.** Into genuinely big events (Brexit, US elections) Lakha repeatedly watched **at-the-money vols get bid while the (normally richer) wings compress** — the surface goes from convex to concave, because "we're going to move away from where we are, then stabilize at the new equilibrium," making ATM the ideal hold.
- **Crypto worked example — the Ethereum Merge (Sept 2022):** the most anticipated crypto event ever; everyone debated a ±30% spot move. What actually happened: implied climbed relentlessly into the date ("120 vol"), spot drifted down 10–15%, and **vol gapped 120→90 in a heartbeat** — a ~30-vol reset. "The most obvious short vol I'd seen in crypto in ages." Lesson: vol resets are near-instantaneous, not a glide — you must be positioned beforehand.

**Unexpected shocks:**
- Surprise shocks produce the **largest** vol spikes (because nothing was priced) and mean-revert quickly — "volatility almost has a gravity to it; what goes up inevitably comes back down; it's just a question of when."
- **Variance swaps** are disproportionately damaged by shocks: unlike a vanilla option that moves "off the map" and shuts its Greeks down after a 30% crash, **a variance swap grows when vol doubles** — it gets bigger and nastier exactly when liquidity disappears (Lakha's own 2010 experience, Part VI).

**Gamma squeezes (the 2021 phenomenon):** retail coordinate via social forums into the same short-dated OTM calls; dealer delta hedging buys stock; the rising stock and rising implied vol (through **gamma** and **vanna**) grow the dealers' delta further, forcing more buying — a self-feeding loop. GameStop, AMC, Tesla. GameStop's implied vol reached **~500**; Roger recalls realized vol printing **999**. Signals visible beforehand: upside open interest building while the stock got hard to borrow. Aftermath: when the squeeze breaks, vol resets violently from absurd levels (500→100 fast), because spot itself is unsustainable. **Trap for hedgers:** buying puts after the spike means paying 200 vol that halves in two days — "even if the stock dumps by half, I might make no money." Corollary: **selling naked calls in single stocks is the riskiest end of the spectrum** ("a random single stock can 2–3x fast; an index isn't going 10x overnight — selling index calls is far more defensible, though still risky").

### 4.5 Volmageddon — anatomy of a short-vol blowup (Feb 2018)

The course's canonical cautionary tale, taught in detail:

- **The machine:** 2017's record-low realized vol made **short front-month VIX futures, rolled daily**, a money printer. Wrapped as Credit Suisse's **XIV** ETN, it compounded ~**9%** monthly-annualized, $30→$130 in two years, topping every leaderboard, sucking in retail AUM (including the famous supermarket worker who reportedly made millions).
- **The fragility:** the strategy is **anti-mean-reversion at scale** — for a mean-reverting, zero-floored asset, XIV sold *more* futures the lower vol went, because bigger AUM needed the same 9% from the same ~1-vol-point monthly decay. "Like you're assuming a constant trend in something you know to be mean-reverting and floored at zero. Who does that?"
- **The trigger and the cascade:** a hot inflation print → a 2% S&P down day → 4% → **the VIX doubled in a day** → the rolling short was force-rebalanced into a spike → **XIV went from $130 to effectively $0 overnight**; the entire ETN complex blew up.
- **The lessons taught:** price-insensitive, crowded, levered short-vol is systemic fragility; "it works until it doesn't"; and any short-vol harvest must be sized for the day the trade's own mechanics amplify the move against it.

## Part V — The Strategies

Every strategy below is given as taught: construction, rationale, when to use it, risks, and the worked examples from the course.

### 5.1 Directional strategies (the hedge fund / retail playbook)

Directional trades are "most people's entry point into the world of options." The course's framing: there is no universally best structure — you are balancing **leverage, cost, duration, and risk profile**, and the structure must reflect your conviction. "Find that balance that meets as many of your objectives as possible — certainly those that are your main priority."

**(a) Long call / long put (outright).**

- *Expiry selection first.* What's your horizon? Catalysts? Macro risks that could derail and need recovery time? "There's no point buying a one-month call on a fundamental bullish view that could take a year to materialize... I've often seen people buy calls that expire worthless month after month when a one-year position for a bit more premium would have been far more successful. **Marry the time horizon of your idea and the trade.**" Tactical, technical views (chart levels, positioning, sentiment) justify shorter expiries for a quick move. Term structure matters: in an inverted curve, short-dated options carry **vega risk** (implied can mean-revert down before your move arrives); a steep contango makes serial short-dated buying pricey versus one longer-dated buy.
- *Strike selection.* ATM and modestly OTM are the usual opening choices (lower price, tighter markets). "A lower price doesn't mean cheap." Further OTM = more leverage per dollar, but "by the time the stock moves, the option may have lost too much to theta and never get through your strike." Far-OTM options are for (i) very high conviction on a big, fast move, or (ii) hedging unforeseeable crashes — "the market assigns a very low probability to that outcome, and usually the market's right."
- *Leverage = conviction = delta choice.* The Lesson 8 dialogue: 50-delta ATM is "I want to be very sure this goes through the strike"; 5–25-delta OTM is "I'm so sure it rallies a lot I want to multiply my premium — high octane, and I can lose it all." The more OTM options you own, the more **acceleration** (gamma) you get heading into the strike — "the car analogy again."
- *Skew check.* Buying equity calls benefits you (calls trade at a vol discount to puts); in gold/commodities the reverse — which is why bearish trades are often better built with puts in metals. For single stocks, skew varies — check.
- *The vol-price question.* The single most important check for an outright directional buy: "**How expensive is the implied vol, and how much downside does it have over the duration of my trade?**" Screen ATM implied percentile vs its own history. But Roger's caveat, endorsed: don't let 3–4 expensive vol points kill a trade where you expect a 50% move — "when you're trading direction, it's about the premium and whether that premium will multiply. Expensive vol is expensive for a reason; sometimes those are the best options you can buy." With high conviction, evaluate: *what is this premium worth in my scenario, at my time frame, net of decay?* If it hits your risk-reward, do it.
- *Run to expiry?* Almost never by default. Near expiry the position is binary ("lose all your premium or multiply through the strike"). You bought 4–6 weeks **to give the view time**; with 1–2 weeks left and unchanged conviction, the position's nature has changed — "reevaluate the premium left and roll into something longer-dated more in keeping with your conviction." (Same logic as Part III's gamma/theta warning.)

**(b) Call spread / put spread (vertical).** Buy one call, sell a higher-strike call, same expiry (or the put mirror). *Rationale:* cuts premium cost, cuts theta, and cuts vega (short leg offsets the long leg) — a **lower-conviction, lower-timing-risk** expression for "moderately bullish, not sure how long it takes." *Risks/tradeoffs:* **harder to monetize early** (the short leg bleeds against you; "it's not just wait for the move and take profits — sometimes one leg works against you"); payouts are expiry-shaped, so spreads suit patient holders. *The 3–4x rule of thumb* (Roger's, adopted by Imran): "If it doesn't make you 4 times or more, it's not overly interesting — because when the market pukes and you want to monetize early, you might only have doubled your money. If the original payoff was 2-to-1 at best, you'll make very little." Note the skew asymmetry: in equities, a **put spread** enjoys better payoff geometry than the same-width call spread, because you buy the lower (richer-vol) strike and sell the higher (cheaper-vol) strike on the put side; a call spread buys rich and sells cheap, so "you'll have to accept slightly worse payoffs." (In gold, where skew is inverted, the call spread is the better-geometry trade.) A put spread can also be **used as the hedge in high-vol regimes**: less vega, less theta than an outright put, cheaper carry while you wait; when the short put "has made most of its theta," buy it back and keep the long put.

**(c) Ratio spreads (the 1x2).** Buy one call, sell **two** higher-strike calls (or the put mirror), ideally for zero cost. *Rationale:* maximizes leverage for ~no premium. *Risks:* the payoff has a narrow sweet spot and then **goes negative if the stock rallies too far** — "you've converted a directional trade into a volatility trade; you're now outright short an extra call." Margin: the extra naked leg needs real margin; without portfolio margin there's no netting, so small accounts may be forced to add a far wing (1x2x1, a "call fly"). **"If you are new to options, this is not your entry-level trade."** The course's test before any structure: *what is your conviction — price or volatility?* "If you've got a directional view and your strongest conviction is direction, and you do a ratio with zero delta — what is the point? Your implementation isn't consistent with your view. Ratios are for when you've got views about *conditional* volatility."

**(d) Condors and flies (multi-leg wings).** Structures that own the far wings — used by Lakha for **specific, time-boxed tactical views**. Worked example: S&P oversold into an expiry; view: a two-week bounce that "dies at $4,000" (gets sucked to the pin). A **(broken-wing) call condor** cost ~5–6 index points for a potential payoff of "40-odd index points," with a **contained loss of ~20–30 points** if the market ripped instead. "You can have a very specific view on where the market's going, when, and still cap your risk." Roger's caution stands: Imran had 20 years of experience first; start simple.

**(e) Trading around a core position (core + tactical overlay).** Lakha's own modus operandi: "I used to be a trader that invested; now I'm an investor that trades." Long-term asset allocation in the self-managed pension portfolio; medium-term (3–6 month) option positions for core macro theses (e.g., dollar higher, high yield lower); and **short-dated tactical trades layered on top in the opposite direction** when the market gets oversold and positioning exaggerates moves into expiries — "a one or two week, or four week trade that costs a little and might make 5 or 10x on a tactical bounce. Then you monetize it and you're back to your original thesis." He uses flies/condors for these because they give "good leverage and low premium spend."

**(f) Scalping gamma around a winning option.** The "more sophisticated use": when your call reaches high delta, don't just sit — sell part of the delta (or stock/futures against it), buy it back on pullbacks: "you've effectively scalped some gamma," which "helps cushion the time-decay bill." The aggressive variant taught (from the dot-com era, Part VI): own long gamma **plus** deliberate extra delta in your directional view, flipping the delta sign at turning points while the gamma stops you out — "firepower to aggressively trade a stock that's swinging around."

### 5.2 Hedging strategies (the institutional playbook, retail-usable)

**Why hedge instead of sell** was covered in Part I.3. The menu, cheapest to most convex:

**(a) Outright protective put.** Buy an S&P put struck ~5% below spot (the "**95% strike**") for ~6 months: insurance against a >5% drawdown. *When it's too expensive:* when everyone is scared, implied on those puts is bid — that's when institutions switch to structures.

**(b) Risk reversal / collar — the course's favorite.** **Construction:** long put + short call (higher strike), usually same expiry. The classic institutional version is the **90/110 risk reversal**: buy the 90% put, sell the 110% call; skew means you still typically pay some net premium. More aggressive users sell a closer call to force **zero cost**, at the price of capping upside sooner — "remember, if you're short a call and the market goes through that strike, you may be obliged to pay the difference." Expiries can be mismatched (e.g., buy a 6-month put, sell a 4-month call) so protection outlives the financed premium. *Why it's great:* "you exchange parts of the distribution of outcomes to reflect your specific views on spot and timing" — open-ended downside protection, funded by upside you were happy to relinquish, and it protects the sideways case (call premium offsets put decay) so you can keep hedging longer. *Upside-skew bonus:* in commodities/crypto (and sometimes single stocks), the call you sell can fund a put *closer* to the money than itself.
  - **Worked example — gold, summer 2020 (Lakha's own trade).** Post-COVID stimulus thesis; bought gold; it ripped $1,500→$2,000 April–July; RSI ~90 ("very overbought"), but the long-term thesis was intact — don't want to sell, expect a tactical correction. Gold's skew is for **calls**, so: **sold a 3-month call 20% OTM** (~+20% above spot; ~1% premium) **on half** the position (happy to let half go at +40% on the year), then **spent that premium on a 6-week put** — shorter-dated for more leverage on the imminent pullback. "A few weeks later we corrected a quick 5% in gold. I monetized the put, bought back the calls for nothing, job done — I've had my pullback and I'm back to my long position."
  - **Worked example — silver, 2022.** Accumulated a long-term silver clip in the low $18s (plan: buy more at 16 and 14); within two weeks it bounced to $20 (+10%) with no fundamental change — "this bounce is a fade, but I've got a great entry and I don't want to let go of my allocation." Sold the ~1–3 month call **10–20% OTM (~$24–25)**, bought a put with the premium; the fade came, put paid, call expired bought back for pennies. He does "these every other month" — "my favorite tactical trade."
  - **Worked example — Bitcoin, early 2021.** Holding a 4x position ($8–10k buys, now ~$40k, going "a bit exponential"); call demand was "the most ridiculous vol pricing I'd ever seen." **Sold ~2-week 72k calls (~70% OTM) and bought 2–4-week puts 20% OTM for essentially zero cost.** Asymmetry: "to be taken out of my Bitcoin it has to rally another 70% in two weeks — what's the chance? And it only needs to pull back 20% and I'm making money on the put." Sizing: a third to a half of the position, "wherever I'm comfortable letting go at +70%." He argues systematic risk reversals belong in any HODLer's portfolio, and that retail "need to wake up" to this trade.

**(c) Put spread (and put spread collar).** When vol is already elevated and you're unsure of timing: buy a put, sell a further OTM put (optionally also sell a call — the **put spread collar**). Keeps carry and vega down; cap the protection. Manage by buying back the short put once it's decayed, restoring the outright put (5.1b).

**(d) Deep out-of-the-money puts — tail-risk hedging.** "Lottery tickets" that "should be expected to lose money"; bought for the once-a-decade event. **Nassim Taleb** (*The Black Swan*) is cited as the great advocate. The chart shown: **S&P March 2020 3,000-strike puts** bled to near zero by February 2020 (S&P ~3,300), then **multiplied 100x** in the crash. "People criticize these trades because 99% of the time they lose. What they fail to realize is that in that 1% of outcomes it works, it might save your career or your retirement portfolio." And: **"buy protection when it's cheap, not when you need it"** — by the time you need it, fear has repriced it.
  - **The systematic crash-hedge design (the course's most actionable framework):**
    1. **Budget:** ~**50–100 bps per year** for crash protection, scaled by regime (e.g., cut to ~2.5% of portfolio spend in a euphoric macro; raise it when things look "ropey" — the January 2020 China signal).
    2. **Tenor:** keep duration in the **1–2 month** zone — long enough to survive a late-month selloff and keep time value/vega (the front of the curve is "the part that explodes the most" in a crash), short enough for leverage. **Not one week** ("decaying so damn fast"), not six months (no leverage; the option "won't explode" even in a 10% drop).
    3. **Rolling:** slice the budget monthly; **never let the put get too short-dated** — roll back to 1–2 months of life continuously; strike **up** as the market rallies so protection stays relevant ("protection that's getting further and further from the market will never help you"); when the crash comes, **monetize** — with 1–2 months left you capture delta, gamma, *and* the vol spike.
    4. **Accounting honesty:** "Know that the hedge has a negative expected value, and make sure whatever else you've got on has a positive expected value; net them. If the total is still positive, fine." Pair the loss-making hedge with positive-alpha strategies — "if you don't have any positive alpha in your book, you can't afford the hedge." (Roger's corollary: the premium is house insurance — expiring worthless can be the *good* outcome.)
    5. **Escalation when a tail emerges:** either raise the budget (double the monthly spend for six months) or **finance convexity**: "sell some 30-delta puts and buy three, four, even five times as many low-delta puts."
    6. **The 20%-fall nuance (Roger's question, Imran's answer):** if you expect a 20% fall, don't buy the 20% put and hold passively — a single short-dated OTM put that gets there late in its life can still pay nothing. The rolling 1–2-month structure exists precisely so that when the move comes you hold time value and can monetize before the strike is even reached. After a crash that takes your 5-delta put to 60-delta: roll down/out but **bank most of it** — "you might even roll into a put spread, because vols are now high and the strikes you're resetting to aren't cheap." Systematic crash-hedgers may legitimately roll all the way back down to a fresh 5-delta; discretionary traders usually shouldn't.
    7. **Post-crash income flip:** with dry powder after the vol explosion, **selling puts at the inflated vols** (struck where you're happy to own) is "a very valid strategy — there's so much juice you're basically earning income to get long." Caveat: "there's a difference between really wanting to buy the stock and being happy to buy the stock — you'll always get the income, but you won't always get the stock."

**(e) VIX-based hedges.** See Part IV.2: high-conviction, imminent-selloff instrument; roll cost = expected bleed; monetize spikes immediately (March 2020: VXX +500%); the "restriking put" regime at vol floors. "It's not a buy-and-hold strategy."

**(f) Stock replacement.** **Construction:** sell the stock (or don't add), buy calls instead — ideally **ATM or ITM, 6–12 months or longer** ("a 3-month 110 call is not stock replacement — in three months it's dead and you've got nothing replacing the stock"). ~70–80 delta at purchase, capturing most of the upside. *Rationale:* "if the market completely crashes, all you lose is the premium" — and unlike a put hedge, the payoff doesn't depend on how fast the selloff is or how vol behaves. *Skew note:* in equities, calls are the cheap side (put skew), making replacement efficient; in gold, buying the put instead is the skew-efficient hedge. (Roger's arithmetic on locked-in gains: sold stock at 100, rolled 2% into calls, stock drops 20% — "the option served its purpose"; or put spends 2% while stock makes 10% — you made 8% net and "you shouldn't care that you underperformed by 2%. It did its job.")

**(g) Cross-asset hedges.** Hedge the *correlation*, buy where it's cheapest: TLT puts as the cheapest hedge for a tech-book against rising yields (Roger's 2022 setup: reverse head-and-shoulders in yields, clear technical level, "asymmetric — yields might fall, but if they go up, a hell of a lot"); dollar-yen for rates vol when TLT implied is rich (Part IV.3). "In that tail outcome, the correlations you're witnessing will hold — so look for where the value is in volatility space."

### 5.3 Income strategies (overwriting and underwriting)

- **Overwriting (covered call selling).** **Construction:** own the stock; sell upside calls such that exercise takes you flat — struck above market, so exercise means selling at a profit; keep the premium otherwise. Repeat monthly/quarterly to "really enhance returns... if the stock doesn't rally too sharply." *Who does it:* one of the biggest institutional flows — mutual/income funds enhancing yield, long-shorts selling calls against overweights. *Retail verdict:* "I don't think it's dangerous for retail. Several of my long-standing clients say it's their consistently best strategy... it helped them manage drawdowns." Start in partial size ("nibble"), and check your own psychology: "if you're annoyed you haven't got all the stock you used to have, maybe don't do it next time."
- **Underwriting (cash-covered put selling).** **Construction:** underweight or in cash, happy to buy lower; sell downside puts struck where you'd happily own; collect "often a handsome" premium; if assigned, you buy at a price you wanted anyway. For long-shorts, selling puts up to the size that restores benchmark weight makes it a **covered** sale. In the zero-rate era this systematic covered selling was enormous — "it supplies the dealer community with short-dated options and pushes down implied vol of all short-dated options — why you see that contango shape."
- **Credit spreads.** Selling an option financed by buying a further-OTM one: caps the open-ended risk, cuts margin, "you're still earning premium if nothing really happens... by doing a spread, you limit your risk."
- **The discipline clause (repeated, and the course's behavioral core for income trades):** only sell strikes you are **genuinely happy** with in *both* directions — happy to buy at the put strike, happy to sell at the call strike — and accept the fixed time horizon of the expiry. **"Don't change your mind about being happy when it goes wrong."** The known opportunity costs: the stock can touch your strike mid-life, reverse, and expire worthless (you keep the premium but miss the trade — "you get your premium, but you don't get to sell the stock at that price"); and when you sell an option you are *giving away optionality* — "the swings and the path typically won't work in your favor." The dream scenario (rally through the call, get assigned, stock retraces, buy back) exists but can't be relied on.

### 5.4 Vol/event trades (the professional end)

Summarizing the vol-trading playbook for completeness (mostly not retail trades, as the course stresses):

- **Long event vol:** identify the event early; buy it when cheap or fair; hold to within ~a week of the event; exit or flip if it richens (Part IV.4). Watch event-date/definition risk on calendars.
- **Short event vol / vol reset harvesting:** sell the overpriced event premium delta-neutrally (professionals) or via VIX futures/puts (cleaner for others); target the post-event collapse (election 2020: −12 vol points; ETH Merge: 120→90).
- **The 2008 relative-value trade (desk-scale, the course's grand case study):** as Lehman wobbled (September 2008; the melt really came in October), the S&P term structure inverted ~**10 vols** (1-month ~10 points *above* 6-month) — yet **skew was merely average, nowhere near panic levels**. The cause: big institutions were doing **1x2 put spreads** (buying near-the-money puts, selling two far-OTM puts) for cheapness, crushing the wing vols. The desk **bought as much skew as they could get** (long downside vs upside vols across FTSE and Euro Stoxx), financing the bleeding carry with **calendars: selling 1-month vol ~10 points above where they bought 6-month vol**, "a ticking time bomb... carrying the book fairly flat. Then the market goes down 20%, vol goes to 100, and you make loads of money." The safety logic: entering a calendar on a *super-inverted* curve pays heavy carry and can't easily invert further — "if vol keeps going up, the whole curve lifts and they work with each other; in the downside puke you pick up all the skew. That's when you make out."
- **Brexit (June 2016):** kinks built massively into FTSE and Euro Stoxx expiries; cable (GBP/USD) priced some vol but the *leave* outcome was genuinely underpriced — cable fell 15%, equities knee-jerked ~10%, and the FTSE then *rallied* (weak-currency exporter story) within hours. Long FX vol into the event did well; long FTSE vol had "a very short moment in time" to monetize. Lesson: into shocks, long options face **opportunity cost** if you're slow; short options face **ruin** — "it takes a brave person to sell vol into big material game-changing events. Exercise caution, own the wings, protect those tails."
- **General short-vol risk management:** "The way you manage risk is not assuming your view is right. It's asking *what if I'm wrong? What can I buy against it?*" Every time Lakha sells premium, he buys the offsetting wing.

## Part VI — Getting Started Practically

The closing lesson's practical on-ramp:

1. **Match the tool to your sophistication.** Payoff diagrams and formulas are "dry" but needed for proficiency; however, "if you want to be a basic user of options and you stick to *buying* options and not selling them, you don't need all the ins and outs — you're already protecting yourself, and the most you can lose is what you spend."
2. **Open a reputable options-enabled broker account.** Decent platforms break out the Greeks and vol analytics (implied, term structure, skew, realized, history). Named: Interactive Brokers, Tastyworks, TD Ameritrade; UK traders commonly use US brokers for access to the deep US listed market. Expect account-balance-based tiering: frequency limits and freezes on small accounts ("annoying... but they're trying to stop you doing anything stupid").
3. **Learn the basics → paper trade → go live small.** "Learn the basics, apply them on paper, build confidence, then start putting on real positions." Then live with positions: "feel the theta bleed every day, feel the vol resets, feel the gamma P&L. You won't really get it until you live and breathe it."
4. **Margin discipline.** Keep the book simple enough to replicate/close "in as few entries as possible." **Never use more than ~70–75% of available margin — keep a 20–30% buffer** for requirement spikes or faster-than-expected moves. Remember brokers can raise margin market-wide after events (GME) and you can be carried out *by the margin call, not the position*.
5. **When a trade goes wrong, shrink it; don't decorate it.** New traders hedge a problem with a new leg and end up "long, short, long, short, with no idea what the book looks like. If this position gives you a problem, cut it in half."
6. **Keep implementation consistent with conviction** (delta on directional views; ratios only for conditional-vol views).
7. **A first experiment:** if you already trade spread bets or futures, allocate part of that risk budget to an equivalent option trade and compare — "that futures position might get stopped out in a heartbeat while you're sleeping; the option's staying there for the next three months. It's got a natural stop. I know what I can lose. It keeps me in the game."
8. **Find your temperament.** Lakha's clients span "theta junkies" (sellers only) to conservatives who only buy one-year options. There is no single right profile — "all that matters is what works for you."
9. **Data/analytics for the curious:** SpotGamma (dealer gamma positioning); GVol for crypto vol analytics ("arguably better than TradFi analytics"); broker platforms for US-listed analytics; scenario-visualization websites for "if the stock goes +30% in six months, what's my option worth?"
10. **Expectations management:** the closing message — professional traders need ~6 months to get up to speed; "you don't need to be a math major... start super small and see if that suits you." And Lakha's keynote: **"Options don't have to be scary or risky. Once you understand them, they help you manage your risk. They give you a ridiculous amount of flexibility to express your views. They're called options for a reason."**

## Appendix A — Key Rules, Frameworks and Parameters

| # | Rule / framework | Parameter / detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Rule of 16** | Annualized vol ÷ 16 ≈ expected daily % move (16 vol = 1%/day; 32 = 2%; 48 = 3%; 40 = ~2.5%; 100 = ~6%) |
| 2 | **Cheap/expensive vol screen** | ATM implied vol percentile vs trailing 1-year own history; vol is mean-reverting; high vol can be cheap (realized 120 vs implied 80), low vol expensive (2017: realized 4–5, VIX 10) |
| 3 | **Expiry for directional views** | 4–12 weeks to "buy time to be right"; never let options run sub-~3 weeks near the money (binary gamma/theta); don't buy 1-month for a 1-year thesis; roll before the binary zone |
| 4 | **Put spread / call spread payoff rule** | Want ≥3–4x expiry payoff (pay 1 to make 4+); early monetization typically nets only ~half the expiry promise |
| 5 | **Risk reversal (collar), hedge version** | Long put + short higher call; classic 90/110; mismatch expiries optionally (6m put / 4m call); in upside-skew assets the call can fund a closer put |
| 6 | **Tactical risk reversal sizing** | 1–3 month expiry; sell call 10–20% OTM; call sale on ⅓–½ of the holding; do it when overbought but long-term bullish (gold 2020, silver 2022, Bitcoin 2021 examples) |
| 7 | **Stock replacement** | ATM/ITM calls, 6–12+ months; a 3-month OTM call is not stock replacement |
| 8 | **Systematic crash hedge** | Budget 50–100 bps/yr, regime-scaled; buy 1–2-month OTM puts; slice monthly; roll to keep 1–2 months of life; strike up as market rallies; monetize in the crash; escalate by raising budget or selling ~30-delta puts to buy 3–5x low-delta puts |
| 9 | **Hedge EV accounting** | Hedges have negative EV by design; net them against positive-EV alpha elsewhere — "if you don't have positive alpha, you can't afford the hedge" |
| 10 | **VIX facts** | VIX ≈ ATM 30-day S&P vol + 3–5 pts (skew-integrated variance calc); can't trade spot; futures = forward vols, contango bleeds ~1–2 pts/round; long VIX = imminent-event trade only; monetize spikes (Mar 2020 VXX +500%); safer short-VIX = VIX puts |
| 11 | **Event pricing** | Event kink = implied vol of event expiry minus adjacent expiries; convert forward vol to implied move (Google example: base 40 vol, +5–6 pts vs weeklies, ~5.1% implied move); vol reset hits within days of the event (2020 election −12 pts; ETH Merge 120→90) |
| 12 | **Pinning vs acceleration** | Street long gamma near big expiry + collapsing realized = pin; street short downside gamma + adverse move = dealers sell futures, move exaggerates into expiry, bounce after (Dec 2018, Mar 2020 lows on first day post-expiry); calibrate in gamma exposure, check vs % of daily volume |
| 13 | **Expiry calendar** | Monthly = third Friday; quarterlies Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec biggest; 2022: owning S&P *except* expiry weeks ≈ flat vs −20% buy-and-hold |
| 14 | **Skew signals** | Equities: persistent downside skew (crush down, grind up); commodities/crypto: often upside; FX: smile. Skew nosebleed + vol spike = peak panic, short-term low; retest of low on *falling* vol/skew = real bottom (2003, 2009, 2022); quiet put-bidding in a grinding rally = smart-money warning |
| 15 | **Cross-asset RV** | Compare vol + 25-delta skew across indices, oil, gold, copper, TLT, FX; same driver, cheaper vol wins (dollar-yen 12 vol realizing 14 vs TLT 21 vol realizing 13) |
| 16 | **Gamma squeeze mechanics** | Coordinated retail OTM call buying → dealer delta hedging + rising vol (vanna) → more hedging; watch upside OI build + borrow getting harder; vol resets violently when squeeze breaks (GME implied ~500) |
| 17 | **Short-option safety** | Never naked short single-stock calls; cap with spreads/wings ("own the wings, protect those tails"); credit spreads cut margin; without portfolio margin there's no Greek netting |
| 18 | **Income discipline** | Only sell strikes you're happy with come-what-may (buy at the put strike, sell at the call strike); "don't change your mind when it goes wrong"; selling = giving away optionality; expect touch-and-reverse misses |
| 19 | **Margin buffer** | Use ≤70–75% of available margin; expect market-wide requirement hikes after events |
| 20 | **Position hygiene** | Keep the book simplifiable to ~5–10 closing trades; answer "what/why/spot-vol-time view?"; fix problems by cutting size, not adding legs; count transaction costs if intraday |
| 21 | **Crypto options** | Deribit dominant, 1–2 vol-point markets; margin/premium in crypto makes short options "hockey sticks from hell" (dollar liability vs crypto collateral — BTC 20k→10k example wipes a 1-BTC account on an ATM put); US: BITO ETF options, CME micro options-on-futures, LedgerX |
| 22 | **Liquidity** | Option liquidity evaporates faster than underlying liquidity in crises; when the option market closes, monetize via delta (futures); beware peg/defended-level breaks — "all hell breaks loose" and there's no exit |

## Appendix C — Stories & War Stories

- **The VIX call ladder (Imran, early career).** Bullish the VIX but too cheap to pay full premium, he bought a call, sold a higher call, and sold a third call beyond that (open-ended upside risk far away). The VIX spiked 25→45 within his 1–2 month expiry — he was right, **and lost money**. "There's nothing worse in trading than being right and losing money. Being wrong and losing money is expected." The permanent lesson: **never cheapen a trade in a way that negates your positive scenario — if you can't afford the payoff structure, cut size instead.**
- **2010 — the liquidity lesson (Imran's worst year).** After great 2008–09, he was encouraged to warehouse huge risk — quoting clients **5 million vega** ($5m per vol point; a Euro Stoxx 20→21 move = $5m P&L). In April–May 2010 the Greek sovereign-debt crisis evaporated options liquidity in about a month: from trading 5m-vega clips instantly to "moving vol 2 points to do a couple of grand." Holding **variance swaps** (which *grow* when vol doubles, unlike vanilla options whose Greeks shut down far from the strike) with no way to hedge made it brutal. Lessons: option liquidity dies faster than underlying liquidity; in a closed option market you monetize through delta (futures); and never assume liquidity is permanent.
- **Gold 2013 (Roger's trade).** Post-GFC gold topped ~$1,900, ground down to test $1,500; fourth test came amid taper-tantrum yield fears with gold vol at a record-low **12–13** — a textbook "fundamental story + key technical level + cheap options" alignment. Support broke; gold cascaded $1,500→$1,200. The structure worked because options price statistical daily moves, not mind-inflection points at technical levels.
- **Bond yields 2022 (Roger's trade).** US 10-year yields in a reverse head-and-shoulders with a big level; asymmetric risk (yields might fall modestly, or "go up a hell of a lot"). Rather than shorting tech stocks, the cheapest expression was **TLT puts** — the same rates driver, clean level, reasonable vol — hedging a tech portfolio through the correlation.
- **The dot-com "100-vol only" client (Roger's).** A client who would only buy options trading at 100+ implied vol — apparently insane — because those names were *realizing* ~150. He traded the swings around long-gamma positions, carrying deliberate extra delta, flipping short delta at turns while the gamma stopped him out. Also the origin of the scalping-gamma technique (§5.1(f)).
- **The meme-stock hindsight trades.** GME realized vol printed ~999; implied hit ~500. Early participants who got in before dealers re-priced made fortunes; anyone buying *protection* afterwards paid 200 vol that halved in days — "getting your direction right on the stock and making nothing" via the wrong instrument.
- **The supermarket worker and XIV.** The face of the 2017 short-vol trade: retail compounding ~9% monthly-annualized on the rolling short — until February 2018 wiped the product to zero overnight (§4.5).
