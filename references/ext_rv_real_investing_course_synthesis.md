# The Real Investing Course (Real Vision Academy) — Consolidated Course Notes

**About this course.** The Real Investing Course is Real Vision Academy's flagship introduction to how professional investors actually think and operate. Rather than one instructor, it brings the Real Vision roster — Raoul Pal, Julian Brigden, Peter Brandt, Denise Shull, Pierre Andurand, Chris Cole, Darius Dale, Lyn Alden, Harris Kuperman, Dave Floyd, Mark Ritchie II, Tony Greer, Roger Hirst and others — to teach the full stack: what investing is, how markets are driven (secular, cyclical, tactical), how to read the business cycle with real indicators, charting as professionals use it, the macro plumbing of each asset class (FX, commodities, bonds, equities, digital assets), and finally trade construction, position sizing, and portfolio risk management. ## Foundations: Four Ideas and the Anatomy of an Investment
<!-- from lessons 1, 3 -->Theory versus reality: "in the real world, things never work out the way they looked in the textbook." Individual advice is unanswerable without knowing your goals, horizon, and risk tolerance ("Should I buy XYZ?" is like asking a supermarket stranger whether to buy this can of beans), and more market content is not the answer: "we are all drowning in that stuff." The method: learn from the best investors — studying how they think, build theses, execute, and manage — then **try, adapt, replace, evolve**. No quick fix; the goal is to **put the odds in your favor** and let that compound.**Four basic ideas to carry through everything:**1. **There are a million ways to make money in markets and they change with the times — but only a few consistent ways to lose money.** Learning how *not* to lose matters as much as learning to win.2. **Investing is personal**, and one of the only competitive domains where novices play side by side with professionals. Build your own approach — or an ongoing dialogue with your advisor.3. **Information is no longer an edge.** Novices gain an edge simply by distinguishing **signal from noise**.4. **The answer to almost every investing question is "it depends."** No formula solves markets; **judgment** beats any specific trade idea.**A profitable investment requires getting multiple things right at once:** thesis, time horizon, security selection, entry/exit, position size, risk management. Failure modes: a contrarian idea **without a catalyst** means fighting the market; right on everything but the **wrong time horizon** means profits taken too early or the round trip back to zero; most painful — right on everything but the **wrong instrument or too much leverage**, leaving you right *and* losing money.Course structure: (1) the game of investing, (2) market drivers, (3) charts, (4) the asset class universe, (5) building a portfolio. Guides: **Roger Hirst** (ex-Goldman Sachs/Morgan Stanley sales-trading), **Jamie McDonald** (ex-Point72 PM, insurance), **James Helliwell** (ex-Lex van Dam; multi-asset), hosted by **Maggie Lake**.

## The Inner Game: How Markets Actually Work
<!-- from lesson 3 -->**Markets are not a single entity.** "Mr. Market" is a fiction; under one price is "a confluence of ideas and an accumulation of positions across different time horizons."**The poker analogy (Denise Shull).** Markets are a global poker game: you win not with the cards but by **betting against other people's bets**. Neuroscience shows those best at predicting price action are **predicting people** — value is a collective social perception. **Markets are harder than poker:** relationships change (in poker aces mean aces; the bonds/dollar/gold/oil relationships shift), and the game never ends — new information constantly conflicts with old, there is no clock, and almost no one can make anything happen. **Sports analogies lose money** — in sports the point is to make something happen; in markets almost nobody can.Four lessons:1. **No formula or holy grail.** Kyle Bass's opening question to John Burbank — how do you size a trade? — gets the answer: "There are no answers that anybody knows… Everybody gets their time horizon wrong. Everybody gets their stop losses wrong… Nobody knows what they're doing, because there is no perfect answer. What there is, is a process of honing your skills — but never believe there is a holy grail." If a formula existed, prices would be flat, gapping only at binary events.2. **Non-stationarity.** Jared Dillian's analogy: mid-chess-game, the rules change and knights move diagonally. Markets are a complex adaptive system of **regimes** (low-vol, momentum, stocks/bonds negatively correlated) that break; keep trading the old regime "and you're going to get carried out." A strong-dollar short-commodities view can fail — some environments saw both rise together; your portfolio may rely on relationships that already broke.3. **The game never ends, so you must exit.** Proactive decisions, systems, or decision-minimization all work: dollar-cost averaging monthly regardless of price, or salary-deduction pension plans, reduce open-ended decisions.4. **It's not personal.** "The markets don't know you people. They don't know where you live… why do you take a loss personally? It's not a personality defect. It's a loss." You work through many losses to find the trades that matter.**Sentiment can't be quantified:** "People try to quantify finance. **Finance resists all attempts to quantify.**" Raw-emotion markets — March 2020 — are often **the most profitable**, since you're trading pure sentiment.**War story — the $700,000 lesson (a trader addressed as "Mr. Dillion" in the transcript).** Long BAX futures (Canadian Bankers' Acceptances — short-term rates), betting rates fall, he returned from vacation in 2017 to find them down ~16 ticks after deputy Bank of Canada governor Carolyn Wilkins hinted at hikes. "Canada is on the verge of recession. This is impossible." He **picked a fight with the Bank of Canada**, which hiked five times; he refused to sell, bottoming about **$700,000** down, and rolled the futures **three years** — until the 2020 pandemic cut rates to zero, when he made it back and doubled it. "The story has a happy ending, but that is my worst trade, because I was so undisciplined." Lesson: **the desire to be right is a flaw — the goal is to make money, not to be right.** Conserve capital; never fight a position.**Play the world as it is (Dan McMurtry).** Don't invest for the world you *want*: do the analysis and place money on what will actually happen. "My clients have not hired me to be a financial justice warrior… They've hired me to protect their portfolio and make them money." And: "I will not go down on my own sword. I don't do holy wars anymore." A just cause is not a catalyst.**Emotions are information; emotionalism is the enemy.** Don't swing from taking losses personally to becoming a robot. "The hero and the coward feel the same; they just act differently." Denise Shull: "Your emotions are information. But the only thing you have to control is your behavior — your choice." Build "a dictionary on yourself" mapping physical experiences to their meanings — without emotional conviction you couldn't decide at all. She warns against rote "plan the trade, trade the plan" discipline-worship: it is not how the human brain assesses "the hardest game on the planet."

## Defining Your Own Game: Motivation, Style, and Framework
<!-- from lesson 4 -->**Be honest about three things:** what you're trying to accomplish, what method you're comfortable with, and over what time frame. A trader can't force themselves to be a long-term investor or vice versa. "Marry your personal style and strengths to how you actually invest — if they're in conflict, you're not going to have a good result." Rising emotions are a diagnostic: losing sleep means something is out of sync (position too big, or not understood).**Start with "What do you want?"** Maximal wealth — and *for what*? Or providing for family and playing long? **Path versus outcome:** never take path risk that could get "really weird" just to slightly tilt your outcome; don't go all-in gold or lever up on end-of-world views. Keep it simple: diversified portfolio, grown over time, taking care of family. Constraints matter as much as goals — especially **path risk**: how will you make decisions mid-drawdown? Paraphrasing the famous saying: **if you don't know who you are and what you want, the market is an expensive place to find out.** Exercise: write your motivation, then your last trade — do they match?**Two style buckets (Jason Buck and Chris Cole).** People enter markets through "the world of Buffett" (value investing, long-term business fundamentals via stocks and bonds) or "the world of Market Wizards" (Jack Schwager's traders — strict technical or macro lenses; the asset is just a vehicle). Cole's path: tried value in the early 2000s, Larry McMillan's option strategies, then systematic strategies — systematic/vol stuck. Buck's favorite *Market Wizards* lesson: **many strategies work; the edge is finding the one that fits your personality so you can stick to it for the long haul.****Framework = simply a set of parameters for how you invest:** motivation, time horizon, asset classes, markets, instruments, risk tolerance. Start simple; evolve.**Pierre Andurand's rules (energy trader):** step back, big picture, not noise; **only liquid instruments** you can exit when right — cut losses, "survive and live to fight another day"; **never short options** (flat or long) — the tail risk doesn't fit him; **don't trade a lot** — very short-term moves are noise, medium-term moves are trend; harvest noise with algorithms, which don't get emotionally exhausted.Diagnostic questions: motivation and life stage (twenties can afford more mistakes); time horizon (intraday to decades); how much capital; **what risks wouldn't you take**; fundamental vs. technical/macro; asset-class/instrument preferences; and are you even interested — if not, use professionals. Revisit answers regularly; run a long-term portfolio *and* a short-term trading account; diversify across horizons.**Two books in one head — Tony Greer.** His **trading book** ("shooting gallery"): tight position/trading/loss limits, aiming to "shoot big game" daily/weekly/monthly; method: study **the closes and performance of every equity index, commodity, and sector ETF — daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly** — building a mosaic of what's working; long what works, short what deteriorates; cut losses fast, trail winners. His **investment book** (retirement): reviewed quarterly to twice yearly, rotated incrementally (e.g., slowly out of tech into natural resources), two-to-four rebalances a year. When hearing anyone's idea, ask: **"Is that for the trading book or the investing book?"****Easy bets, not conviction bets.** "My goal is to keep my process where I don't need heroic bets — 200% off the bottom going all in, risking blowing my fund up… because I want to play a 50-year or 70-year game."**The five capitals** (from a short book the speakers call *Oikonomics*): **financial, physical, intellectual, relational, spiritual**. The wealthy show high depression/anxiety/suicidality — the speakers' argument: they sacrificed the other capitals for financial ones. Track all five like a dashboard; people "throw in the towel too early on anything" — the holistic flywheel keeps you in the game.**Decision preconditions — Dan McMurtry.** Studying why brilliant people (PhDs, Olympians) blow up: **they were unable to make a good decision** — body, mind, or social situation. The finance reflex "you didn't work hard enough" is wrong; extended stress impairs even basic decisions, and the spiral runs: stress → stress eating → skipping the gym → friction with loved ones → **self-isolation** → more stress. Practices:- **Track conversations** (inbounds, outbounds): during extreme stress the count drops **60–80%** — the earliest visible warning.- **15-minute heart-rate rule:** sustained elevated heart rate resets the brain's stress interpretation ("this is the fight I was waiting for"); his non-negotiable is 15 minutes of light cardio daily — "once you get to minute 16, it's insane."- **Prioritize sleep:** the extra 2–3 night hours are a bad trade in a judgment role.The standard is not brilliance: **consistently good decisions compound**; in the worst moment, an okay decision is priceless. Napoleon defined genius as doing the average thing when everyone else loses his mind (cf. Kipling).**Survival first.** Excess leverage means **you can be forced to trade** — forced out of positions, or into forcing your family into hardship. Read *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator*. McMurtry regularly de-risks into very low-risk assets so his family is protected if income turns variable — never mortgage the house on a Twitter conviction bet. Covering the downside is what lets you take big calculated bets later.

## Idea Generation: Own Every Idea You Buy
<!-- from lesson 5 -->**First ingredient: it's your idea** — not necessarily originated by you, but owned through your own process. People skip this to make money fast. TV, newsletters, Twitter, TikTok, Reddit are fine raw sources; wholesale adoption is not. The essential skill is **context**: ideas come wrapped in someone else's framework, portfolio, and life. "Conviction," at best unclear and at worst reckless, is no substitute. A Real Vision member's confession: newsletter trades left him with "a hodgepodge of discrete trades that may or may not work in a neat framework" — so when markets moved against him, he didn't know what to do. Curated research is an **input**, not trades to copy.**Worked example — Mark Ritchie II trades someone else's theme.** He never bought Real Vision's "buy bonds, wear diamonds" bias (Raoul Pal's refrain) and traded the long end (10s, 30s), not the front end. Then John Burbank and Alex Gurevich presented the front-end idea using **calls** with real **asymmetry**: "a scenario where if my view is wrong, this should work — and a scenario where if my view is right, this may still work." He bought futures first (they went nowhere), then calls, watched price action until the technicals made sense, and added — wishing in hindsight he'd added 10x. Transferable rules: the best traders are **incremental** (no all-in days); to test interest, **buy a little — a position so small it seems meaningless** — to get a pulse; if unsure you're too big, **take a little off**; outside your confidence zone, keep it small.A great investment is **buying the right amount at the right time and selling the right amount at the right time**. If you're not in constant contact with the idea's source, you won't know what to do — and they don't know your portfolio; only you do.**Twitter (Dan McMurtry and Chris Brown).** "Probably the most powerful research tool available" — seniors dismiss it, yet invest in technology whose internet culture they don't understand. The edge: "hundreds of thousands of people working on a subject instead of the six sell-side analysts who cover it" — *if* you learn the community's language and build reputation. **Don't build an echo chamber** — the commonest misuse is following opinion-confirmers. War story: Jim Chanos tweeted the **Tilray/Aphria arb** had blown out absurdly; Brown put it on pre-market the next morning and made roughly **$600–800,000 within hours**. Tony Greer's counterpoint: he deleted social apps — go in **with a purpose**, don't let the feed dictate behavior. Rules: learn the community's language; follow respected voices; run every idea through your own process; beware overload and news paralysis.**Traditional media — ask "who is saying this and why?"** Financial TV optimizes for entertainment, not correctness — expect one-sided views. Bank analyst reports carry positive bias from corporate relationships ("conflict of interest is rampant at large financial institutions") — use for facts and data, then dig yourself. **Magazine cover indicator:** a financial theme on a mainstream cover is fully known and probably fully invested — late cycle. BusinessWeek's "Death of Equities" marked the 1979 low; The Economist's "$5 oil" marked the oil low; one study found ~**65–70%** of bullish Economist-cover markets lower within ~**six months**. The question: **who is left to buy?****Information abundance is the modern problem.** More information used to be an edge; now the edge is knowing **what's important and what's noise**. You'll never have complete certainty; at some point make a **probabilistic assessment** and decide. McMurtry's filter: "**What do I need to have a view on to take XYZ risk — and what can I manage?**" If he can offload cognitive load via portfolio structure, he does: "I want the bets that I need to know the littlest about… I'm getting a higher Sharpe in terms of my process." Conflicting expert views usually reflect different styles, horizons (three-month trade vs. eighteen-month thesis), or existing positions — understand full context before dismissing either side.**The catalyst.** Ideas need the spark that changes perceptions — "something being cheap or expensive is not a catalyst; it means nothing." Examples: the housing bubble bursting collapsed the over-levered system; in 2014 oversupply was the oil bear case but the **rising dollar** likely tipped prices lower; uranium's clear bull thesis sat dormant until the 2020 pandemic sparked it; Tesla shorts fought 2019–2022 without a reversal catalyst. Conversely, when riding a profitable trend, watch for the catalyst that reverses *your* thesis — know when your idea is wrong.**Checklist for external ideas:** Who is saying it and what's their incentive? What type of investor are they? Does their time horizon align with mine? How is it structured (position size) and how does it fit *their* portfolio? Do I have similar exposures already? Does it fit my portfolio and my life?

## Market Drivers: Secular, Cyclical, Tactical
<!-- from lessons 6, 7 -->**The frame.** "In the short term the market is a voting machine; in the long run, a weighing machine" — every security reaches true value eventually, but volatility on the way fools many. Right-and-early is common and expensive: you could have seen subprime in 2006–07 and still bled through false starts until late 2008. **Good ideas often fail to make money because timeframes are misunderstood.**Definitions: **traders** — nimble, opportunistic, monetizing short-term sentiment and news flow; **investors** — broader macro, theses playing out over years; most people blend. Both coexist because **markets are driven on multiple horizons simultaneously**. Jamie McDonald held some positions for years and some for hours — know each position's horizon and its driver there.**The three categories:**- **Secular** — decades+: seismic shifts in **demographics, debt, technology, globalization**. Cases: US boomers entering the workforce in the 60s–70s (now exiting); China's 2001 WTO entry.- **Cyclical** — months to a few years: the **business cycle**, policy changes, liquidity. Example: the unprecedented pandemic policy response powered an ~18-month cyclical upturn across major assets.- **Tactical** — days to weeks: **sentiment and news flow**. Example: a scandal headline drops a stock 20%; homework shows the move was emotional, so buy the bounce for ~10% over a day; or data-release volatility.Whatever your dominant horizon, know where you are on **all three** — regimes change, exogenous shocks (wars, pandemics) happen, and comfort with your trend can blind you to a regime shift that invalidates it.**Trend duration is unknowable — in time and in distance.** From dot-com and 2006–08: everyone knew "this time is different" was bollocks, yet nearly everyone was short too early; the bubble can run 50% further first. Institutions played manias from the long side; hedge funds tried to catch the turn and mostly ended up long — "you play the trend even though it is ludicrous in its size, and hope you can shift out" (a small-AUM advantage; long-only can't). Roger, judged annually, aimed at **1% a month** and lived weeks-to-months; most practitioners converge in the middle.**Blend all three:** a 20-year equities-over-bonds theme; a tech preference within a growth-phase view; a two-week earnings bet on one stock.**Secular trends need catalysts too.** Jamie's insurance example: shorting life insurers on longevity demographics was a ten-year bet with no catalyst — chronically hard. 2008 taught the same: right on overheating in 2006–07, paid only when the 2008 event supplied the catalyst. **No catalyst, no inflection — you can be far too early.****Policy super-cycles within the demographic super-cycle.** **1945–1980: interventionist/fiscal** (spending and taxes; became inflationary — OPEC era). Around **1980: flip to free-market/monetary** (Volcker, Thatcher, Reagan; central banks dominant; rates trended down 40 years). As of 2022's spike, is another inflection — back to interventionist/fiscal — underway? And 2022's inflation differs from the 1970s: then demand-driven with real wages rising ("everyone says stagflation, but there was growth"); now fiscal stimulus + supply disruption + demand destruction.**Demographics — the deepest driver.** Oxford demographer **Danny Dorling**: the *rate of change* of world population growth **peaked in 1971** — the decade of inflation. Mechanism: expect 100 million new people and 200 million arrive → inflationary; expect 2 billion and 1 billion arrive → deflationary, even though 1 billion is far more in absolute terms.**Practical cyclical framework:** identify the era (monetary vs. interventionist), then use the **yield curve (2s10s)** as the growth gauge — rising curve = growth cycle, historically three-to-four years before peaking ("leverage up my positions") — and always test the thesis.**How much of return is macro?** One rule of thumb: **~80%**. The discussion's decomposition: roughly **40–50% the asset class** (equity beta), **20–30% the sector** (whether financials are in vogue, whether flows come), the remainder security-selection alpha, amplified by leverage with controlled risk. Hence: play the expansion first, then pick the leader (China) within it.**Case study — China joins the WTO (2001).** "A billion people we didn't realize were in the population." China hoovered commodities at a record pace; EM commodity currencies outperformed, the dollar weakened, and the weak dollar boosted the commodity cycle further — self-reinforcing. It *felt* like it ended with the August 2008 commodity squeeze, but demand ran to roughly **2016**. Simultaneously, the US **leverage cycle**: the Fed hiked 25bp per meeting, always behind the market; cheap capital fed mortgage leverage; together they built the 2008 bubble — oil at **$150 in 2008** (inflation-adjusted, above 2022's peaks).**Cycles within cycles.** Demographic supercycle → policy shift (fiscal → monetary) → post-2009 **QE cycle** (the Fed balance sheet became a new factor; everyone expected CPI inflation, got **asset price inflation** instead) → post-pandemic fiscal return with shocking price levels.**Sector reality check (Jamie's insurance experience).** Long-good/short-bad insurers, hedged macro — but the non-life re-rating stalled because "financials were just out of favor. No one gave a shit about financials… **You need the flows to come.**" Deep relative-value requires knowing companies through their accounts — hard for newcomers; macro building blocks are easier (commodity currency up ⇒ growth; EM ↔ copper ↔ Aussie dollar). Past relationships never guarantee future ones — keep testing.

## The Present as Data: Reflexivity, Real-Time Indicators, and Changing Reaction Functions
<!-- from lesson 8 -->**Most macro data is coincident.** Macro data (ISM, consumer confidence) mostly **coincides with the equity market**. Why: in the monetary era corporates stopped capex and bought back shares, so corporate sentiment rose with the market — creating **reflexivity**: higher markets → higher sentiment readings → more risk appetite → higher markets. Since 2000, markets show "up, collapse, bounce straight back out" pockets instead of the 2000–03 peak-then-grind-down — the data and the market define each other.**Read the rate of change of asset prices instead.** If the momentum of a growth-proxy asset rolls over, the economy probably turns — and the ISM confirms later. Within the ISM, watch **subcomponents**, especially **new orders versus inventories**.**Real-time data era.** Macro's old informational edge (talking to central banks first) is gone; big data — footfall, travel-site traffic, real-time transactions — gives faster clues, and the **consumer cycle now leads the business cycle**, not the reverse.**Retail investors and instruments.** Retail was huge pre-2000, hurt by dot-com, subdued for twenty years while **passive replaced active**; returned "with a vengeance" in the pandemic (WallStreetBets "the main mania of 2020"). The durable change: **almost any asset anywhere is reachable via an ETF** — paying 1% to a manager who will basically *be* the market is unnecessary.**Crypto as secular trend.** The dawn of **decentralised finance/Web3** probably is one — a more efficient way of doing business; the ferocity of the tribal battle itself signals change. Still retail-driven, with institutions entering gradually.**Cycle lengths may shorten.** Business cycles: **1991–2000 (nine years), 2000–2008 (eight), 2008–2020 (twelve — the longest ever)**. If GDP volatility and the policy reaction function pick up, the future may resemble the 1960s–70s: **two-year cycles instead of ten** — "apply a tactical, hit-and-run mentality to a cyclical trend." Nothing is in stone — **flexibility is what macro ultimately is**.**Central bank reaction functions change.** The Fed ran a **growth mandate** (every growth scare → cuts/QE); it shifted to a **price mandate** (easing while inflation is high = failure) — "from supporting growth to capping price," which supported equities a decade but cuts both ways. EM central banks were always price-focused; China (little CPI) could keep supporting growth. **Japan:** the BOJ has fought **demographic deflation since 1989** and won't pivot on a tick of inflation — yield-capping forces the **yen weaker** (2.5% in the US vs. 25bp in Japan → outflows), with imported commodity prices adding pressure. Japan's *standard of living* nonetheless rose through 30 flat years ("GDP is an abomination"). **Abenomics 2012** was the exception: the macro crowd said "Japan always fails," but the bazooka plus explicit yen weakening doubled the Nikkei from ~8,000 to ~16,000 in about a year (Roger exited clients at +50% — see charts).**Forward secular trends named:** peak globalisation/de-globalisation (supply chains, chips, energy at home — underway before the pandemic and Ukraine); demographics (can India — or India + Indonesia + Southeast Asia — repeat China's move? Possible but needs deliberate leadership); a rising-rate environment. Metaphor: secular trends are **supertankers** — they define everything but turn slowly ("I'll be dead before the end of the next supertanker trend"); trade the cyclical and tactical layers, more hit-and-run as cycles compress.

## From the Pros: Tailwinds, Regimes, and the Long-Term Drivers
<!-- from lessons 9, 10 -->**Harris Kupperman (Praetorian Capital) — the macro tailwind.** "There are a lot of cheap stocks in this world. **Cheap stocks stay cheap.**" Find the **macro tailwind** first — it pushes revenue and margins and "bails you out of your mistakes" — then drop down and pick the best-structured, cheapest way to play the theme. People love themes and narratives (valuation "is just a spreadsheet thing… always backwards looking"); when a theme catches, moves are huge — play themes "with less risk and upside still."**Chris Cole (Artemis) — diversify by regime, not asset class.** Choose diversifiers by **which regime they win in**, not by trailing correlations. The optimal portfolio: **~one-fifth in each of five buckets** (the **Dragon Portfolio**):1. **Equity-linked / secular-growth** — equities, real estate, value; wins in growth.2. **Interest-rate-linked** — bonds.3. **Fiat alternatives** — win in rampant inflation or distrust of central government; gold is canonical.4. **Long volatility** — wins in turbulence.5. **Commodity trend** — trend-following in commodities/currencies; wins in deflation/stagflation (the 1970s).At any point in history one to three buckets produce **nonlinear returns that carry the portfolio** — through the 1930s, the 1970s, and the 40-year growth era. 2020 was the perfect case study: "an entire business cycle in 12 months."**Raoul Pal — how a macro investor works.** Macro = the macroeconomic impact on asset prices; data arrives monthly/quarterly — **the fewer the data points, the longer your time horizon must be**. Starting routine: **pull 50-year charts of every asset**. Secular facts: equities up; yields down; currencies in bands; gold rises. The long drivers:- **Demographics — the biggest driver.** Boomers drove equity valuations up relative to the economy (market-cap-to-GDP, P/Es) and **disinflation**: older households have less marginal consumption and shift into fixed income, lowering yields.- **Debt.** Companies issued debt to buy back stock; more debt makes raising rates structurally harder — rates fall over time under the debt burden.- Other views: technology/SaaS (Stan Druckenmiller's favorite; equity-specific, outside Pal's zone), and Pal's **"monsoon countries"** — Indian-Ocean region (India, Middle East and neighbors) with favorable demographics and low debt; be a buyer into large sell-offs. Secular views give a **bias**, not a trade.**Lyn Alden — the inflation question.** "Most inflation is transitory, but **transitory can last for years** — the 70s were 'transitory.'" Base case: **the 2020s more inflationary than the 2010s**, 1970s/1940s-like — but never a straight line: inflationary decades contain disinflationary waves (price and wage controls; overdone positioning unwinding and re-spiking — European natural gas). Money supply ↔ CPI correlate strongly on **five-year rolling periods**, but disconnects occur when technology unlocks abundance: the late 1800s (internal combustion engine, electrification, oil, unlimited US land) and the past 25 years (globalized labor arbitrage — money growth without domestic wage inflation). That force is exhausting — **peak globalization** (stopping acceleration, not unwinding) is incrementally inflationary.**Debt and growth — four consistent research findings:** (1) a little debt is good — lifts output above potential, pro-inflationary; (2) a lot is bad for growth; (3) the threshold is remarkably consistent across dozens of studies (not just Reinhart–Rogoff): **80–90% of GDP**, applying to household, business, government, *or* financial sectors; (4) beyond it, the decline is **nonlinear** — an upside-down U (US 20-year annualized real GDP growth now shows that shape). Mechanism: too much debt → private investment suffers → infrastructure deteriorates → overcapacity and excess labor → lower real growth → lower inflation.**The cardinal discipline:** don't mistake cyclical upturns for new secular regimes (the last decade's recurring mistake — "you ran into the secular ceiling"); don't let a 10–20-year secular view (dollar/fiat collapse) blind you to the **business cycle in the middle**.**Business cycle shape (Pal).** Up-cycles **4–8 years** (the last the longest ever); down-cycles **18 months–3.5 years**. Simplest play: **buy risk assets at the trough and hold through the expansion**. Operational rule: when the **ISM crosses 50 on the way up** (a properly formed economic base — he judged 2022 not to be one, but allowed error), hold risk assets **until it crosses back down through 50**. His secular thesis (yields fall) matched his cyclical thesis, so he refused to short bonds — why fight both? Shorter-horizon traders trade the sentiment instead.**Cycle-peak pivots — Darius Dale (42 Macro).** At the peak of the growth and/or inflation sine waves: **high-grade quality** in credit; **high-grade capitalization** in equities; **pivot back into bonds** from riskier assets and commodities; and **buy the dollar and buy gold**. Obvious in theory, brutal in the moment — backward data still looks hot and "the biggest bubble I see out there is the bubble in narratives."**Liquidity — four channels at once (Jem Carson).** When the Fed withdraws liquidity: (1) direct **asset demand falls** (less Fed buying; less liquidity for buybacks/LBOs); (2) **higher rates** kill TINA — alternatives appear; (3) the **discount rate** worsens, undermining NPV-accretive investment; (4) — the long-vol specialist's emphasis — **risk premia and volatility rise**, so risk-adjusted equity returns deteriorate. Why bubbles happen despite everyone seeing them: strong monetary policy is a virtuous cycle; withdrawal is the opposite.**Financial conditions — what policymakers actually target (Julian Brigden).** Fed funds itself "has literally no relevance to anything we do in the economy." **Financial conditions = five metrics**: short rates, long rates, credit costs, the dollar, equities — tracked via the **Chicago Fed** and **Goldman Sachs** indices; they capture mortgages (~7-year yields), car loans, wealth, corporate borrowing, and import costs. Powell said it explicitly: policy works through financial conditions. "If Fed funds go up and nothing else responds — did you tighten financial conditions? Did the tree fall in the woods?" The US is uniquely equity-sensitive vs. bond-oriented savers elsewhere — US financial conditions dictate when the Fed stops and starts.**Tactical view — Tony Greer.** "I'm a reactionary trader… The Fed is just another window on my dashboard." His mechanism: **the Fed balance sheet** — while taper was discussed, it had added **$1.7 trillion** that year with no sign of backing off; for him that was the mechanism driving equities. Common thread: every pro has a clear **framework** and respects other horizons even when not trading them.

## Workshop: Reading the Cycle with Real Indicators
<!-- from lessons 11, 12 -->*(Roger Hirst leads.)***Trends within trends — the fractal market.** The "chart of truth": 10-year Treasury yields over 60 years. Two secular waves: yields **rising ~1960–1980**, **falling 1980–2022** — the two policy eras: **1945–1980 interventionist/fiscal** (spending, taxes; boomer demographics) and **1980–2022 free-market/monetary** (Reagan/Thatcher; rates dominate). On a log scale the downtrend is no straight line — sharp cyclical oscillations inside. Zoom to 1993–94: the Fed's hawkish surprise produced one of the toughest bond sell-offs of many careers — and fortunes. Lesson: a secular bond bull who started in early 1994 was right about the next 20 years but might not have survived the entry. Zoom further — down to intraday — and the swings continue: **markets are fractal; trends within trends within trends.** And always **test whether the trend is breaking** — each test of the yield channel's top raised "is it over?"; the 2022 inflation spike was doing something completely different from the 40-year trend.**Secular variables checklist:** demographics (population-growth rate peaked ~1971; Japan's working-age 18–64 population down ~**15% since the mid-1990s** with GDP per capita flat — technology substituting for workers; India's growth supports output if resources keep up — the Pal/Grant Williams "monsoon" theme); debt (total household + corporate + government debt/GDP rising for decades); technology (steam, internal combustion, electric grid, autos, air travel, computers, internet, big data, crypto); globalization (China 2001 — commodity prices soared yet consumer prices stayed contained because globalization + technology offset input costs; **peak globalization** plus the renewable transition could mean a secular phase of **commodities outperforming stocks** — an observation, not a prediction).**Stylized business cycle and asset behavior** (all "very stylized" — each cycle differs):- **Early expansion:** economically sensitive cyclicals lead (industrials, basic materials); industrial metals too; EM commodity-exporter currencies outperform in a global cycle; inflation starts rising, bond prices fall.- **Growth moderating:** steady-earnings **growth stocks** outperform (annoyingly, "growth" here ≠ economic growth).- **Rolling over:** defensives (utilities); correlations push toward **1 on the downside** — everything sells off together — consider exiting risk altogether.- **Recession:** bonds have historically rallied, yields falling.Caveats: in the Eurozone crisis (2011–12) some yields *rose* while growth slowed (default fears). Post-1980 cycles extended; the 1970s–early 80s produced recessions in quick succession — **the secular backdrop defines the cycle's length and depth**, and policymakers tinker (rates, balance sheets, regulation) to manage **financial conditions**.**Indicator taxonomy: leading, coincident, lagging.** Focus on the US (still the main global driver), knowing other economies have their own drivers and equivalents.**Yield curve (10y−2y).** Watched for **inversion** (2y > 10y). Facts: inversion precedes recession but by **9 to 34 months** — weak timing. Since 1990 the **deepest inversion point** often coincided with something near the S&P peak — but you never know you're at the deepest point, so it can't tell you when to sell. In the 1970s the dynamic differed: inversion still preceded recession but **continued deepening into it** — high-inflation regimes signal differently.**ISM manufacturing PMI.** Monthly business-sentiment survey, richer than its headline; manufacturing is watched for sensitivity to activity. Rules:- **50** = expansion/contraction threshold.- Post-1990, recessions begin near **~45** — but the ISM has been under 50 many times without recession.- 1970s recessions began with the ISM **above 50 or even 60** — regime-dependent.- Largely **coincident with equities**: S&P YoY change mirrors ISM momentum — combine with other indicators for timing.- The lead sits in subcomponents: **new orders minus inventories** slightly leads the headline (negative = no excess demand; often consumption pulled forward, inventories built, demand waning). Read **respondents' comments** by sector, cross-referenced to the **NAICS** industry/stock database.**Consumer and small-business sentiment.** **NFIB Small Business Optimism + University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment**: consumption ≈ **70% of US GDP**, and small business employs more than ISM's large-firm respondents; combined falls relate visibly to recessions.**Central bank balance sheets.** Post-GFC QE expansion accompanied a decade grind higher in US equities; the 2018 shrink attempt was abandoned ("the Fed capitulated") as the S&P wobbled. Perverse detail: **QE ran anticyclical to yields** — bond-buying periods saw yields *rise*; no-buying periods saw yields *fall*: yields fell when the outlook weakened, forcing intervention; "Pavlovian conditioning of safety" lifted yields as the economy stabilized; stepping back, things rolled over.**Credit spreads.** Corporate-vs-government same-maturity yields — a top real-economy health indicator. Widening = rising cost of capital = slowing business; "unlikely we'll experience a recession if corporate funding costs are still extremely loose." The **HY−IG spread** blows out as investors shun junk; like inversions, often precedes recession — and reverses when the Fed cuts.**Inflation cycles.** Every CPI peak on record occurred **during** a recession — except 2000 (just before) and the exogenous COVID-era spike of 2022. Surges squeeze margins and trigger tighter policy; timing the peak in real time is extremely hard.**Don't forget China.** In 2016, amid a global profits recession, China had "stuffed its channels with credit" — the tell was **commodity stocks outperforming the broader market**. Long-term watch-item: China's shift from biggest *rate of change* of raw-materials demand toward consumption. Regional surveys (Germany, Japan) are equally valid for your own framework.**The open 2022 question:** was the inflation spike the first cyclical shot of a **new secular regime** (higher inflation base, shorter cycles) or a one-off?**Tactical trading.** If the cycle shortens or volatility rises, tactics matter more. The dot-com bust: the cyclical trade (short) was practically brutal — **two ~20% rallies in 2001 alone** stopped out shorts; **Nasdaq 100 volatility averaged ~50% through 2001–2002** (scale: the S&P is called volatile when the **VIX exceeds 20**). Tactical triggers: news flow and events; data releases and earnings; **major options expiries** (the December 2018 and March 2020 S&P lows came the first trading day after a major expiry); **sentiment extremes**. Events price in advance: the 2020 lows arrived around lockdown announcements. Tactics are timing devices for entries/exits, mattering less to multi-year investors. Homework: which indicators will *you* start with, and how often checked — then play with the data (St. Louis Fed's FRED has wonderful charts).

## Charts: Your Best Friend and Worst Enemy
<!-- from lessons 13, 14, 15 -->**What a chart is.** "A visual representation of the ideas, emotions, and positioning of millions of market participants across multiple time horizons." Corollary: **two people trading the same security in opposite directions can both make money** — different horizons.**What it is not: a predictor.** You *can* make money from charts alone — some do, with discipline and framework (chartists like Peter Brandt, Dave Floyd, Tom DeMark) — but for months-to-years investing charts are one part of the case: an excellent **filter and history lesson**, seductive enough to make you think you see the future.Operating rules: match the **chart's timeframe to the thesis's timeframe**; **unless the chart confirms your thesis, you will lose money** — if price disagrees, another set of investors knows something you don't; reassess ("tens of thousands of tradable assets — why does it have to be this one?"); and regime changes alter behavior (recession → historical ranges break).**"A chart looks great until it doesn't."** Roger's former colleague Andy Billet's catchphrase: the perfect bottoming pattern, the bounce off the level — then cover the last month with paper and see it went straight down.**What one glance gives you:** price history, key levels, inflection points; yields and valuations, not just price; a frame of reference ("history doesn't repeat, but it rhymes"); and — since technical analysis is partly **self-fulfilling** — a read on mass psychology. **Fibonacci** is used sparingly precisely because it is self-fulfilling — it hands you **hard price levels to react off**.**Peter Brandt: "The chart doesn't define the direction."** It gives **management tools**: play the break if a pattern breaks, but know it might be a false break — maybe 50/50 — so pre-define the get-out level and profit levels from the chart. A head-and-shoulders guarantees nothing; if price follows the classic formation, build trades around it.**War story — the head-and-shoulders that nearly killed him (Roger, 2015–16).** The S&P set up a classic top, looking like 2000–01 and 2007–08; in 2016 it broke the neckline. "There but for the grace of God go I" — he was away skiing the week he would have told clients to go maximum short; by his return the break had reversed and the market "didn't look back for another three or four years." Context: a global profits recession from the commodity bust, ISM below 50, the Fed trying to hike (blinking after China's mini-devaluation) — then **China unleashed credit** (commodity stocks turned *early 2016* — the missed tell) and a **G20 accord in Shanghai** stabilized the dollar; "everything just shot off."**The screening workflow.** Mark Ritchie: ~**200 charts a day**, pick the ~**5** best setups, trade them with **tight stops**, rules-based. The best macro investors start identically — find the breakout, then check fundamentals: **technicals + fundamentals agreeing = a much more powerful idea** (still with stops). The backwards approach — love a story, then hunt for a chart — is weaker.**Structural calls made with charts + macro:**- **2010–11, S&P vs. EM:** consensus said "EM is the bull market," but the S&P/EM ratio formed a **reverse head-and-shoulders** while Deutsche Bank's strategists called a dollar bull run (EM outperforms when the dollar is weak). You may not know where the dollar goes, but you own the conditional. Raoul's simplicity test: "You could give these charts to a small child — 'if that happens, that happens.'" The chart can't tell you **when the relationship itself breaks** — monitoring that is your job.**Flow and positioning charts.** Chart **flows**: weekly gold-ETF inflows vs. spot, where divergences can presage pressure. Extremes can also mean "something's broken": in 2008 at Morgan Stanley, a strategist's overbought/oversold indicator hit insanely oversold; one read was "bounce now," the right one was **step change — Lehman followed**.**Classic retail mistakes:** catching falling knives; picking bottoms and tops; anchoring to prior prices; emotional trading; loading up without homework; taking profits too early. If there's momentum behind it — which you get from the chart — it can be expensive, more expensive, yet even more expensive." Mirror: some of the **biggest sell-offs start from oversold**, running over "it's cheap now" buyers. Go with momentum and trend, not the so-called experts on valuation.**Bear-market anatomy.** After a big sell-off, the first bounce brings fundamental buyers and bottom-pickers — but half the market is trapped underwater, waiting to "sell this rally." Caveats: volume's signal has diminished — ETF/passive flows and dark pools distort the daily bar. Where it still works: **capitulation volume** — a big down-move on huge volume (heaviest on the down leg, not the rebound) means the "get me out" crowd cleared; a contrarian bottom indicator. **Crypto**: volume and emotion still read cleanly — one of the purest traders' markets left, like futures in the 1980s.**Charts across asset classes?** Yes — Brandt and Floyd trade everything; "close your eyes — you can't tell whether it's dollar-yen or copper."

## The Professional's Approach to Charts: Timing and Risk, Not Prediction
<!-- from lesson 16 -->Charts alone are not enough evidence to trade on unless you're an experienced technical practitioner. What they're superb for is **timing** and **risk management** — the best hedge fund managers start with the chart.**Peter Brandt** (profitable trading chart construction for 45 years):- "Chart patterns in and of themselves do not provide a significant edge... They're overrated." They do not predict direction.- The edge is trade and risk management: "It's not in direction. It's more in timing." Patterns show the *path of least resistance*, prior support, asymmetric reward-to-risk — "a way for me to pull the trigger and know where I'm wrong."- A good trade must be right **directionally** *and* **tactically**; wrong on either, it's a wrong trade.- **Reliability decays down the timeframes**: one-minute charts "morph" more than five-minute, than ten-minute, hourly, two-hour, six-hour, daily, weekly. A counter-trend pattern on a low timeframe usually resolves with that timeframe's dominant trend.- Still, a consistent chart framework matters: without a routine "to hang your hat on," you may as well throw darts.**Mark Ritchie II** (Chicago fund manager; his stated view matches Mark Minervini's chapter "A Picture's Worth a Million Dollars"):- "Technicals are not the cause of anything. They are the **effect**." Buyers and sellers move markets — a chart pattern never *causes* a move.- ~95% of charts are noise. His firm **manually screens 300–700 charts a day** — a million-plus over a decade — training the eye to know when something "just doesn't have the look."- Poker: "You can tell a good poker player by the hands they play, but a great one by the ones they fold." Don't let charts invent opportunities.- Terrible chart + good fundamentals = watchlist; monitor the "temperature." He'd **rather pay up once the wind is at his back**: "If it's so great, why is the market saying otherwise right now?" He runs positions on multiple time horizons at once.**Jason** adds emotional discipline: the market can be irrational longer than you can stay liquid. War story: a LinkedIn acquaintance publicly short Tesla since ~$80 split-adjusted — "it went up nine times in your face." Cocksure certainty is "the first thing I look for when I'm looking for people to fade." Minimum fix: wait for one down week before shorting. Distinguish a **vanity short** (Nasdaq/Tesla because the narrative offends you — never) from a legitimate one (Facebook/Google into regulation *only* with a topping structure): "Unless you see actual evidence of price structure, your narrative is wrong. It may be right, but it's not the predominant narrative — and I won't trade things that aren't my narrative."**Raoul Pal**: the chart is a market's *history* in one snapshot, but it never draws him in by itself — the chart's narrative must match his thesis, or he doesn't act.**Pierre Andurand's** (oil) hierarchy, technicals last:1. Global **supply/demand** for this year and the next three; a big surplus or deficit forces a repricing.2. **Positioning** (Commitments of Traders).3. **Reaction to news**: can't rally on bullish news → near an end or a breather; rises on bearish news → bad news priced, add risk.4. **Psychology**: what the market is missing and *when* it will get it — early, but not a year too early.5. **Technicals** — "probably towards the end of the list."

## Chart Workshop: Patterns, Sentiment Indicators, and Behavioral Traps
<!-- from lessons 17, 18 -->### Chart types and timeframes- **Line chart**: daily closes; funds report close-to-close, so it's valid for that purpose.- **High-low-close**: adds the intraday range — e.g., 24 February 2022, the S&P's lowest intraday print of the year, yet it closed far higher.- **Candlesticks**: high/low/close plus direction of travel; bullish, neutral, and bearish formations.- **Timeframe must match horizon**: monthly data needs *years* of context — CPI over 18 months is a diagonal line; over 30–40 years you see how dramatic the 2021–22 spike was.### Support, resistance, inflection pointsGold 2012–13: support at **1520–1530** was tested repeatedly. Roger tried to get clients short on the **third** test and "failed miserably" (gold rallied hard first). The **fourth** test coincided with a macro story (QE) and historically low gold options volatility — a far better setup. The break began a downtrend that bottomed nearly three years later, over 30% lower. Lesson: inflection points fail; a failed setup plus fundamentals and cheap options can set up a better second attempt.### Continuation and reversal patterns- **Pennants/flags/triangles** = consolidation before continuation, differing in the consolidation's angle. Example: a bullish pennant on USD/JPY after the 2012–2016 rally, five years of consolidation to 2021, then the break above the upper trendline (clear only in hindsight). These don't predict — they give entry and stop levels; "the direction should take care of itself."- **Head and shoulders** (reversal): a market fails at new highs as momentum fades — head, two lower shoulders, **neckline**. Trap: after the break, shorts press, then "a vicious rebound back to the neckline" — the big move often starts after that **retest**. Measured target ≈ **head-to-neckline height** (approximate).- **Reverse H&S** bottoms: a 20-year reverse H&S was completing on USD/JPY in early 2022; the head-to-neckline distance implied "well beyond 160" if it played out.- Patterns needn't be perfect: a "wonky" reverse H&S on the US 10-year yield in early 2022 (off-center shoulders, skewed neckline) still broke out to nearly **3%** — roughly its measured target.### False breakoutsA cross of the trigger line that fades back inside the range. Even Brandt concedes the edge from setups has diminished in recent decades; many traders profit on a **50% win rate** if sizing and stops reflect it. The skill is recognizing failure and moving on — "a chart looks great until it doesn't" (Jamie).- **2016, 30-year Treasury**: after 30 years of falling yields, with Brexit, the US election, and China mini-devaluations in the air, the break to new lows sucked in a crowd; smart money faded it, and the break reversed sharply.- **2016, S&P 500**: a two-year H&S top broke down during the profits recession — Roger nearly advised clients to get very short. Then the Fed paused (one hike December 2015, none for 12 months) and China flooded credit; the S&P reclaimed the neckline and "never looked back for another two to three years."- A false breakout is itself a **reversal signal** — the setup was rejected and overwhelmed.### Falling knives, capitulation, volumeCharts constantly display behavioral bias (the course points to Daniel Kahneman). **Anchoring** makes lower prices look "cheap" versus the old high regardless of fundamentals:- **Peloton**: peaked **$170** January 2021, halved within months, faded to the prior ~**$80** low into earnings, rallied to ~**$100** ahead of the print — then a bad report gapped it below $80. RSI far below 30, and it still lost nearly **two-thirds** more. "Some of the biggest sell-offs occur from oversold conditions."- **Capitulation shows on volume**: Bitcoin ground lower while RSI sat oversold, until a cathartic flush on very heavy volume marked the low — then rallied. Volume also confirms breakouts.### Sentiment indicators- **RSI**: 0–100, lines at **30/70**. **Divergence**: higher low in RSI with lower low in price = bullish; lower high with higher high = bearish. Warning: oscillators work best in ranges; trends stay overbought/oversold for long stretches, and you'll sometimes fade the range just as it breaks — that's what risk management is for. Bollinger Bands serve the same purpose.- **Breadth**: % of S&P stocks above their 200-day average. You want it *high* — low readings mean narrow leadership (dot-com; FAANG-led Nasdaq). Below **50%** bearish; at ~**20**, maybe **10**, contrarian *bullish* — "there is only one direction from there."- **Rate of change**: a PMI can sit in the mid-30s in recession and take months back above 50, but the rate of change turns positive the moment the economy goes from super-negative to somewhat-negative — the slowdown is slowing.- **Citi Economic Surprise Index (CESI)**: realized data vs estimates. Below zero = data weakening *or* consensus too optimistic; above zero = beating estimates *or* analysts too pessimistic.- **Positioning**: crowded trades leave no marginal buyers. Early 2021: every FX strategist was short dollar/long euro — and CFTC euro futures longs sat at **record highs** (tiny market versus OTC FX, but indicative). Be skeptical of a macro call everyone already owns.### Overlays and ratio charts- **Overlays** expose hidden sameness: gold vs real yields (inverted scale, near-identical); EM equities, AUD, and copper trace the same pattern — three "diversified" holdings that are one trade.- **Ratios** show relative performance (rising = first outperforming). Classic: dollar index vs S&P/EM — falling dollar, EM outperforms; held for 20 years (breaks on short windows). Strong dollar is generally bad for commodities too.- **Industrials/staples vs ISM**: rotate to industrials as ISM rises, staples as it falls (especially below 50). Variants — discretionary/staples, financials/utilities — are plays on growth expectations and yields.- Method: zoom out for the secular trend, then the cycle, then recent action; find historical patterns; overlay related assets.

## Commodities and Currencies: Drivers and Relationships

## Bonds: Pricing Growth and Inflation
<!-- from lessons 20, 25 -->Bond traders price two things: **expected growth** and **expected inflation**.### The risk-free rate anchors everythingGovernment yields are the risk-free rate; everything else prices off them plus a risk premium. US mortgages (~20-year space) track long treasuries plus premium; car loans (5–7 years) track the **"belly"** of the curve; short-dated bank borrowing tracks Fed funds; credit-card rates are "a million miles" from policy. Know where any loan sits on the curve.### Gauges for growth- The **ISM** leads GDP — inflections preceded the recoveries after 2008 and 2020 and the slowdowns of 2012 and 2016. Yields track the ISM far better than GDP, because **GDP is lagging**. Note the "chart of truth": yields trended down for decades while the ISM oscillated around 50.- The **Atlanta Fed's GDPNow** aggregates 13 subcomponents into a real-time GDP forecast — reliable, but public, so no edge; use it as a consensus marker against which a contrarian view becomes a trade.- Many argue causality runs the other way — the **bond market is the better growth predictor** — hence the **yield curve** as the key business-cycle variable.### Inflation, breakevens, TIPS- **Nominal yield = real yield + breakeven.** April 2022: the 10-year approached 3%, but real yields stayed very low because breakevens had surged.- The **Cleveland Fed** runs a well-calibrated inflation tracker. The market gauge is the **5-year breakeven**: below **0.3%** in the 2020 recession, above **3%** by late 2021 after stimulus; nominal yields followed on a hawkish-Fed repricing.- The **TIPS/nominal-treasury ratio** is essentially the same chart. Unless you own inflation protection, **inflation is the enemy of bonds** — investors bought zero- and negative-yielding 10- and 30-year bonds just years before inflation surged.- **Term premium** (Adrian-Crump-Moench/ACM — the short- vs long-yield gap) was suppressed for years, which is how yields could sit at 2–2.5% in early 2022 while inflation arguments argued 4–5%.### Central banks, credit, limits of the last 20 years- **QE paradox**: central banks were the biggest flows, yet when they *bought*, yields often *rose* (and fell when they sold). Across the QE era yields still declined secularly. The **2013 taper tantrum** showed how violently markets react when the purchases themselves are threatened.- The dominant players are the **Fed, ECB, BoJ**, and mega pensions (Japan's GPIF) — not hedge funds. Their intervention created the **reach for yield**: falling risk-free returns pushed investors into riskier credit.- **Credit spreads** track conditions and policy: recessions widen, recovery compresses. The Eurozone crisis re-rated "risk-free" — quality **AAA corporates are often considered safer than many governments**.- **Junk = BB or lower**, "highly speculative" per Moody's/S&P — the key word *speculative*: owning it is a **high-beta play on the bond market** (growth, inflation, credit, policy).- Post-2022 warning: don't apply 20 years of inflation moderation to a possibly structurally higher regime. The bond-equity hedge decayed — near zero, bonds had little room to rally when equities fell, and portfolios that rose together began **unwinding together**; by the time the trend break is visible, the best of the move may be gone.

## Equities: Sectors, Style Factors, and the Business Cycle
<!-- from lessons 22, 23, 24 -->### Regimes and catalysts beat valuation (over your horizon)Value investors seek intrinsic worth; thematic investors accept that what matters changes. **Stan Druckenmiller to Seth Klarman (2021)**: "You're a value investor. I'm not. I don't know what value means... My North Star is every event in the world affects some security. I try to imagine the world as it is today and see if there's some kind of seismic change going on and how the world might look in 18 months — and if it does, what are the securities that would be priced very differently? A lot of investors live in the present, which is a disaster long term." **Eric Basmajian** explained his whole equity approach without once mentioning valuation. Reconciling principle: **the longer your horizon, the more relevant fundamental value becomes** — but you need time to capture it.**Scott Bessent** (Soros/Druckenmiller colleague): "**50% of a stock's move is the overall market, 30% is the industry group, and maybe 20% is the extra alpha from stock picking.**"**Chris Cole**: what matters is not the asset class but **how it performs in the market regime** — will your assets keep protecting against the regime events you fear? Threats can be structural, flow-based, or regulatory (a tax change could make real estate the better way to play secular growth). He broadened "gold" to "**fiat alternatives**" — open to crypto replacing it, but with 2,000+ years of history, gold remains the standard-bearer in his Dragon Portfolio.Raoul's longest-horizon tools: the **50–100-year charts** (copper, all major equity markets, sectors, currencies, bond indices; nominal, inflation-adjusted, and ratio views) for "variant observations." Gold looked historically **cheap vs equities** — a long-term bias toward gold in certain environments. The US banking sector *in euros* looks like the European banking sector — a currency difference masquerading as a sector difference.### The cycle playbook for sectorsSectors split **cyclical vs defensive** (utilities are needed in recessions; luxury goods aren't). Phase-to-expression:- **Growth**: value and cyclicals — basic materials, industrials, financials.- **Peak**: energy tends to do well.- **Accelerating inflation**: real estate, staples, healthcare.- **Slowdown**: defensive growth.- **Recession**: utilities — one of the few long hiding places — or short financials/industrials/discretionary.Beta maps to the cycle: **low beta** = staples, utilities, quality; **high beta** = small caps, cyclicals, EM. Hardest part is knowing the phase — and keep checking the sector/stock *behaves* as the playbook says: "they don't always conform." Cycle length is unstable (2–3 to 8–9 years) depending on the secular regime (monetary era 1982–2020/22 possibly giving way to a fiscal one with shorter cycles); yield curves give clues but **no precise timing** — keep re-testing the thesis.### Style factors trump sectors (Darius)In a growth slowdown, **Darius** wants healthcare, staples, utilities, REITs, plus mega-cap secular growers — "the high-quality compounders, as they call them in Boston" — and *not* financials, materials, industrials, energy. He tracks **50 US equity sectors and style factors on month-on-month Sharpe ratios** as a real-time proxy for pod-shop flows (Citadel, Millennium, Balyasny, "the .72s"), whose fundamental bets plus systematic hedging dominate turnover. His three pivots: **low beta over high beta, mega-cap over small-cap, growth over value** — "make those three pivots and you don't need sector-level work."### A balanced-framework tilt (Basmajian)Start from an **all-weather base** — long bonds, intermediate bonds, stocks, gold, commodities — tilt toward the long-term trend (his bias: treasuries/growth-slowing), then overlay **short-term indicators**. Nominal growth rising → equities best, commodities second; within equities, cyclicals, small caps, industrials. On that overlay he had tilted to commodities and small caps. His bank trade: **short US regionals since 2018**, pared to minimum because an upturn steepens the curve and helps banks — but the secular trend of rock-bottom rates fights them; regionals are pure spread plays he expects to "go the way of Japan and Europe." Energy: he dislikes the *sector* — the fracker model (heavy capital into a depleting well, forever, financed by debt/equity issuance) — preferring **the commodity itself**. "Value" also splits: defensive value vs cyclical value behave very differently.### Long-short expressions- **Intra-sector** (Jamie's insurance pairs): isolates company risk; hard — try comparing two miners' accounts.- **Inter-sector via ETFs**: 2021's long energy (XLE) / short consumer discretionary (XLY) — energy-led inflation squeezing the consumer as the Fed began hiking.- **2007**: long miners / short UK mortgage banks — macro thesis plus some micro work on the short side.### When relationships break: 2021–22 divergencesRoger's anomalies heading into 2022 — each a sign "something is different":- **US banks/S&P ratio vs the 10-year yield**: normally banks outperform as yields rise; instead "yawning alligator jaws" — ratio falling while yields surged, after a decade of lockstep.- **Commodity index vs inverted dollar**: normally inverses; both rose together.- **Fastest hike-to-inversion ever**: first Fed hike to curve inversion = **zero months**, vs a normal 30–60 and a prior record of **one month (1980** — also high inflation). Most pattern-matching uses 1990–2022 — the "period of moderation" — which may not apply.- Also: US leading indicators lead global growth (manufacturing is globally correlated), and — counterintuitively — **stronger US growth tends to weaken the dollar**, boosting international assets; Basmajian raised international exposure (VXUS) from summer 2020 on that logic.- Sector "recipes": energy runs on oil and gas, miners on base metals, financials on interest rates — a value chain for expressing one idea across asset classes.

## The Interconnected Market: Correlations, Volatility Regimes, Rate of Change
<!-- from lessons 21, 23 -->### Correlation to oneCross-asset correlations go **to one** in crises — everything falls together. Uncorrelated assets stop being uncorrelated: gold sold off in 2008 ("I just need cash" overrides everything), and Bitcoin traded with the Nasdaq once institutions owned it. Design for this.### Volatility regimes and leverage lessons- **Volatility is never constant** — equity vol can be 80 or 8, and where it sits reflects the cycle.- Stock betas: ~0.5–1.5 typical; Tesla ~3; "basket cases" 4–5. A $10M position in a 2-beta stock is really $4M of exposure.- The dirty secret of long-short: a fund making 2% in a year the S&P made 15% could **lever 6x** and call it 12%. Repo desks harvest basis points between near-identical assets at hundreds of times leverage. **LTCM** was the archetype — relative value levered enormously, liquidity risk forgotten; when the Asian crisis rolled into the Russian crisis, positions blew out.- **EUR/CHF, January 2015**: flatlined so long its measured volatility was near zero — then the SNB abandoned the floor and it fell 20% in a day, among the largest major-pair moves ever. Backward-looking volatility is not forward-looking safety.### Rate of change, not levels- **What matters is where you got levered**: mortgages 3→4.5% (a 50% increase) hurt far more than 10→11.5% (15%). After two years of zero rates, 2% can be devastating. Think in **relative moves**.- Gold tracks not the level of real rates so much as **their momentum** (absent safe-haven flights).- Of every correlated series ask: "What's changing here? Is it accelerating? Is it bending?"### Early warnings and causalityBig macro data are often **coincident**; micro signals — bank lending, credit figures, relative sector performance — can be the **early warning**. Cross-asset volatility gives checks: bond vol spiking while equity vol collapses — one is wrong (usually assumed bonds; not always); a safe-haven yen selling off in risk-off tells you something. "This whole thing is joining the dots." And *why* a move happens matters: rising rates from overheating are mixed-to-negative for equities; rising rates pricing healthy growth are positive. Gold/dollar "in a recession" — depends. On FX hedges: **CAD/JPY** (oil up helps Canada, hurts Japan) and **AUD/JPY** serve as equity proxies; Roger likes buying **FX volatility when it's breaking out but not yet crazy high**. **Julian** (trading dollar-yen since 1996) warns against betting on correlation *breaks*: August 2015's China devaluation should have sent USD/JPY down (risk-off), but it rallied — CNH was collapsing and everyone bought dollars — "and it wasn't persistent." If you have a sector view, play the sector; timing a correlation break is brutally hard.

## Digital Assets: An Evolving Asset Class
<!-- from lessons 21, 22, 23, 25 -->### The secular adoption storyCrypto behaves like a **secular adoption trend with cyclical oscillations** (regulation, sentiment, leverage). Adoption was under **10% of the global population** at recording; the internet-in-the-1990s and mobile-phone analogies are the field's favorites. **Jurrien Timmer's** chart (Fidelity's director of global macro, "dispassionate") maps **Bitcoin addresses with a balance over $1** onto mobile-phone adoption: 13 years in, tracking the S-curve "remarkably well," right where it lifts off. Market cap grows, **Bitcoin dominance declines** (healthy), **DeFi total value locked (TVL)** is the ecosystem's AUM equivalent.**Chris Tyrer** parallels commodities circa **1998**, when they first became institutionally investable. The commodity thesis rested on three pillars, all present in crypto: **diversification** (long-horizon correlation low, though "everything is correlated in a risk event"); **positive expected return** (commodities had roll yield; crypto has **proof-of-stake yield**); **bullish fundamentals** ("if you believe in network adoption, it gives you a positive expected return that's quite ridiculous").### From uncorrelated to correlatedBitcoin trades tightly with the **Nasdaq 100 since 2020** and tracks the **high-beta/low-vol stock ratio** — **liquidity is a major determinant of price**, as with traditional risk assets. **Anthony Pompliano** dates the shift to 2020 — "season three": (1) **career risk removed** once Paul Tudor Jones and Druckenmiller allocated publicly; (2) the **holder base changed** — macro-aware players who sell when they hear "interest rates." Upside: drawdowns temper — a legacy **-85%** bear became **-50%**. Catch: the more successful the adoption, the more crypto resembles a normal risk asset across the cycle. The Fed drives everything — "the S&P 500, interest rates, housing, and yes, increasingly digital assets."### Valuation and expressionNo settled valuation framework exists — it may take years (equity analysis took a century). Practical approaches: **relative valuation** (Bitcoin vs gold's roughly $10 trillion ballpark) and **portfolio construction** over all-in bets — e.g., 5% gold plus 5% crypto as diversifiers that would outperform in war or horrific inflation. One presenter's best-ever personal investment in the space was *not* a directional coin bet. If the volatility doesn't suit you, the sizing lever lets you hold 1x instead of 5x — "at least you're in the game."### A taxonomy for tokens (Jeff Dorman, CIO of Arca)Map tokens the way fixed income maps bonds — by **issuer** (governments/CBDCs, DAOs, individuals, corporations like Binance or FTX), by **decentralization spectrum**, by **token type** (currency; asset-backed — security tokens backed by real estate, or Nexus Mutual backed by its capital pool; **pass-through** — quasi-equity/quasi-membership), and by **features** (inflation/amortization schedule; dividend or buyback). Then "value investing is really easy — look for tokens with certain characteristics." Analogy: a token is Amazon shares and Amazon Prime combined — membership perks plus financial upside. **BNB** is canonical: fixed supply, customer discounts, profit-linked buybacks.

## Trade Construction: Expression, Entry, Sizing, Running Positions
<!-- from lessons 26, 27, 29 -->This is where skilled investors spend most of their time: "most people focus on the *what*, when the *when* and the *how much* are usually more important." **Kyle Bass's** first question to **John Burbank** (2015) was how he sized positions; **Mark Ritchie II** rarely discusses trade ideas — only position sizing. "I was right overall, but I was wrong on timing is another way of saying I lost money." Trading means **buying risk you believe is priced too cheap and selling risk priced too expensive** — every asset carries risk, even cash and government bonds.### Golden rules and knowing yourself- Size so you can scale in or cut a small loss. Only invest what you can afford to lose. **Leverage is the silent killer.** Diversify — think holistically about job, home, income, family. Understand liquidity. Know your risk types: market, sector, stock, plus liquidity, counterparty, political. Be honest: "Do I need to have this trade on?"- Your home can be your safe asset — it has *utility* even if it falls 10%; you still live in it.- **Buckets of wealth**: job, home, savings. Don't double-count — a tech employee with company options shouldn't also overload on Nasdaq-like equity (Brent Johnson); a UBS equity-sales salary was itself equity-correlated. Diversify investments *against* your life.- Know how many positions you can manage — Raoul: ~**10 concentrated**; a 50-stock portfolio, "I'd be absolutely lost." Never hold what you don't understand well enough to survive a drawdown (his crypto example) — or hold it at 1x instead of 5x, so even zero is survivable.- **Fastest learning curve: doing it, at a size you're comfortable with.** "Trading one future can give you more experience than three years of university finance." Start with an amount you don't expect to make money on; Ritchie II: open small, watch how it trades, learn the drivers.- **Patience**: Roger's cricket coach **Phillips Gray** — "Good batsmen know which balls to play, but great batsmen know which balls to leave." Private investors don't have to be invested; waiting is a position.### Expression: many ways to say the same thingChoose the instrument deliberately, including **cost of carry** (shorting bonds = borrow cost + coupon + margin; is there a cheaper expression?). Bullish gold can be metal, miner ETF, junior-miner ETF, a single miner (same driver, different risk bundles), or a **call** — limited risk, embedded leverage, unlimited upside. Roger's 2012–13 gold trade worked because the macro setup (Fed withdrawing liquidity) met cheap options (historic-low vol) — shorting gold or gold stocks would have needed more capital for the same exposure.Two canonical stories:- **The eurodollar trade (early 2000s, as Raoul tells it)**: thesis — the dot-com bust and a Fed cutting rates for three years. Instead of shorting equities, whose 20–40% bear rallies from 2000–2003 ripped stops, ride **eurodollar futures**, which simply priced lower rates the whole way down. Same macro view, far more survivable expression.- **Japan 2012**: long equities, short yen, or bonds? It turned out to be something between the yen and the equity trade — and the equity leg was probably better. Different expressions of one idea have different kickers; pick consciously.- Hedges can live inside the portfolio: a reflation book plus a **long-dollar hedge** (the milkshake worst case — a dollar that rips destroys every risk asset) — "if it goes wrong, one will counteract the other." But don't over-hedge into a book that "never went green or red — you've become the market."### Time horizon: idea horizon = trade horizon**Paul Tudor Jones, quoted by Raoul**: "The best investors I've ever seen and the best traders have a time horizon that matches their idea horizon." A business-cycle view = **6–18 months**; a sentiment trade = a week — "don't trade a central-bank bubble and these big narratives and then trade a two-week time view." Horizon is also how two people with opposite positions can both be right — and it must fit your life: a 30-year accumulator absorbs a 40% drawdown; two years from retirement cannot.### The risk spectrum (Kupperman's oil trade)**Harris Kupperman** ranks commodity expressions from most to least risky: **junior exploration stock** (no revenue, "hopes and dreams") → **producing company** (geology, operating economics, political risk) → **large multi-mine company** → **the commodity itself** → an **options structure**: sell puts, buy a call spread — on for roughly zero capital out, or a **net credit**, with no permanent capital at risk. "People go brute force instead of finessing how to approach a thematic idea." His case study: long-term bullish oil, no view on next week; he avoided company risk (resource managements "tend to overpay for assets because they want a bigger kingdom") and bought **December 2025 oil at $52–53** (mid-$40s a year earlier; outright oil went negative once): "risking about five bucks to make a few hundred." Plus a **December 2023 $90×$100 call spread for $0.65** — at $101 oil, **15x**. "I don't know that many junior stocks that are 15-baggers." Of any idea ask: **is this the best expression?** For economic news it is "almost in all circumstances" the bond market — until the move is late and options take over.### Entries: start small, scale in- Jamie: **put half the intended position on first**. Any price is roughly 50/50 (crane-pointing and pet-picking stories are confirmation bias — "something to hang your hat on"); half-sizing tilts the odds your way. Up: take some off. Down: you have ammo.- **Druckenmiller (2018 Real Vision interview)**: open with **one-third of maximum size**, feel the market, **press on a technical break**. His 2014 EUR/USD short after the ECB went negative: opened at **1.39**, pressed on the break of the head-and-shoulders top at **1.35**. Incremental entry echoed by Pierre Andurand, Mark Ritchie II, and most guests.- Take a **tester position** almost too small to matter, just for a pulse — "the best traders I know tend to be incremental; they don't make all-in limit-long/limit-short decisions in one day."- Know the **range before entry**: "Stock at 30; I think it's worth 40–45; if it gets to 25, ceteris paribus, I'm probably wrong — that's the stop. I've only lost five."- **Write the plan down**: reasons, targets, horizon; if it changes, be honest about why. Cautionary tale (told by "James" in the workshop): on the Fed's March 2020 stimulus he bought **Bitcoin around $3,800–4,000**, then ejected days later when a 50% snap felt "too good to be true" — a sizing failure, not a thesis change. A year later Bitcoin was above **$60,000**. Hindsight alternatives: keep only the profit in (cut size two-thirds, stay in the trade), or rotate to gold (which he did — same QE theme, different expression — "but the far better trade is just to remain long Bitcoin").- Roger's client anecdote: a hedge fund manager printed a chart and asked him to **draw the entry and exit lines** — fix levels *in advance*; in-trade it's too easy to lose your way.### Sizing long-horizon trades: Gurevich's dollar-yen**Alex Gurevich**: best horizon **2–5 years** — "it requires me to be right almost every time, but I find the long-horizon trades are easier to be right on. The hard thing is sizing them small." Career-best trade: **dollar-yen from 2010**, entered at 82–83 on Japan's unsustainable debt/deflation (he didn't know the *mechanism* — the DPJ's fall, Abenomics). Two years of "torture" as the yen strengthened to 76–75; small position, adding and trimming a little. When it turned he **pyramided** — being in from 78 makes adding at 90 feel natural: "you've taken the pain, so you're comfortable with the pain... macro trading is all about pain and knowing how to take it." Looking for a 30% move, "there is plenty of time to add." Position **five times bigger at 105 than at 78**, average ~90 — "nice to make the money from 90 to 118." Other career bests: dollar-euro and long US bonds. Multi-year ideas don't need early size; the market gives many chances to pyramid.### Running winners, cutting losers- **Ritchie II**: people handle losses (they hurt, so you act) but are "really, really bad at running our winners." Windfall fix: if a 20–30% trade with a 5–10% stop moves 30% quickly, **take off half to three-quarters, move the stop to breakeven** — "I've now paid for that investment in full; I'm in the trade for free." Letting a *winning* trade become an investment is how you ride a bigger theme. The reverse is fatal: **never let a losing trade turn into an investment**.- Standing discipline: "I have my target and I take my profits" — any consistent rule (e.g., partial profits at +20%) beats cleverness. Roger's regret — his **Japan trade**: out at +50% ("that's got to be enough") before half the move; he should have run the macro thesis.- **Pierre Andurand, May 5, 2011**: much of a bad year's loss came in one day — the bin Laden raid day — when silver dropped over $10 on no fundamental news while he carried one of his largest positions. When a market moves in a way you don't understand, "you feel like maybe somebody knows something I don't" — he cut hard; the market recovered days later ("all that for nothing"). Lesson: **don't be too big precisely when the market is already going your way**. That year every position went against him for a month before working — no fundamental misreads, just size too big for chop. Macro traders struggle in chop; the answers are survival basics: stay alive, don't lose much, trust the good days return. "I'd rather grab the easy 20% in the meat of the move."- **Volatility cuts both ways**: blessing for the tactical trader, nightmare for the trend-capturer — the latter should *reduce* size when vol rises; that preserves the ability to live another day.

## Portfolio Construction and Risk Management
<!-- from lessons 28, 30, 31 -->"The first rule of investing is still to be around to trade again next year." Risk management balances enough exposure to hit return objectives against not blowing up — and it depends on **psyche and lifestyle**: near retirement, no gung-ho directional leverage; in your late 20s without dependents, time and earnings power justify more risk. Approach follows income, outgoings, ability to recover — and time, the compounding ingredient (the "eighth wonder" line attributed to Einstein appears misattributed). Style must fit logistics: 50 positions demand real hours; day traders like **Dave Floyd** and **Peter Brandt** are "bolted to the chair."### Exposure accounting: GMV, NMV, beta- **Gross market value (GMV)**: longs plus shorts — $100M long/$100M short on a $200M book; on $50,000 of capital, $100,000 gross = **2x leverage**.- **Net market value (NMV)**: longs minus shorts — directional exposure. Jamie's desk beta-adjusted everything (HSBC <1; recap banks very high; $2M in a 2-beta stock = $4M exposure), paired matched longs and shorts, and ran a small **net** — e.g., $10M net on $200M GMV = **5% net long**; a 1% market move moves the book ~5bps. At zero net, all return is stock-picking alpha. Three risk layers: market, sector, company — strip the first two, get paid for the third.- When volatility rises, cut **both** gross and net: each position's dollar-swings have grown.### Cash as a position; buying the forced seller- The cheapest way for retail to be short is to **be in cash and wait**. Cash is a position — it buys **optionality** when everyone else is forced to act.- When correlations go to one, good assets fall with bad: "the market takes no prisoners — that's when you can buy really good companies at reasonable prices." **"The best time to buy something is not when someone's selling it — it's when they *have to* sell it"** (the forced, price-insensitive seller; the south-of-France villa). Crisis is when great assets get cheap.- Hold cash so *you're* never the forced seller; fully-invested books needing cash crystallize losses at the worst moment.### Liquidity risk: markets close, margins spike- "Liquidity is always there on the way up; it's never there when you need it." Exchanges close: shorts of Russian stocks in 2022 were right — and couldn't buy back. **LME nickel**: ~$15,000 to over $100,000, trades cancelled, a large Chinese short behind it. Only Bitcoin trades truly 24/7; most currencies gap over weekends.- Options are exchange-listed — **limit-down** means you can't get out, potentially repeatedly toward zero.- **Counterparty risk**: OTC options (FX) in 2008 faced counterparties that disappeared.- **Margin**: initial (deposit) + variation (daily losses) — and in a vol spike **initial margin can triple overnight**, forcing closures.- Fund structure: quarterly redemption windows mean two bad quarters trigger withdrawals, forcing loss-crystallization in illiquid books.- **Bid-offer**: a "$1 a trade" platform with a 10% spread costs 10% on a round trip; platforms can halt (Robinhood, GameStop/AMC). Rare, but stress-test for "high downside correlation plus a couple of closed exchanges."### Leverage: the silent killer- Two levers: **borrowing** (10x) versus **optionality** (a mountain of out-of-the-money puts = huge notional, only premium at risk). Know which you're using.- **Personal leverage counts**: job + company shares + mortgage = "very highly levered," undiversified; add startup options and it's worse. Job + mortgage + portfolio ≈ **three times levered to the economy**.- **LTCM**: founded by Nobel laureates Myron Scholes and Robert Merton (a caution that "there can be a vast gulf between the theory and the reality"), 21–43% annual returns in its first years, then **lost over $4 billion in as many months** on the Asian and Russian crises; a fund of a few hundred million carrying billions in positions nearly took the system down and required central-bank rescue. **Archegos/Bill Hwang**: ~5x leverage turned $200 million into $20 billion — all lost in 2021.- **Munger's** three L's — "liquor, leverage and ladies" — leverage atop illiquidity is the real killer. Arithmetic of ruin: **down 50% requires +100%** — "not without taking excessive risks."- **Jimmy Haber**: hit singles — "the biggest chance you have of being a success is just still being at the crease." Many funds took the big bets, had great years, blew up; their chances were gone.### Diversification that isn't fake- **Concentration is the enemy** — positions can be "the same trade wearing a different pair of shoes." Diversify counterparties too (one bank or broker?).- **Pairing inversely correlated bets** — bonds+gold, dollar+gold — makes a portfolio robust enough to run more risk: if the Fed floods liquidity and short-banks loses, long bonds and gold win.- The rookie error is **over-trading**: "every idea is a good idea... short the banks, short Tesla, short oil, long gold — before you know it you've got a million bets. You can't follow them. You have no portfolio — just somebody taking too many bets to express a simple view." Nearly everyone over-trades at first.- Interrogate every position: *Should I be doing this? Why?* ("The chart looks good" isn't good enough.) *Does it fit the portfolio? Is it the best expression? Is this view already covered?* If a better expression appears, switch — **always be improving**.- Raoul's two standing questions: **"Where are we making money?"** (nowhere = big problem) and **"Where are we losing money?"** — if you're not losing *somewhere*, **you're not truly diversified** (egg-in-one-basket works for some, at higher risk; his job is also to protect). Judge the **top line**, not each line.- **Illiquidity is "an accident waiting to happen."** Allow it only with a defined catalyst, cap it (~5%), never let it drive the portfolio. The triad — **illiquidity, leverage, concentration** — kills: "I'm fine using them, but not at the same time and not uniformly; those are situational."- **Brent Johnson's** Silicon Valley field notes: founders with 99% of net worth in one stock, refusing at every level ("I'll wait for the next earnings call... I can't sell now, I'm 20% below"). What works: "Just sell 20–30%. Buy your house, pay it off so you can always live in it." Be honest about drawdown tolerance — checking prices six times a day means trim earlier; embracing volatility means wait longer.### Doing nothing is a position"Good traders and investors know what to go for. Great ones know what to avoid." Tennis forces you to return the ball; the market serves and volleys — **and you may simply not show up**. Not playing is a decision — "a very valuable option" requiring experience, discipline, "anti-psychology." One guest (Dan) has "massively downgraded conviction... almost to the point where I don't think it belongs in an investment process": no holy wars, no conviction bets, only **easy bets**. Special caution on shorting: "It's not good for the human brain to believe there is a conspiracy against you — it's really not good if there actually is one." (An allocator asked a friend raising a short-only fund about "the risk that basically all short sellers inevitably go insane.") The goal is a process that never needs heroic bets — no "200% off the bottom going all in" — because the game is **50–70 years; compounding time matters more than any single year. Never risk a blow-up.** Alex Gurevich: don't overplay your hand — opportunities come around, especially for cyclical and secular traders.### Stops vs drawdown control (Gurevich)Different tools, regularly conflated:- A **stop** is per-bet: decide the maximum loss up front; if hit, out — re-entering later is a *new, independent* bet (buy gold on a Russia-invades-Ukraine thesis, exit at -$50/oz; a later re-entry is fresh). What matters isn't losing money — it's **being wrong**. Set **stop and size simultaneously**: "I am going to lose as much money as I'm willing to lose on this trade at the time I've lost faith in it." Never still-believing-but-too-far-down, or disbelieving-but-holding-powder — either wastes capital.- **Drawdown control** is fund-level: lose 5% in a month → cut risk — because 10% brings ISDA cancellations and redemptions. You'll put the risk back on later in the same fund, so be deliberate about taking it down and putting it back on.### Recovering from a drawdownThe re-entry protocol (Pierre, Sagar): restart with **small, defined-risk positions** — e.g., OTM options risking **2% of AUM over six months**, plus a little futures; increase only as P&L improves, letting position grow with it. Take an hour or a day off after bad days (never overtrade); sometimes stop for **weeks or months**, run only what's working, and "prove it out" day by day, month by month, before scaling risk up. **Druckenmiller**: "You need to **earn the right** to start playing big again — use small bets to reestablish the rhythm." Stops help here too: they tell you exactly the most you can lose — priceless in the rough periods everyone gets.### Volatility-adjusted sizing (ATR)You never feel big enough when a trade works, always too big when it doesn't; too-small breeds boredom, too-big breeds sleepless nights — both break your horizon. Fix: **size by volatility**. Use the **Average True Range (ATR)**, lookback **14 days/periods**; ATR ÷ price = volatility in percent. Risking a base **2% of capital** per position, scale by relative volatility: Bitcoin's ATR% four times the euro's → Bitcoin gets **0.5%** (2% ÷ 4), so each position moves the book comparably. Equity-only: compare a stock's ATR to the index's.### Spreads and hedgesExpression itself can hedge: sector **vs index** (hedges market risk); sector **vs sector**; stock **vs stock, same sector** (intra-sector — hedges market and sector, leaves two idiosyncratic risks). Beta-check: if longs' average beta is twice the shorts', a 1% drop costs 2% on longs, earns 1% on shorts — **not hedged**; cut longs half or double shorts. Often the best hedge in a new volatility regime is simply **cutting GMV** or removing the position: "when in doubt, always go for simplicity."### Closing checklistUnderstand your financial/lifestyle situation; set goals, match investing to them. Many ways to express an idea — pick the one for your risk tolerance. Align idea horizon and trade horizon. Size appropriately (room to add; consider a stop at initiation). Before every trade: *why do I need this position on?* Manage sizing throughout as a lever. Diversify and simplify. Respect leverage and total exposure. Don't be afraid not to trade. Execution is where the money is made — "a well-executed okay idea in a robust portfolio will drive consistent returns."
