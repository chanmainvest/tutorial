# Investing with the Business Cycle — Course Synthesis

**Real Vision Academy** — recorded live in New York (March 2023) and released as a three-part series plus three follow-up AMAs (July–August 2023). Course host: **George Staneov** (leads Real Vision's Academy and memberships teams).

**Instructors:**

- **Raoul Pal** — CEO and co-founder of Real Vision; author of *Global Macro Investor* (GMI) since ~2004. Started his career in 1990, worked on the Goldman Sachs derivatives desk, then ran his own hedge fund research business at GLG, where he was one of the first people to build investing models around the business cycle. His first GMI piece, 19 years before this course, was titled "Debt, Demographics and Deflation." He has been in crypto since 2013.
- **Andreas Steno Larsen** — founder of Steno Research (Copenhagen); former chief economist/strategist for various investment banks. He spends most of his professional time predicting the business cycle, with a special focus on dollar liquidity.
- **Julien Bittel** — head of macro research at GMI, working alongside Raoul Pal. Roughly a decade in financial markets: analyst, then multi-asset investment strategist, then manager of several multi-asset portfolios at Pictet Asset Management (Raoul: "an asset allocator at Pictet running a $5 billion fund") before joining GMI.

**What the course covers:** A complete macro framework for positioning investment portfolios around the business cycle. Part 1 (Raoul) establishes the secular backdrop — population, productivity and debt — and shows that the business cycle drives all asset prices. Part 2 (Andreas) reduces cycle-forecasting to three variables (liquidity, growth, inflation) and names the specific indicators used to track each. Part 3 (Julien) converts the framework into a concrete asset-allocation playbook: four growth quadrants, four macro "seasons," a season-by-season asset table, and a five-pillar market checklist. Three AMA sessions then answer audience questions with live portfolio decisions. The stated ambition (George): a book called "The Science and Art of Asset Allocation" — the science is in these sessions; applying it to your own circumstances is the art (the course repeatedly uses the analogy of Peter Attia's *Outlive* for health: principles, not personal rules; the stakes are too high to delegate).

## Part 1 — The Framework: Secular Trends and the Business Cycle (Raoul Pal)

### 1.1 The magic formula of GDP

Raoul's foundation: at the top structural level, **GDP growth = population growth + productivity growth + debt growth**. Nothing else matters long term.

- **Population** — more people, more activity. Shrinking populations and throttled immigration mean shrinking trend GDP.
- **Productivity** — output per person, which is really "productive output per unit of energy input." Technology is the tool that raises it. Raoul notes the inflation-adjusted price of a barrel of oil has been roughly **$40 for 60 years** — a fixed input; cutting it to $10 would raise productivity roughly **3x**. This is why he treats the green-energy transition as an economic inevitability ("follow the money, not the politics").
- **Debt growth** — the catch-all: "if we don't have population or productivity, we borrow and pretend to be rich." Debt drives growth for periods "until it doesn't," and interest payments then become a growing drag on the economy.

Chart described from narration: the **10-year-on-10-year rate of change of the US population** — "classically top-left, bottom-right." From this one chart alone Raoul says you can declare the US trend growth rate is falling. US births have "basically gone to zero"; the trend rate of US GDP growth is now about **1.75%** and falling. Output per worker looks stable on a chart but is in fact gently downward-trending — despite a technological revolution — because retirees dilute productivity.

**Japan** is the extreme worked example: a population that ages Europe by ~10 years and the US by ~15; working-age cohort and total population both shrinking; yet GDP held flat because **debt growth — i.e., Bank of Japan balance-sheet monetization of government debt — replaced organic growth**. Raoul argues China is about to become an even more extreme case (negative population growth already).

### 1.2 Secular drivers: debt, demographics, productivity — deflation as the outcome

Raoul's career framework started as "debt, demographics, deflation"; he now phrases it as **debt + demographics + productivity being the drivers, and deflation being the outcome**. Structural logic: aging, indebted societies produce falling trend GDP, and "if GDP falls over time, it's almost impossible to generate long-term inflation." Inflation is a **demand** phenomenon, not a supply one: supply shocks get solved by technology or new supply; demand shocks drive sustained inflation. The 1970s were inflationary because an enormous baby-boom cohort bought houses, cars and everything else simultaneously — a demand shock (comparable to China's 2000s commodity demand shock). The 2021–22 inflation spike was a short-term demand shock (reopening) plus a supply shock (reopening logistics, Russia) — "not repeatable" without sustained above-trend GDP growth.

Secular conclusions Raoul says the formula produces:

- **Structural bond bull** — falling trend growth plus secular deflation; "the trend of my career."
- **Structural dollar bull** — the world borrows in dollars; in slowdowns, debts must be serviced in dollars, producing structural dollar shortage dynamics (eurodollar).
- **Pension crisis** — an aging, indebted West cannot fund promised retirement expectations; boomers must eventually sell assets "to whom, and at what price?" Raoul ties this to political polarization: most people got left behind by the demographic math.
- **Crypto** — after 2008/2012/2023 banking stress, "nobody owns anything" (rehypothecation, deposit debasement, reserve freezes); crypto is the self-custodied, programmatically scarce bearer-asset answer. "God's gift to debasement."
- **Technology** — solves the productivity and demographics deficits; the one sector that outgrows currency debasement (see 1.7).
- **The "Monsoon" thesis** — in 2015 Raoul literally took a world map, crossed out every country with bad demographics or structurally large debts, and was left with the Indian Ocean rim: **India, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Swahili coast, the Gulf to Iran, across to Morocco** — 5,000 years of trading history, young populations, high savings, low debt. India (1.3 billion people, average age 28) is "the one super-important country for the next 30 years," likely a geopolitical buffer between the US and China rather than a new hegemon. On **Reliance Industries**: it spans the world's largest solar plant build-out, telecom/data networks, the largest refining complex, and the applications/e-commerce layer — "a pretty straightforward bet," accessible via its UK listing or simply an India ETF (Reliance is among the largest index weights). Caveat: Asian mega-conglomerates historically trade at a discount to NAV.

### 1.3 The business cycle: definition and four phases

Every economy oscillates. Raoul's framing: **"the only probability with the business cycle is timing — not whether it works."** The four phases:

1. **Recovery** — the cycle is in contraction territory (ISM below 50) but turning up; forward-looking indicators inflect first.
2. **Expansion** — ISM above 50 and rising; "usually a good Goldilocks phase."
3. **Slowdown / overheating** — the economy is above trend but momentum peaks; inflation pressures and rate hikes appear.
4. **Contraction** — ISM below 50 and deteriorating; the phase the course says we were exiting in 2023.

GMI's operational version renames these the **four seasons** (used throughout the course):

| Season | Growth | Inflation | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spring** | rising | falling | Disinflationary boom |
| **Summer** | rising | rising | Inflationary boom |
| **Fall** | slowing | still rising | Stagflation |
| **Winter** | falling | falling | Full contraction |

Raoul's dating at the time of the course: **2022 was winter; macro spring began in October 2022** (when the forward-looking indicators bottomed), with the move into **macro summer expected in Q4 2023** — the phase where growth has bottomed, inflation is falling toward its trough, and "more cowbell" arrives: rate cuts, fiscal stimulus and central-bank balance-sheet action.

### 1.4 The ISM: the monthly proxy of the cycle

The **ISM Manufacturing Index** (a survey of purchasing managers covering new orders, production, inventories, etc.) is "just basically a monthly proxy for the business cycle." It is an **oscillator** — "the key is in the title" — and its up-and-down path *is* the GDP cycle, leading the economy by about a month. The probabilistic punchline Raoul delivered at Cambridge ("why everything they teach you about economics is bullshit"): a three-year-old can see that after the oscillator peaks it falls. Once the ISM rolls over you are pre-warned; the damage arrives as it approaches 50.

Charts described from narration:

- **ISM vs. S&P 500, year-on-year, back to 1950** (Bittel zooms in; Raoul shows the same): "they're the same." When the ISM rises, equities rise in YoY terms; when it falls, equities fall.
- The same overlay works for **emerging markets (EEM)**, **oil**, **copper**, **aluminum**, **lumber**, the **Korean won**, **high-yield credit (HYG)**, and **crypto** — "everything is the business cycle."
- **Cyclicals vs. defensives** (materials, industrials, consumer discretionary vs. staples, utilities, healthcare) tracks the ISM; Bittel shows S&P 500 vs. the consumer-staples ETF to make the point.
- **High-beta vs. min-vol** (style-factor version of the same trade) is, per Bittel, **the most correlated ratio to crypto — roughly 85% correlated to Bitcoin** over the period (crypto YoY comps run to 1000%+, so it must be mapped via ratios, not levels).
- **Bond yields** also track the ISM "in every case — except recently," an unprecedented dislocation discussed below.

### 1.5 The magic nexus: where the cycle meets the secular trend

The course's central investing rule: **secular trends are where real long-term wealth is made (3–10 year horizon); the business cycle gives you the timing.** "Where the business cycle hits the secular trend — that's where the magic happens." Buying secular trends (technology, crypto, India) at cyclical troughs gives you both tailwinds; the cycle then tells you when to add or trim allocation. Use **log charts** for secular trends. Raoul's own 2023 execution: he held technology and crypto as secular core, used the spring upturn, and notes you may trade *around* the position — but he personally prefers sizing by the cycle rather than trading the core.

### 1.6 2008 regime change: QE, debasement, and the 3.5-year debt-refi cycle

Raoul's most important structural claim: **everything changed in 2008.** With US total (private + government) debt-to-GDP around **240%** and every borrower at the same wall, the system passed the point where debt could generate growth. The response — quantitative easing — is currency debasement: **a 97.5% correlation between the equity market and G5 central-bank liquidity** ("a ludicrous match"). Bittel's version: central-bank liquidity since 2008 explains roughly **97% of the variability in equities** (R² on a scatter of the G5 net-liquidity proxy vs. the S&P 500, back at "fair value" as of August 2023).

Two sub-discoveries:

1. **The business cycle became perfectly cyclical after 2008.** The ISM and the cycle troughs now print on an almost metronomic schedule. Why: roughly **70% of US debt (government and private) is clustered in the 2–5 year maturity bucket**, whose midpoint — **~3.5 years — is the business cycle**. Every ~3.5 years the debt must be refinanced; rates get reset to zero, the central bank absorbs the government's debt onto its balance sheet, and the currency is debased again. "They're paying the credit card with another credit card." Raoul suspects the BOJ and ECB run the same mechanism ("I think they all know what they're doing at the same time").
2. **QE does not flow into equities as money.** "I can mathematically prove none of that money flows into the equity market — volumes don't go up." Instead, assets are **remarked higher** the moment the market collectively realizes the currency is being debased (his wine-auction analogy: announce 10 glasses and $10,000 of bidding, and the first glass instantly trades at $5,000).

Deflating the S&P 500 by the Fed balance sheet, the market has gone **nowhere since 2008–09 — same as Europe** (the US "outperformance" is a reserve-currency remarking effect). What *did* outperform the balance sheet: **technology and crypto** — the secular trends. What underperformed: **gold** ("supposed to be the great inflation hedge; it has been terrible against debasement — I don't understand why it's done so badly"), and **house prices** in unlevered terms (leverage is why homeowners still did fine). Lesson: own secular trends, because "secular trends outperform cyclical trends," and debasement makes scarce, trend-backed assets reprice while wages (variable incomes) lag.

### 1.7 Debasement is not inflation

A distinction Raoul hammers: printing money via QE creates **debasement** — falling purchasing power *versus scarce assets* — not consumer-price inflation. The Turkish stock market (in dollars) is the visible case; the US case is invisible because all fiat currencies are debasing simultaneously ("relative weakness everywhere"). The 2021 inflation spike to 9% "had nothing to do with money printing." Debasement is the **mutualization of debt-servicing costs** and a steady transfer of wealth from wage earners to asset holders: houses in 2021 were "remarked higher" by devaluation, not bought higher — trading data showed no demand surge. Assets are deferred consumption; debasement makes your future self poorer.

### 1.8 Bubbles: never short them

At cycle tops and bottoms "you usually get a bubble and usually get a crisis" (2023's pair: crypto and US regional banks). A real bubble is a **mass delusion / reflexive narrative** — Raoul recommends Soros' *The Alchemy of Finance* — not merely "a market that went up a lot." Within secular trends, apparent bubbles burst and recover (every Bitcoin high is eventually taken out); commodity bubbles like **gold in the 1970s** or silver can persist; **real estate 2007** was lethal because it was debt-levered against cyclical cash flows. Rules: **never short a bubble** ("shorting a bubble with leverage is a sure way to go broke — it lasts about three minutes"); per Soros, "if you see a bubble, invest in it," with one eye on the exit.

## Part 2 — Measuring the Cycle: Liquidity, Growth, Inflation (Andreas Steno Larsen)

### 2.1 Three variables and the regime matrix

George's bridge into Part 2 sets the discipline: forget 100-variable multivariate models; track **five to ten indicators**, and ignore anything on the *lagging* side of the indicator board (slide of leading vs. lagging indicators):

- **Lagging — ignore:** payrolls ("on the first Friday of the month, turn off Twitter and the news — it's 12–18 months behind; people managing real money do not use it"), the unemployment rate, wages, shelter CPI ("probably the worst one"), industrial production, durable goods, GDP (a month behind for the previous quarter).
- **Leading — use:** the growth, inflation, and liquidity indicators below. Real-money managers know the payroll *trend* 3–6 months in advance from these.

Andreas' framework is three variables — **liquidity, growth, inflation** — because liquidity (central-bank balance sheets) now drives everything, while growth and inflation drive the central banks that decide liquidity. His regime matrix (traffic lights) ranks the eight combinations:

- **Best: liquidity up, inflation down, growth down — the "QE-like scenario."** His March 2023 read (reiterated through the July AMA): already in its early stages — liquidity rising daily (contrary to mainstream narrative), inflation falling (measured CPI is a *lagged* representation), growth declining from the prior liquidity squeeze.
- **Worst: liquidity down, inflation down, growth down simultaneously** — worst case "for a broad-based asset allocation, on the long side at least."

### 2.2 Dollar liquidity: the proxy and the agents behind it

**The proxy: dollar liquidity = Federal Reserve assets − Treasury General Account (TGA) − overnight reverse repo (ON RRP) usage.** TGA and ON RRP withdraw liquidity and can counteract Fed trends. When all three flash green at once, "it's a very, very strong signal to buy risk assets." The correlation of this proxy with the S&P 500 is "very strong — stronger than ever right now," and Andreas credits it for technology and Bitcoin strength amid recession scares and the banking crisis: "liquidity drives everything, even amidst turmoil."

The agents (three in the live session, expanded to five in the AMA):

1. **The Federal Reserve.** The Fed reacts mechanically to *measured* growth and inflation ("they only take decisions based on things they can measure today"), so falling growth/inflation data mechanically produce balance-sheet expansion. QT detail (AMA): the stated runoff cap is **$95bn/month**, but actual QT runs **$80–85bn/month** because mortgage "golden handcuffs" (2020–21 vintages) suppress refinance turnover — the Fed redeems holdings only when mortgages pay off. Emergency lending (March 2023): the **discount window** (old, expensive), the **Bank Term Funding Program — BTFP** (launched March 2023, accepts even weaker collateral — "you can almost show up with your desktop and get a loan"), and **Other Credit Extensions** — loans to the **FDIC** after bank failures (First Republic), which the FDIC is now the biggest borrower of. The shift toward Other Credit Extensions *drains* liquidity (dollars stop circulating when the FDIC holds the debt), which is why liquidity faded from May/June despite the March support.
2. **The US Treasury and the TGA.** A dollar is held by the private sector or the public sector; when the Treasury parks issuance proceeds at the Fed, liquidity is withdrawn. The TGA is "almost the Strategic Petroleum Reserve of dollar cash" — and by law it must be drawn down close to **zero ahead of a debt-ceiling deadline** (to keep politicians negotiating), which injects liquidity into private markets. After the June 2023 deal, the Treasury rebuilds it — Andreas' live estimate: **$400–500bn** of re-absorption; by the August AMA the published target was **~$750bn by year-end** (~$300bn still to go). Crucially, **composition matters**: money-market funds can absorb T-bill issuance one-for-one out of ON RRP, but cannot buy the 2-, 5-, 10-, 20-, 30-year **coupons** the Treasury announced — so coupon-heavy issuance drains liquidity faster.
3. **Money market funds.** MMFs have direct Fed access via the **ON RRP**, where ~**80%** of usage is MMFs. When T-bills are scarce they park cash at the Fed (draining liquidity); when T-bill supply surges they withdraw from ON RRP and buy bills (countering Treasury drains roughly one-for-one). Over summer 2023 MMFs offset "**70–80% of the total liquidity effect**." Practical risk note (AMA): MMFs also hold commercial paper — "the risk is larger than what was marketed in the spring"; know what your fund holds. On the deposit side: with de facto (post-March) deposit insurance above the formal limits, and MMF yields far above bank deposit rates, moving cash to MMFs is rational — the banks *cannot* raise deposit rates, because they fund short and lend long, so every inversion forces banks to hold deposit rates near zero, which is exactly what triggers deposit flight.
4. **The US banking system / private credit creation — "the dark horse."** Private banks *create* dollars by lending. In **2018–2020** the Fed's balance sheet contracted while M2 kept rising on private credit — and equities did fine. In mid-2023 Andreas sees the same early pattern: broad money rebounding for 6–7 weeks on private-bank credit despite QT; loan-demand surveys (ECB and Fed, ~August 1) turning up even with tighter standards. You cannot forecast it and the data is only weekly/monthly — "as soon as you start watching private credit creation, you end up trading on outdated information" — which is why the tradable proxy uses only the first agents. When the liquidity-vs-S&P gap widens and people call a sell signal, Andreas' answer is: include private credit, "then the answer is not that crystal clear."

### 2.3 Growth indicators: three ways to call the ISM

Andreas' growth target is the **ISM Manufacturing** — "the cyclical part of the economy is what we price equities against; we have a long-term anchor and price the volatility with the cycle."

1. **The Chinese credit impulse — "the best leading indicator in the world."** China is "the leading indicator of leading indicators" (for the last 5–10 years), and its liquidity impulse leads US ISM by roughly 6–18 months (he uses "about a year" in the AMA: "typically we see in between a year from the actual pickup in credit until we see the end result in manufacturing"). In 2023 China was the one place on earth with a strongly positive liquidity impulse, implying an ISM trough then "a sharp reversal higher."
2. **The yield-curve method.** Take the **average of the 2-year and 10-year US Treasury yields**, compute the year-over-year rate of change, and **forward it ~15 months**: "an extremely neat correlation" with ISM turns. The yield curve itself he calls "the best forward-looking indicator of US activity." Mid-2023 reading: an ISM low at the end of Q3 / early Q4 2023 — "exactly the timing where you want to be long risk assets with a very sharp overweight."
3. **Cyclical/defensive equity ratios.** A ratio of cyclical equity sectors (e.g., **industrials vs. healthcare**; more broadly a basket with commodity and consumer footprints) to defensives (pharmaceuticals, utilities) leads the ISM — "you can see how neat the correlation is since 2010." A rising ratio is "a very, very strong signal" of a cyclical upturn, reusable across asset classes.

### 2.4 Inflation indicators: "inflation is very easy to predict"

Andreas: timing is hard, trends are easy. Four methods:

1. **Ask companies — the "wheel of inflation."** Input costs (PPI) precede output costs and consumer prices (CPI), with a margins layer in between. **PPI leads CPI by 1–2 months** (US, Europe, UK, China alike) — "for some reason central banks refuse to look at PPI trends, so it gives you a slight edge." The finer version is the **NFIB survey of SMEs' net price plans** (do you plan to hike prices in the next 3 months?): it **leads core inflation by ~6 months**. Reading at the time: ~**25% net** planning hikes → consistent with core inflation of **~3–3.5%** six months forward; base case: core bottoms around **3%, not 2%**. The same survey flagged the 2021 spike in advance — "companies told you and no one listened."
2. **Follow the money.** **Broad M2 shifted forward 17 months** explains ~**30–40% of the variance** in inflation — weak fit, huge lead, still tradable. The then-shocking data point: US broad money was contracting at a **>5% annualized rate — the first negative annual reading in modern time-series history** (no observation back to the 1960s). Consequences: the Fed "has already overdone it"; bank deposits shrink (his explanation of the deposit crisis); **deflation risk is on the table for 2024** ("China already has actual deflation").
3. **Freight rates.** The **Shanghai→LA containerized freight index** (and China→West rates generally) gives a *daily* signal that **leads retail goods prices by up to six months**; by mid-2023 rates were back to pre-pandemic levels, "the downturn in goods prices is not over."
4. **Traded commodities** — fertilizer and oil prices lead inflation components by **1–2 months**.

### 2.5 The banking crisis as the liquidity inflection point

Andreas' March–July 2023 thesis: the regional-bank crisis was the *symptom* of shrinking broad money and the *trigger* that forces the next liquidity wave, "because central banks have no choice."

- **Deposit flight mechanics.** Money-market fund yields vastly exceed deposit rates; digitization makes flight "lightning speed"; every inverted yield curve produces this. His forecast: a more concentrated, "Europeanised" US banking system (fewer banks, easier to control).
- **The Yellen hearing.** Senator James Lankford (R-OK) asked whether an Oklahoma bank would get SVB's treatment; Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen would not answer, ultimately deferring to a "systemic importance" review with a threshold around **$200bn in assets** — which, Andreas argues, *accelerated* the flight from small banks to large ones.
- **Why the post-2008 regulation fails.** In a fractional-reserve system "no bank can sustain a large deposit flight for a prolonged period — no bank, no matter the regulation": ~**50–60%** of large-bank funding is deposits. Two pillars: the **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)** assumes household deposits are flightier than corporate deposits — but Eurozone data showed **corporate deposits left at double the speed of households** (European MMF regulation of 2018 blocked retail access, so corporates did the fleeing). And **HQLA** rules let banks count Treasuries at par with no haircut — but the long-US-Treasuries index drew down ~**40% from its 2020 peak**; SVB (unhedged) is the case study. The Fed's BTFP — lending against underwater Treasuries *at par* — exists precisely to make the regulation's fiction true. Conclusion: the framework "didn't pass a reality test," so authorities will cave and add liquidity — the inflection.
- **Knock-on effects (in-room Q&A):** banks will make credit conservative for quarters after emergency lending → **real estate and illiquid assets suffer with a lag**, while liquid assets re-rate first. Minimum policy fix: Fed funds down toward **~2%** to un-invert the curve, "probably a bit below to stop the deposit flight." **Don't own bank equity** — "damned if they do, damned if they don't."
- **The TGA wildcard:** a debt-ceiling deal plus QT would be "the perfect storm"; the Treasury pre-announces issuance plans, so this risk is watchable in advance (target rebuild $400–500bn, later formalized at ~$750bn).

## Part 3 — Asset Allocation Across the Cycle (Julien Bittel)

Julien opens with Howard Marks: *"the key to dealing with the future lies in knowing where we are, even if you can't know precisely where we're going."* His job in the course: convert the cycle into allocation. His 90-slide masterclass (two hours live) makes three moves: measure the cycle across countries, map growth×inflation into seasons with a season-by-season asset table, then stack a five-pillar market checklist on top.

### 3.1 Everything is the business cycle

Recap of the evidence (all charts described from narration): **ISM vs. S&P 500 YoY since 1950**; **EEM**; **cyclicals vs. defensives**; **high-beta vs. min-vol** (and its **~85% correlation to Bitcoin**); **oil, aluminum, copper, lumber**; **bond yields** (currently a historic dislocation — "these alligator jaws will converge, that's why we still like bonds"); **high yield (HYG)**. The business cycle drives all asset prices "over a six- to eighteen-month time window, depending on what you're looking at." And the cycle should be drawn **on top of the secular trendline** — the flat ISM oscillator overlaid on the rising secular charts of tech and crypto.

### 3.2 The four growth quadrants and country breadth

The cycle is quantified per country with the ISM proxy and binned into four quadrants:

- **Recovery:** ISM below 50 but accelerating
- **Expansion:** ISM above 50 and rising
- **Slowdown:** above 50 but peaking
- **Contraction:** below 50 and deteriorating

(He stresses these are pedagogy for the framework, not literal ISM prints for every country.) Then aggregate: **the percentage of tracked countries in each quadrant**, a series he can show back to the 1970s–80s. The regimes **rotate in sequence**: peak contraction → peak recovery → peak expansion → peak slowdown → peak contraction.

The worked history (the course's core demonstration):

- **May 2021: 88% of countries in expansion** — the extreme. Next comes slowdown: the global manufacturing PMI peaked that month.
- **November 2021: 82% of countries in slowdown** — on high alert for the rotation into contraction. It "pretty much nailed the highs" — Bitcoin topped in November 2021, equities in December.
- **October 2022: 88% of countries in contraction** — the most extreme since COVID and, before that, the global financial crisis. "You can't statistically get any worse" — so he shifted to lead indicators: the **China credit impulse had bottomed in November 2021**; the share of OECD countries with rising monthly indicators had fallen to **0% in May 2022**; his GMI financial-conditions index had inflected. Equities bottomed in October 2022.
- **Mid-2023 update:** ~**30% recovery, 6% expansion, 65% contraction, 0% slowdown**; ~**47% of countries improving**; OECD breadth up to **71%** rising month-on-month; ~**70% of countries in the spring season quadrant**; but very few countries growing *above trend* (US trend GDP ~1.75%) — "room to run."

Also: the **GMI growth impulse leads the S&P 500 YoY by ~5 months**; the growth tables lead the inflation tables by about a month (early warning); **the biggest opportunities are at the extremes** (above ~80–88% in any quadrant), because the probability of the regime persisting is then very low.

### 3.3 The four seasons: beta vs. alpha

Julien's key strategic distinction: **getting growth right is a directional (beta) decision — buy or sell. Getting growth *and* inflation right is where alpha comes from** — the relative calls that let you earn returns throughout the cycle. The seasons (Spring = disinflationary boom; Summer = inflationary boom; Fall = stagflation; Winter = growth and inflation both down) are tracked with the same country-breadth tables:

- **May 2021: 81% of countries in summer** — the peak before fall gathered steam.
- **June 2022: 100% of countries in fall** — extreme; oil topped that month and commodities rolled over (at the May 2022 GMI roundtable in Grand Cayman his oil target was **~$60**; against "the inflationistas calling an oil breakout every five minutes").
- **December 2022: 75% of countries in winter** — and "what comes after winter? Spring." Which is where the market was as of the course.

### 3.4 The season-by-season playbook

The heart of the course: for each season, an asset-allocation table — asset classes, top/bottom three equity regions, sectors, fixed income, currencies, commodities, style factors — with **GMI traffic lights** (historical **Z-scores**: green = statistically significant positive, red = statistically significant negative, yellow = noise) and **absolute vs. relative returns for a one-standard-deviation move** (relative benchmarks: equity regions vs. MSCI ACWI, bonds vs. the JP Morgan Global Bond index, commodities vs. the Goldman Sachs Commodity Index, style factors vs. global equities, currencies vs. the dollar). The framework is backtested as far as data allows (~35+ years; MSCI limits; crypto can't be significant — insufficient history). "It is not a black box. It's just a probability" — the same phenomena as the ISM charts, tabulated.

**Spring (growth up, inflation down) — "what's working now":**

- Long **equities and credit** — *not* commodities; bonds don't normally work here (the 2023 bond dislocation being the noted exception — "we were early; we're still confident").
- Top regions: **China, EM, US** (the dollar is the bottom-ranked currency — statistically significant — and a falling dollar lifts China/EM).
- Short region: **Pacific ex-Japan** — i.e., mostly Australia — whose index is materials + financials; being short it while long the spring sectors means **you were cyclical without owning banks** through the regional-bank crisis.
- Sectors: **consumer discretionary, semiconductors, technology**; you're paid to pocket the **credit-risk premium** (BBB spreads vs. Treasuries).
- Commodities: copper works; style: growth-type factors.
- Absolute returns: "everything's up indiscriminately" — which is why the relative table matters.

**Summer (growth up, inflation up):**

- Top overweights shift to **commodities and equities**; credit still fine, but **not cash and bonds** (inflation is rising).
- Cyclicals *change character*: sectors become **materials, industrials, financials**; shorts become **healthcare, staples, consumer services** — "people think cyclicals are just materials, industrials, financials. It's not."
- Fixed income: **TIPS / inflation-linked bonds** enter the top three.
- Currencies: **commodity currencies**; dollar short with an extreme Z-score (**4.5**).
- Industrial commodities: **crude oil, lumber, copper**; style: **small caps** over quality. Prefer **long crude, short gold** — gold is *not* the best inflation-rising hedge.
- Absolute returns still positive — a full risk-on regime.

**Fall (growth slowing, inflation still rising) — "the trickiest season":**

- Commodities still overweight but **selective** — growth has peaked, so split **cyclical vs. defensive commodities** (see below).
- Regions: **UK and Switzerland** outperform (energy plus defensive/staples exposure).
- Sectors: **energy, utilities, staples** — "definitely not technology, and not everything you owned in spring," because bond yields are rising.
- Fixed income: long **TIPS**; and the curve signal — long-duration bonds in the top three, short-duration in the bottom three — reads as a **bear flattening**.
- The dollar starts creeping up.
- **Absolute returns go negative across the board**: margins take a "double whammy" (input and wage costs rising while top lines fall). This is where active relative allocation earns its keep.

**Winter (growth down, inflation down):**

- **Bonds and cash. "Cash is king — cash is not trash here."** Equities and commodities are a no-go.
- Top region: **Switzerland** (the SMI is mostly staples and healthcare — "really the most defensive market in the world").
- Sectors: **consumer staples, healthcare, utilities**; short **financials, materials, industrials**.
- Fixed income: **government bonds** — no credit risk.
- Currencies: **dollar, yen, Swiss franc**.
- Commodities in relative terms: **long gold, livestock, wheat; short lumber, copper, crude oil** — exactly what traded until October 2022. The behavioral logic of "staple commodities": lose your job and you downgrade the meat, "but you're not going to go vegan"; you keep eating cereal (wheat). **Cotton**, conversely, is *cyclical* — clothing is consumer discretionary. "You can own commodities all along the cycle — you just need to pick the right ones."

Plus the **GMI cheat sheet / asset-class watch list**: the same traffic lights applied across spring/summer/fall/winter for every index, country, sector (including home builders, regional banks, miners) they track. Workflow: identify the season → read what works → check charts → decide.

### 3.5 The five-pillar checklist (the October 2022 worked example)

Julien thinks about markets through five pillars — **macro (growth), valuations, earnings, sentiment/positioning, liquidity** — and walks through why October 2022 was the buy point:

1. **Growth:** the ISM on his macro overbought/oversold indicator (ISM ranked as a percentile; green below ~15, yellow near ~90, and it overshoots both ways) entered **oversold**.
2. **Valuations:** P/Es had fallen **26%** (recession norm ~20%); at the extreme the S&P was **-15% YoY** while the valuation model pointed to **+30% YoY** upside. Historically, valuations have only been a genuine constraint **five times** — when his two lines meet: **Q4 1980, Q2 1987, Q1 2000, Q4 2007, Q4 2021**. As of August 2023 equities could rise another **~20%** before valuations bind.
3. **Earnings:** revisions had tanked to the lowest since COVID (and before that 2008). Revisions bottom *before* the ISM, then often double-bottom — "can they go lower? Yes. It doesn't matter." The danger signal is the reverse: Q4 2021, when revisions priced an ISM of **~65** while lead indicators deteriorated.
4. **Sentiment/positioning:** sentiment at the **lowest since December 2008**; AAII net bulls in the mid-20s (above **30%** = warning); Investor Intelligence detrended at GFC extremes; CFTC S&P 500 positioning at a **record net short (~-10%)** — the largest in ~25 years. Institutions are mandated to be invested and had been painfully short all year (visible in BofA fund-manager surveys); retail was long, the pros were not — so shorts unwinding "at a steep second derivative" put a **floor under equities**.
5. **Liquidity:** rising — his call since May 2022 on a six-month lead, "the main driver of equities this year."

October 2022 also had **DeMARK indicators** aligning: sequences of 9s and 13s across daily, weekly and monthly timeframes on tech. His synthesis rule: the pillars exist to **trim probabilities** — the tables give maybe 65%; "add liquidity analysis, add DeMARK, add this, add that, you get to 70–75%." And on the psychology: "when you've done your work and you *still* feel scared — that's the equity risk premium. That's what you're paid."

### 3.6 The GMI dominoes: the lead-lag chain

The "domino chart" orders the indicators from leading to lagging (blue = leading, white = lagging; leads/lags set by eye plus a correlation optimizer):

- **GMI Financial Conditions Index — leads current ISM by ~9 months**
- **OECD country breadth — ~6 months**
- **GMI ISM momentum index — ~6 months**
- **New orders-to-inventories — ~3 months** (this is what equities actually trade with — "the discount window for risk assets")
- **New orders / regional Feds (Philly Fed) — today**
- **ISM — T = 0**
- Then lagging: **durable goods (+3 months), industrial production (+4), unemployment (+6), GDP (+~1 quarter)** — which is why **equities lead the economy by ~6 months** (3-month lead vs. ISM + ISM leading GDP by a quarter).

Working the chain (August 2023): the FCI had inflected sharply higher; OECD breadth at 71%; the ISM momentum index called for a **~7-point ISM rise — from 47.7 (February) to ~54.7 by February 2024**; and in ISM YoY terms, +**2.7 points by September** (back above 50, toward ~54, from -10.7 currently). International corroboration: **Sweden 40.9 (May) → 47.6 (July)** (export-led Sweden is itself a global lead), **Norway 48.7 → 56.7**, plus Brazil, South Korea, Australia improving — the same pattern as Chicago PMI's 38 print in November 2022 and the April Philly Fed low: extremes mean-revert. Yes, the ISM could still dip toward **~40** (FCI, CEO Confidence, Philly Fed all allow it) — "but does it matter?" The **Nasdaq had already priced an ISM of ~37.7, the S&P ~45**.

### 3.7 The GMI Financial Conditions Index

A regression on **interest rates, commodity prices and the dollar** (no equity prices, unlike the OECD LEI). It **leads the Goldman Sachs FCI by ~8 months** and all economic data by ~9 months. Falling commodities → falling dollar → falling rates = easing = EM and risk assets outperform (EM is "a pure play on easing financial conditions"). Mid-2023: easing sharply (dollar down, some -4% days in oil), forecasting **~20% commodity-price gains by May 2024** and continued improvement so long as **rates come down in Q4** — the condition to watch.

### 3.8 The anatomy of CPI — why "sticky inflation" is a lag illusion

Julien's structural decomposition (the key to the Fed-watching half of the course):

- **Weights:** energy ~**7%** of headline CPI, food ~**13%**, **core ~80%**. Within core: **goods 21%, services 59%**. Inside services, **shelter is ~34–35% of headline CPI, ~45% of core, and ~60% of services** — "the biggest Babe Ruth weight" — and the **most lagging** component (shelter lags house prices by ~15 months; on the domino chart wages lag the cycle ~13 months, shelter ~17).
- **The inflation dominoes run like the growth dominoes:** commodities → goods → services. Energy had collapsed; goods inflation was falling; services/shelter "still high" — but that is lag, not stickiness.
- **CPI ex-shelter was 11% YoY a year earlier and ~1% YoY now** — "there's nothing sticky about this chart."
- **Commodities get over-weighted in people's minds:** only ~28% of CPI (energy + goods) is commodity-linked. Evidence: **China enters the WTO in 2001; oil goes $40 → $150 (~500%+); CPI averages ~3.5% from 2001–2008**, spiking to ~6% only at the very end, then going negative.
- **House prices:** the median existing one-family home price (advanced 18 months against shelter CPI) implies the 35%-of-CPI shelter component rolls over hard over the following year — GMI called **5–10% YoY house-price deflation**. The supply of new homes had already peaked (a future price support that won't reach CPI for ~12 months).
- **Wages:** JOLTS **quits rate leads wages ~10 months**; job openings in cyclical sectors, small-business compensation plans, and the Dallas and Richmond Fed wage surveys had all rolled — so the Atlanta Fed wage tracker follows them down. "Wages aren't sticky. They're just extremely lagging."
- **PPI:** leads CPI by 1–2 months; still soft (German PPI **-6%**, Norway around **-40%**, Brazil/China/Spain negative) — the Fed doesn't target PPI, so he won't fight the hawks over it.
- **Fed reaction function:** core month-on-month annualized had run **below the Fed's target for two consecutive months (first time since Feb 2021)**, and core-ex-shelter had printed outright monthly deflation — "the Fed's inflation job is done." What it waits for is the labor market: **"the Fed has never not cut rates after a 0.5% rise in the unemployment rate"** (back to 1950). With unemployment at 3.6% and models pointing to ~**4.6%**, and the rise expected **around Q4 2023**, easing follows. The Fed operates roughly **six months behind the ISM** — Fed funds lagged six months against the ISM is "the same chart going back to 1950" — and this time went further than usual (the only comparable bond decoupling was the 1970s), because Powell's September 2020 declaration that the Fed would never again trust forecasts changed the reaction function.

**Liquidity pillar, quantified:** **G5 net liquidity** (Fed/ECB/BOJ/BOE/PBOC balance sheets minus TGA, ON RRP and equivalents — you can no longer look at the balance sheet alone). The ISM inverted and advanced 15 months says liquidity rises because the cycle forces it; the projection was a **~40% YoY rise in G5 net liquidity by mid-2024** (the "Everything Code" thesis). The Nasdaq had, by late July 2023, priced roughly an **$800bn injection above realized Fed liquidity** — an overshoot (2019 is the analog), which is what normal, orderly pullbacks are made of. **FedNet liquidity vs. the S&P 500, vs. the Nasdaq in relative terms, vs. bond yields (inverted), and vs. Bitcoin**: "liquidity is the tide that lifts all assets."

### 3.9 Where the course said we were (state of play, mid-2023)

Year-to-date the market priced **spring**; month-to-date it drifted toward pricing **winter**; summer was "not even on the radar MTD." Julien computes **implied probabilities of each season** by matching cross-asset performance against every historical instance of each season (back ~50 years) — and uses mismatches as opportunity finders: if the market prices winter but you believe spring, you go to the spring table and buy what's statistically favored — e.g., **China** (broke out, retraced 50% of the move to the 50% Fibonacci, tracing an inverse head-and-shoulders) and **semiconductors vs. the S&P** (straight-line outperformance), the **GMI exponential-age basket +40% YTD**, **BBB credit** for the carry, short the dollar. The Conference Board LEI and every confidence survey sat in "100% recession territory" — but those are the lagging side; the leading side (China credit impulse, FCI, OECD breadth, new orders/inventories) had all turned. His summary judgment: **"once you know about the business cycle — growth, inflation, liquidity — you can't unsee it."**

## Part 4 — Q&A: The Practical Answers

The AMAs and in-room questions carry the most directly actionable material. Answers below are the speakers' actual positions, condensed.

### 4.1 Raoul Pal (AMA, with Ash Bennington)

- **Where are we in the cycle?** Macro spring (began October 2022), heading to **summer in Q4 2023**. Sequence to expect: ISM bottoms first (the hoped-for ~40 low was late but "the up-and-down is science; the timing is art"), **GDP bottoms Q4 2023–Q1 2024** ("one last flush lower to confirm recession"), headline inflation bottoms "around zero or less" late 2023/early 2024, core CPI deflates most of 2024, unemployment keeps rising — and that combination brings "more cowbell."
- **What to own by season?** He defers sector detail to Bittel but gives his blunt version: spring→summer favors **cyclicals — miners, oil, commodity currencies — with a weaker dollar**; his own book is concentrated in the secular trends (**technology + crypto**) because "nothing creates long-term returns right now except the secular trends." Evidence he cites: last Fed pivot (2018–19): **S&P +15%, Nasdaq +20%, his exponential-age basket +30%, Bitcoin +300% in the first six months**; this cycle: **crypto +80–100% YTD vs. Nasdaq +15%**, his basket +71% YTD. "Once you're in crypto, 15% is a rounding error."
- **Has his cycle view ever been badly wrong?** The 2009 story: he nailed the recession, then built an apocalypse narrative ("1929 again; -50% isn't enough, it has to be -70%") and expected a double-bottom that never came — "I convinced myself out of the cycle with my own emotions." The fix: in 2020 he covered all shorts purely because the cycle said so, and in 2022–23 he and Bittel caught the October low the same way. **"Markets do what they do based on the business cycle. What you want is not what markets do."**
- **Bonds:** he's losing money on them — they decoupled from the cycle — but the reason is intentional Fed lateness: the Fed must get rates below trend growth because **~$6 trillion of debt rolls in 2023 and another $3–4 trillion in 2024 at higher rates, with interest payments already at ~$1 trillion**; it needs inflation to undershoot for cover, issuing at the front end meanwhile. (Bonds re-engage — see Bittel's answer, 4.3.)
- **Banking crisis part two (CRE, more runs)?** "A narrative." The cycle has turned, so the *probability* of bank trouble has fallen; and if it happens anyway, "it just means more liquidity faster" — a 20% market drawdown on a bank failure is a **buy** for tech/crypto, not a short. "I trade the economic signals, with one eye on the banking crisis."
- **Blocking out noise:** have a framework; then check *who* is talking — "look at their Twitter feed and see what kind of person they are… you might find they are gold people," emotionally attached to an asset class and rooting for the apocalypse that validates it. Recession calls in 2023 ignored that the crash already happened: **in 2022 growth tech fell 75–80%, the Nasdaq ~35%, crypto ~85% — the recession was priced then.** "Don't ever look at today to understand markets. Live in the forward-looking data."
- **Tech innovation and the cycle:** innovation doesn't remove the cycle (credit still turns), it changes the *trend rate* — usually disinflationarily ("everything that gets digitized goes to zero in cost"). Shale is the oil-price case study. His endgame: AI + nuclear → energy cost toward zero within ~20 years → a possible **20–30 year mega-boom** ("a renaissance"), with commensurate social disruption; boomers exiting the workforce are replaced by AI/robots.
- **Bubbles:** never short them; leverage into a bubble "works really well until it doesn't"; secular-trend bubbles recover, so he doesn't trade around his crypto core — he sizes it and buys cycle downturns.

### 4.2 Andreas Steno Larsen (in-room Q&A + AMA, with Ash Bennington)

- **Country Garden (August 2023):** second-biggest Chinese developer, missing dollar-bond coupons, suspending onshore trading — **not a recession trigger**: bondholders had already marked **90–95% losses**, the international system isn't exposed, and "what happens in China stays in China, apparently." The unknown is the unmeasurable **shadow credit market** ("everything that we cannot measure"). Long-term China demographics are "not pretty," but any can-kicking just defers the reckoning.
- **August regime read:** liquidity still dwindling (TGA refill, coupon-heavy issuance), headline CPI re-accelerated to **3.2%** in July, but growth re-accelerating beneath the surface — against consensus, including his own earlier recession call ("to be brutally honest").
- **Are banks a buy?** Cyclically, a thumbs-up (manufacturing rebounding, private credit rebounding) — with one caveat: the Chinese property story is bad for financials.
- **EUR/USD traders:** the euro gains against the dollar on global cyclical upswings, so the pair is an **embedded equity hedge** (dollar strengthens when equities sell off). Practical rules: if you're a US investor holding US assets, leave the FX open; **in cycle downturns seek shelter locally** (a US holder of euro assets takes the equity hit *and* the FX hit — "a double whammy"); conversely, in an upswing like 2023, buying foreign assets adds an FX tailwind for a US investor.
- **Deposits:** moving deposits to money-market funds — "well, hell, yeah" (the rate gap is the whole story); expect the Fed funds rate to need to reach **~2% or slightly below** to stop the flight; **don't own bank equity**. Europe already regulates deposits into stickiness (2018 MMF rules shut retail out — "it's almost impossible for me to buy a T-bill in Denmark unless I buy 10 million of the series"); Switzerland treats bank deposits as non-fungible with cash; negative rates were partly a deposit-flight defense — "hence… crypto" (Raoul), with CBDC as the institutional version of that control.
- **Real estate:** after emergency lending, banks turn conservative for 2–3 quarters — credit tightens into Q2/Q3 2023, so **illiquid assets (real estate above all) suffer with a lag** while liquid assets re-rate first.
- **Liquidity-forecasting humility:** four of the five agents are forecastable daily; **private credit creation is the dark horse** — it rescued liquidity in 2018–20 and again in summer 2023, and it's why "the liquidity gap must close" sell signals are not automatic.

### 4.3 Julien Bittel (in-room Q&A + AMA, with Ash Bennington)

- **How can we be "in spring/summer" if recession is still coming?** The dominoes: the ISM *leads* GDP by a quarter, so **GDP prints weak in Q4 2023 even as lead indicators rise** — the classic bottoming window, and precisely the cover the Fed needs to ease. Durable goods lag ISM by 3 months, IP by 4, unemployment by 6.
- **Could this be 2008 again?** Not their base case: no hard landing view; bank credit tightening (near COVID/GFC levels) **was already priced** when the S&P implied an ISM of 45 and the Nasdaq 37.7. Post-recession, unemployment may prove stickier than usual because of AI-driven restructuring — but that's an end-of-cycle story, not now.
- **Bonds — "buy bonds, wear diamonds" when?** They were GMI's "pain point" of 2023 (long from May 2022, too painful to ride). The pressure is a **supply problem, not an inflation problem**: the Treasury issues at the front end to fund ~$1tn of interest, and a possible Chinese defense of the CNY (from ~$3tn of liquid reserves, heavily Treasuries) could mean "double QT" — though his hunch: if China sells, **the Fed reverses QT immediately**. The 10-year had gone from **50bps (July 2020) to ~4.3%**; the trade becomes attractive again **late Q4 2023 / early Q1 2024** as the easing window arrives.
- **How are the implied season probabilities computed?** Take every historical episode of each season (~50 years of data), correlate current cross-asset performance (equities, bonds, commodities, credit, crypto where history allows) against all past instances; the match gives the probability weighting. In August 2023: YTD = spring; MTD = tilting winter; summer next as commodities base.
- **What could break the framework?** Structural shifts (demographics, yield-curve control spillovers from Japan, pension flows) can't be seen in a backtest — his honest answer: "you certainly can't account for them; I don't have an answer." Empirical comfort: the 2023 banking crisis — "not on our bingo card" — changed *nothing* in the allocation model's outputs. The one acknowledged regime break is bonds (the 1970s-style Fed lateness). "There is no black box. It's just a probability."
- **Long term — US stocks for debasement or India for demographics?** Both, but ranked: **only secular bull markets outperform debasement — technology and crypto.** Gold, housing, even the S&P 500 have roughly failed to beat the central-bank balance sheet; **India (the monsoon thesis) is the other secular leg.** Long-term answer: long India, long tech, long crypto.
- **For a private investor with a job — how much work is this?** The framework is being productized so you can run the scenario tables yourself; but the underlying discipline is: identify the season, check the breadth extremes, then let valuation/sentiment/trend overlays trim the odds. On horizon: if your bet is a 10–15-year secular trend, use the cycle for **position sizing, not trading** — "I didn't change my position in crypto; I used the cycle's downside to add." Prepare portfolios *before* regimes flip: the growth breadth tables bottom before the inflation tables, so October 2022-style extremes give you the warning window. "The biggest opportunities are at the extremes; in between, the cycle is less interesting."
- **Doesn't the pivot need a panic first?** (Andreas' risk case.) Possibly — the March 2020 speed came from panic; if the regional-bank index (KRE) breaks down again, "they hit the panic button fast." Either way, the direction of liquidity is up; only the speed varies.

## Appendix A — Key Rules and Frameworks (Compact Reference)

**Cycle structure**

| Item | Rule / value |
|---|---|
| GDP magic formula | Population growth + productivity growth + debt growth |
| Business cycle length (post-2008) | ~3.5 years — 70% of US debt matures in the 2–5y bucket |
| Four phases | Recovery → Expansion → Slowdown → Contraction |
| Four seasons | Spring (growth up/inflation down) → Summer (up/up) → Fall (down-slowing/up) → Winter (down/down) |
| Growth quadrants (ISM terms) | Recovery <50 rising; Expansion >50 rising; Slowdown >50 falling; Contraction <50 falling |
| Extremes rule | >80–88% of countries in one quadrant = regime about to flip; biggest opportunities at extremes |
| Nexus rule | Buy secular trends (3–10y horizon) at cyclical troughs; use the cycle for sizing |

**Measurement toolkit**

| Indicator | Use | Lead |
|---|---|---|
| ISM Manufacturing | Monthly cycle proxy; oscillator | Leads economy ~1 month; GDP by ~1 quarter |
| Chinese credit impulse | "Best leading indicator in the world" | US ISM by ~6–18 months (≈1 year) |
| Avg(2y,10y) yield YoY, forwarded | ISM forecaster | 15 months |
| Cyclical/defensive equity ratio (e.g., industrials/healthcare) | ISM forecaster | Early; neat since 2010 |
| NFIB SME net price plans | Core inflation forecaster | ~6 months (25% net → core ~3–3.5%) |
| PPI | CPI forecaster | 1–2 months |
| Broad M2 shifted | Inflation trend | 17 months; explains 30–40% |
| Shanghai→LA freight | Goods inflation | Up to 6 months, daily data |
| GMI FCI (rates + commodities + dollar regression) | Growth momentum; EM proxy | ~9 months (GS FCI by ~8) |
| OECD country breadth | Global growth momentum | ~6 months |
| New orders-to-inventories | What equities price | ~3 months vs. ISM |
| JOLTS quits rate | Wages | ~10 months |
| Shelter CPI | The lagging "sticky" illusion | Lags house prices ~15–17 months |

**Dollar liquidity proxy**

- **Liquidity = Fed assets − TGA − ON RRP** (+ privately created credit, the unforecastable fifth agent).
- Correlation with S&P 500 ~97.5% (G5 basis, post-2008); Fed operates ~6 months behind the ISM; QT cap $95bn/month, actual $80–85bn; TGA swings from ~0 (debt-ceiling deadlines) to ~$750bn targets; MMFs offset 70–80% of Treasury drains via ON RRP, but only against T-bills, not coupons.
- Best regime: liquidity up + inflation down + growth down ("QE-like"); worst: all three down.

**Season playbook (summary)**

| Season | Long | Short / avoid | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring | Equities, credit; China, EM, US; consumer discretionary, semis, tech; copper | Dollar; Pacific ex-Japan; staples/utilities/healthcare | "Everything up" — alpha comes from the relative table |
| Summer | Commodities + equities; materials, industrials, financials; TIPS; commodity FX; crude, lumber, copper; small caps | Cash, bonds; healthcare, staples, consumer services; gold (prefer crude); dollar (Z 4.5) | Full risk-on |
| Fall | Energy, utilities, staples (UK, Switzerland); TIPS; selective/defensive commodities | Technology; short-duration bonds (bear flattening); everything in absolute terms | Margins squeezed; negative absolute returns |
| Winter | Cash, government bonds; Switzerland; staples, healthcare, utilities; USD, JPY, CHF; gold, livestock, wheat | Equities; credit; financials, materials, industrials; lumber, copper, crude | "Cash is king" |

**Market checklist (five pillars)** — growth (ISM percentile: green <15, yellow ~90, overshoots both ways), valuations (binding only ~5 times: 1987, 2000, 2007, 2021), earnings revisions (bottom before ISM; red flag = revisions pricing ISM 65 into a downturn), sentiment/positioning (AAII net bulls >30% = warning; record CFTC net shorts = floor), liquidity (G5 net liquidity; 97% of equity variability post-2008; "the tide that lifts all assets").

**Behavioral rules** — never short a bubble; bubbles get bought at cycle troughs; the equity risk premium is feeling scared after the work is done; check what a pundit owns before believing their apocalypse; "don't look at today to understand markets — live in the forward-looking data"; Fed cuts after any 0.5pp unemployment rise (no exceptions since 1950); the recession is priced in the *drawdown year before* the recession headlines.
