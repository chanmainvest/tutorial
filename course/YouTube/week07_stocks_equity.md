**VIDEO TITLE:** Stocks and Equity — What You Actually Own When You Buy a Share | Week 7

**RUNTIME TARGET:** ~18 minutes

**HOSTS:** Horace, Stella

---

**[INTRO]**

**Horace:** For the first six weeks of this course we've been
talking about stocks as if everyone already knew what one is.
Today we open the box. What is a company. What is a stock. What
exactly do you own when the brokerage app says you have ten
shares of Apple.

**Stella:** I mean, I know I own a piece of Apple. But I'm not
sure I could explain what that *means*.

**Horace:** That's the entire lesson. And we have to start one
level lower than most courses start — with what a company
*is*, before we can say what owning one means.

---

**[SEGMENT 1: WHAT IS A COMPANY]**

**Horace:** Two views from business school. View one — a
company exists to make profit. Profit is the goal, everything
else is instrumentation. View two — a company has to make profit
in order to *survive*, so it can keep doing whatever it actually
exists to do. Profit is a constraint, not the purpose.

**Stella:** Which is right?

**Horace:** Both are useful. View one is the Friedman framing.
Dominant in US business schools since the 1970s. Tells you when
to trim a position whose business has stopped earning. View two
is the lens this whole course is written from. Under view two,
the deepest fundamental question about a business is *can it
survive, and is it still doing the thing it openly says it does
— or whatever it pursues less openly*. If yes, the numbers will
follow. If no, the numbers will eventually catch up.

**Stella:** And when do the two diverge?

**Horace:** Two situations every investor meets. Profitable
business that has lost its purpose — view one says hold, view
two says the survival clock has started. Barely-profitable
business that is unambiguously doing the thing it exists to do
— view one says sell, view two says this might be the next
compounder. Without both lenses you sell the next great business
eight years early and ride the next zombie ten years too long.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHAT DOES OWNERSHIP MEAN]**

**Horace:** Apple has roughly 15 billion shares outstanding. If
you own one share, you own one fifteen-billionth of Apple.
Legally enforceable property right.

**Stella:** What does that one fifteen-billionth get me?

**Horace:** In a private business the most important word in
"ownership" is *control* — the right to redeploy capital and
command employees. That's what capitalism gives the owner of
capital. In a $3-trillion public mega-cap, that operational
right is *technically intact but practically nil*. Vanguard,
BlackRock, and State Street between them cast 20 to 25 percent
of the votes. Insiders and activists carry most of the rest.
Your hundred shares don't redeploy capital and they don't fire
anyone.

**Stella:** So what do they get me?

**Horace:** Four things. First — a claim on the cash the
business generates. Either paid out to you as dividends and
buybacks, or retained and reinvested in the business so the
business itself grows larger and the share price tracks the
larger claim. Second — a claim on the proceeds of corporate
actions. If someone buys the company at a premium, you get
paid the same per-share consideration as every other holder.
Third — a vote on shareholder matters. Exercise it on
principle, expect zero practical influence. Fourth — a residual
claim on net assets in bankruptcy. The textbook puts this first.
I put it last. In a real bankruptcy the lawyer fees usually eat
the asset value before the equity layer sees anything.

**Stella:** So it's not really like owning a coffee shop with
four friends.

**Horace:** No. It's a minority partnership with no operational
say. Your advantage is liquidity — you can sell any second of
any trading day. Your disadvantage is control — you have none.
Pretending otherwise leads to mental errors we'll correct
shortly.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHY THIS ASSET CLASS EXISTS — CAPITAL IN CAPITALISM]**

**Horace:** Quick stop on the word *capital* in "capitalism."
The whole point of a stock market is to let capital flow to the
businesses that can use it best. A business needs capital to
build factories, hire engineers, fund research. Two sources —
debt, which is contractual, and equity, which is a permanent
residual claim. The stock market is the secondary market where
those equity claims trade hands.

**Stella:** And the price moves capital around.

**Horace:** Exactly. The price *is* the allocation signal. When
a stock goes up the company can issue new shares cheaply. When
it falls the cost of capital rises and the company is starved.

**Stella:** Does that mean only long-term Buffett-style holders
are doing it right?

**Horace:** No. That's an important point this course doesn't
hide. Capitalism's price-discovery mechanism creates legitimate
shorter-horizon plays. Deep-value — buy a business priced near
liquidation value and hold until the gap closes. Mean reversion
— buy what the passive bid has temporarily abandoned. Momentum
or greater-fool — buy something you believe will be flipped to a
less-informed buyer at a higher price. Most retail money made in
the 2010s and 2020s won here, not in Berkshire-style holding. The
long-horizon framing is the *default* in this course because it's
the most reliable on the longest sample, not because everything
else is illegitimate.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: CAPITAL STACK AND ASYMMETRY]**

[VISUAL: image/week07_capital_stack.png]

**Horace:** The financial position of equity. The capital stack.
Suppliers first. Employees next. Lenders. Tax authority.
Preferred shareholders. Then — and only then — common equity.
You sit at the bottom. Residual claimant.

**Stella:** And the upside-downside asymmetry?

**Horace:** When the business does well, bondholders are still
capped at their coupon, suppliers get their invoice, employees
get their salary. Every extra dollar flows to the residual.
Equity captures the upside.

When the business does badly, every senior claim still gets paid
first. Equity eats the shortfall. In bankruptcy, common stock
typically goes to zero. Bondholders take a recovery — 30 to 60
cents on the dollar for senior unsecured, less for subordinated.

**Stella:** Lehman.

**Horace:** Lehman 5.625 of '13, senior unsecured — around 21
cents recovery after a multi-year process. Lehman common — zero.
And by the way, even the bondholder recovery is *before* the
legal fees. In a real bankruptcy the trustees and lawyers can
eat a meaningful slice of what's left. If you ever find yourself
holding the bonds of a company sliding into default, the
practical move is usually to sell to a distressed-debt fund —
not to wait years for "the recovery" while the legal grind chews
half of it.

**Stella:** And that's what the equity risk premium pays for.

**Horace:** Four to six percent a year above Treasuries, on
average, in the US. That's the market's price for being last in
line in exchange for being first in growth.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: THE 7 PERCENT IS A SURVIVOR'S NUMBER]**

**Horace:** Now the part most courses get wrong. The headline.
US stocks return 7 percent a year real over the long run. The
Siegel number.

**Stella:** And it's correctly computed.

**Horace:** Yes. But the *story* most courses attach to it is
wrong. The wrong story — a typical business earns ten percent on
capital, reinvests half, pays out half, you get seven percent
forever. Sounds great. It's describing what *survivors* look
like. It's not what the average business actually does.

**Stella:** And the right story?

**Horace:** The *index* compounds at seven percent. The
*average individual company* does not. Most companies, in the
long run, die or become irrelevant. More than half of all stocks
that have ever traded in the US returned less than T-bills on a
buy-and-hold basis. The seven percent comes from a small
minority of right-tail winners carrying the basket — and from
the index *kicking out the failures* and replacing them with
new entrants.

**Stella:** Survivorship bias.

**Horace:** Built right into the construction rule. The S&P 500
today is not the S&P 500 of 1990. Half the names have been
swapped. Eastman Kodak, Polaroid, Sears, Lehman, GE-as-it-was,
Bear Stearns — out. Apple, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Tesla, Meta,
Alphabet — in. The index that compounds at ten percent is
*actively curated* by a rules-based mechanism that removes
losers and adds winners. It is not a passive bet on "the average
company."

**Stella:** So when someone tells me "just buy and hold for
thirty years" —

**Horace:** They're quietly assuming you do it through a vehicle
whose construction rule is doing the survival filter for you.
Hold a randomly chosen individual stock for thirty years and the
distribution looks completely different. You need either the
basket to be wide enough, or you need to do the work to identify
the survivors. The seven percent doesn't show up just because
you waited.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: FIVE CHANNELS TO SHAREHOLDER WEALTH]**

**Horace:** A profitable business has a small menu of things it
can do with cash. Each one becomes a component of your return.

One — retain and reinvest. Build a factory. Fund R&D. Open a
new geography. If the reinvestment earns above cost of capital,
earnings per share grow and the share price re-rates upward. For
high-growth tech that's the entire shareholder return — no
dividend at all.

Two — pay dividends. Real cash to your account. Taxed at
qualified-dividend rates in the US for most US companies.
Side-note for retirees. The S&P 500 dividend yield has averaged
about 3.5 percent over the long run, the 10-year Treasury about
4.8 percent. Sometimes equities yielded more, sometimes less.
The point is they are not interchangeable. Dividends get cut.
Stocks drop 30 percent in the year you need the income.
Treasury coupons are contractual. Treat a dividend stock as an
equity position with an income tilt, not as a higher-yielding
substitute for a bond.

Three — buy back shares. Repurchase your own stock, retire it,
remaining shareholders own a bigger slice. Mathematically equal
to a dividend at fair value, but the tax bill is deferred. And
— candidly — favoured by management partly because most
executives are paid in stock options and restricted stock. A
buyback elevates the share price, which is the channel through
which the options pay off. A dividend doesn't move the share
price the same way. CFO is rarely indifferent.

Four — acquire another business. Highest-variance use of
capital. Done well it compounds. Done badly the academic
evidence says most large M&A destroys value for the *acquiring*
shareholder.

Five — get acquired. This one isn't on most textbook lists
because it happens *to* the company. A typical takeover premium
is 20 to 40 percent over the prior price. For the small- and
mid-cap shareholder, takeover premia are a real recurring
component of long-run return.

---

**[SEGMENT 7: WHY STOCKS ARE AN IMPERFECT INFLATION HEDGE]**

**Horace:** Stella, remember Week 1. The whole point of
investing was —

**Stella:** — to fight inflation. Cash loses purchasing power
every year, so you have to put it somewhere that grows at least
as fast as prices.

**Horace:** Right. Now we can actually connect that to the
asset we just opened up. One sentence. Cash is a fixed nominal
claim. A bond is mostly a fixed nominal promise. A stock is
ownership of a business whose nominal cash flows may grow with
the price level.

**Stella:** Why "may."

**Horace:** Because it depends on pricing power. Think about
what happens when the general price level rises. A coffee
chain's menu prices rise. A consumer-staples company's shelf
prices rise. A software vendor renews enterprise contracts at
higher numbers. The replacement cost of factories, land,
inventory, brands — all of it rises. If the business can push
those higher prices through to customers without losing them,
nominal revenue and nominal earnings rise with inflation. Every
one of the four-plus-one channels we just covered — retained
earnings, dividends, buybacks, acquisitions, being acquired —
flows through in nominally bigger numbers. You, the residual
claimant, own a slice of a nominally larger business.

**Stella:** A bondholder doesn't get that.

**Horace:** A bondholder is owed a fixed number of dollars. If
the price level doubles, the dollars still show up, but they buy
half as much. The contract is nominal. Inflation is the
bondholder's silent counterparty risk. Equity isn't a contract
for a number of dollars — it's ownership of a real business
priced in the dollars of the day. That's why Siegel's 7-percent
real number even exists. *Real* means after inflation has been
stripped out, and there's still a positive number left.

**Stella:** So stocks beat inflation.

**Horace:** In the long run, yes. But the textbook slogan
"stocks beat inflation" oversells it. The hedge is *imperfect*
and you have to know where it breaks.

One — pricing power isn't uniform. Coca-Cola can raise prices.
An airline competing on a commodity route into a global market
can't.

Two — costs inflate too. Wages, raw materials, energy. Whether
the business *captures* inflation as higher earnings or *gives
it back* as compressed margins depends on which side of the
price-cost gap re-prices faster.

Three — discount rates rise. Inflation forces central banks to
raise short rates, the long-term discount rate the market
applies to future cash flows goes up, the present-value
arithmetic gets a bigger denominator. Even if nominal earnings
rise on plan, the multiple compresses.

Four — and this is the historical part, not theory. 1966 to
1982. Burns and early Volcker. CPI running high single digits
into double digits. The S&P 500 earned its way through that
period in nominal dollars, but the P/E ratio fell from the high
teens to single digits. Real returns over the full window were
roughly flat. Stocks did not protect investors on a five-year
mark-to-market basis even though the underlying businesses were
growing nominally with the price level.

**Stella:** And recently?

**Horace:** 2022. CPI surprised to the upside, the Fed hiked
fast, the S&P fell about 18 percent nominal, and the US 60/40
portfolio had one of its worst years on record. Stocks fell
*with* bonds. That's the short-horizon case.

**Stella:** So how do I square it?

**Horace:** Two sentences. Long-term inflation hedge — yes. A
diversified basket of high-quality businesses with pricing power,
held across decades, grows nominal cash flows roughly with the
price level. Short-term CPI hedge — no. The discount-rate effect
is fast, the earnings-growth effect is slow, and equities can
lose money in real time alongside bonds when inflation
surprises. That short-horizon gap is exactly what TIPS, gold,
and commodities are for — and it's why a serious portfolio
isn't a hundred percent stocks, even for investors who fully
believe in the long-run equity number.

---

**[SEGMENT 8: MARKET CAP AND BOOK VALUE]**

**Horace:** Two numbers every investor sees. Market cap is
share price times shares outstanding — what the market currently
values the equity at. The reason this matters more than retail
investors usually realise — index inclusion rules use market cap
to decide which companies are in your basket, and at what weight.

**Stella:** So the index is biased toward the big ones.

**Horace:** And toward the *growing* ones. A company that grows
from $5 billion to $50 billion in market cap drags the index up
as it rises *and* gets a larger weight along the way. The
"passive" S&P 500 is really an actively-managed momentum
portfolio with the rules pre-specified.

**Stella:** Tiers?

**Horace:** Mega-cap above $200 billion. Large-cap $10 to 200
billion. Mid-cap $2 to 10 billion — the bench from which most
future large-caps emerge. Small-cap $300 million to $2 billion —
Russell 2000 territory, high failure rate. Micro-cap below that
— mostly noise.

**Stella:** And book value?

**Horace:** Accounting alternative. Assets minus liabilities.
Almost never equal to market cap. The *gap* tells you something.
Market cap way above book — intangibles and growth optionality
the accountant can't record. Modern tech sits here. Market cap
below book — sometimes a bargain, sometimes a value trap.

**Stella:** Can book value go negative?

**Horace:** Yes, and there are two reasons that look identical
on the balance sheet. One — aggressive long-running buybacks at
prices above book. Boeing, McDonald's, Philip Morris have all
sat at negative book at various points. Accounting artefact, not
a distress signal. Two — accumulated losses that have eaten the
equity layer. Real solvency warning. You can't tell which case
you're in from the balance sheet line. You have to read the cash
flow statement. We cover the diagnostic next week.

---

**[SEGMENT 9: ONE STOCK VS A CURATED BASKET]**

[VISUAL: image/week07_concentration_vs_diversification.png]

**Horace:** The most important slide in the lesson. Bessembinder
studied every US stock that traded between 1926 and 2016. His
finding — only four percent of stocks account for the *entire*
net wealth creation of the US stock market.

**Stella:** Four percent.

**Horace:** The other 96 percent collectively returned about
T-bills. More than half *destroyed* wealth on a buy-and-hold
basis. The median lifetime return was worse than cash.

**Stella:** And the basket fixes that?

**Horace:** A *curated* basket fixes that. The S&P 500 isn't
"the average of all stocks" — it's the top 500 by market cap
with a quarterly rebalance and an explicit rule that kicks
losers out. You're not buying the average. You're buying the
curated top of the distribution with the bottom of the
distribution removed.

**Stella:** What about narrower baskets?

**Horace:** This is the part most "just buy SPY" courses skip.
A hundred names is a different statistical animal from five
names. The Nasdaq 100 has out-returned the S&P 500 across most
multi-decade windows in the modern data, because its
construction rule biases toward right-tail innovation-heavy
names. The S&P 500 has out-returned the Russell 3000. Each is
a *differently curated* slice of the same universe and they get
*different* long-run returns.

**Stella:** So the choice isn't just "index or stock-pick".

**Horace:** Right. The honest framework is — *always hold a
basket curated by some rule*. Yours or someone else's. Could be
SPY. Could be the Nasdaq 100. Could be a sector ETF. Could be
the S&P 500 minus a sector you think is in structural decline.
Could be a hand-picked fifteen names with a written thesis on
each. What it cannot be is five favourites picked from headlines
— that's the portfolio Bessembinder chews up. "You need to be a
brilliant stock-picker to do anything other than buy SPY" is a
financial-advisor talking point, not a fact.

---

**[SEGMENT 10: PRICE VS VALUE — THE HONEST VERSION]**

**Horace:** Last big concept. Price and value converge in the
long run and diverge in the short run. The textbook does Mr.
Market. Cheerful days, gloomy days, ignore him until he's wildly
off.

**Stella:** And the honest version?

**Horace:** That metaphor is useful for one narrow purpose —
reminding you that you don't have to react to every quote. Take
it no further. The market in 2026 is not an emotional voter.
The flow you're trading against is mostly algos, dealers,
statistical-arbitrage funds, dealer hedging flows, trillions of
passive rebalancing, and retail order flow that gets
internalised by wholesalers. Information leaks — sometimes
legitimately via credit-default-swap moves before earnings,
sometimes less legitimately via insider trading the SEC catches
a few times a year. The price isn't a measurement of "the truth"
— it's the running output of that messy ecosystem.

**Stella:** So is the price meaningful or not?

**Horace:** Both. In the short run the market is noisy. In the
long run the market is a truth machine. Empirically share prices
*lead* earnings — the price often moves before the headline news
that justifies it. That's not magic. Some of the flow has
forward information you don't. Your job is to identify the small
fraction of cases where you have better information than the
flow.

**Stella:** And volatility?

**Horace:** Two cases that look identical on the chart. Case
one — macro shock, business unchanged, market briefly wrong, the
drop is a buying opportunity. Case two — stock falls 30 percent,
three months later the auditor resigns. The business *was* the
same 90 days ago — the rot was already there — but the market
saw it before you did. The price drop *was* information.

**Stella:** And in real time?

**Horace:** You usually don't know which case you're in. Both
stories are real. The discipline is — keep position sizes small
enough that any single name's 30 percent drop doesn't damage
the household. Ask whether anything fundamental has changed
before treating a drop as a bargain. And accept that sometimes
the market is ahead of you, and the right answer is to take the
loss rather than average down into a slow train wreck.

**Stella:** And the screen shows more than one data point.

**Horace:** Way more. Last trade *and* the bid-ask spread —
that's already two prices. Level-1 and level-2 books for depth.
Intraday volume. Option implied volatility across strikes —
that's a forward-looking implied distribution. Short interest.
Borrow cost. Float. Earnings calendar. Recent insider trades.
All of that is price data. A serious investor reads many lines
of it, not just the printed last-trade number.

---

**[SEGMENT 11: HOLD-OR-SELL AND COST BASIS]**

**Horace:** One last thing. Your cost basis is irrelevant to
the hold-or-sell decision. The decision to hold today is the
same decision as the decision to buy today. Would you, at this
price, choose to allocate this capital to this business? If
yes, hold. If no, sell. Your purchase price two years ago is
sunk. Cost basis matters only for tax — long-term vs
short-term gain treatment, wash-sale rules, harvesting losses.
It is never an input to the investment thesis.

**Stella:** And next week?

**Horace:** Financial statements. Income statement, balance
sheet, cash flow. The X-ray of the asset you just learned to
own. The market is reading those statements every quarter.
Reading them yourself is how you decide whether the market's
collective reading is right — or whether you're in the small
fraction of cases where you see something it has missed.
