# DeMark Indicators with Tom DeMark (Real Vision Academy) — Consolidated Study Notes

**Instructor.** Tom DeMark has been in the investment business since 1971 and is considered one of the foremost experts in the world on price exhaustion within trends — a master of market timing who has created a library of 100+ proprietary indicators, including the famous TD Sequential and TD Combo used by Paul Tudor Jones, Steve Cohen, John Burbank, Leon Cooperman, and Real Vision's own Raoul Pal. Both Cohen and Burbank eventually bought stakes in DeMark's company, and DeMark has advised Cohen daily for roughly 27 years. Unlike most technicians, DeMark built his approach inside a fast-growing institutional money manager, which forced him to design tools that anticipate tops and bottoms in advance — so institutions could buy size into weakness and sell into strength.

**What this course covers.** An introductory series in six parts: (1) a long-form career conversation between Raoul Pal and Tom DeMark, rich in market history and stories; then (2)–(6) five essential DeMark indicators taught by DeMark himself — TD Lines, TD Moving Averages, TD Absolute Retracement, and the flagship TD Sequential and TD Combo. Every indicator is presented as a set of exact, mechanical, objective rules that replace subjective chart-reading, with the goal of identifying trend exhaustion and timing market tops and bottoms. As the Real Vision hosts repeatedly stress: there is no panacea — these are tools to tilt the odds slightly in your favor, and the deeper value is that the principles make you a more patient investor who understands "the language of the market."

- The institutional constraint that shaped everything: at $4 billion, **the fund could not buy size after a bottom**. In 1972–74, 10 million shares a day was heavy volume and the market closed half-day Wednesdays for back-office accounting. So the tools had to identify bottoms **in advance**.

### 1.3 Market mechanics Tom learned early (these underpin every indicator)

- **Markets bottom not because of buyers but because of a lack of sellers.** Sentiment is so negative at a low that there are no buyers; buying in a decline is typically short covering or news-inspired. Markets peak not because of smart sellers but because of a **lack of buying**.
- Early buying off the low creates a **vacuum**: the demand gets spent before the bottom, and the market collapses on the next negative news.
- **Reversal days and key reversals are short covering.** After enormous testing, Tom concluded that a new low followed by a close up on the day (or above the prior day's high) is short covering — usually news-driven (or rumor-driven) — rallies last two to three days at most, and the vacuum beneath means the market then declines even faster.
- **Good news is associated with tops; bad news with bottoms.** Bottoms are accompanied by pervasive negative news. Good news does not make a bottom unless it is seismic (Bretton Woods, August 1971, going off the gold standard; a 9/11-type shock — and even then the effect depends on where you are in the stages of a decline; sometimes really bad news *is* the bottom). Gaps off highs and off lows are typically short covering or news selling and usually do **not** coincide with true tops and bottoms.
- **The biggest perpetration of the financial community on the investment community: measuring the market against yesterday's close.** Overnight news changes the landscape; the true reference point is **today's open**. Example: IBM closes 150, opens 160, closes 155 — headline says "up 5," but from the open it is down 5: distribution. Introduce the open plus a few variables and you get a totally different picture — this is what led Tom to measuring **buying and selling pressure** (the basis of his accumulation/distribution work).

## Part II: The DeMark Philosophy — Anticipation, Not Reaction (Lesson 2)

### 2.1 Why the indicators exist

Tom was groomed in an institutional environment at one of the fastest-growing investment companies in the country, where position size, entries, and exits were daily problems. Institutions **cannot** chase: once a market turns up you forfeit the chance to buy weakness at size, face upside gaps, and pay up for entries; on the downside you must sell into weakness. So **85–90% of the indicators he created are designed to anticipate tops and bottoms — to anticipate trend exhaustion** before it happens, enabling buy-into-weakness / sell-into-strength execution. Even the remaining 10–15% (non-anticipatory tools) can be used the same way with the right rules.

His grievances with conventional technical analysis, which he set out to mechanize:

- **Trend lines**: "Everyone seems to draw them, but they draw them incorrectly… not consistent and not effective." Subjective construction means you'll read the same chart differently tomorrow.
- **Moving averages**: "Whatever moving average worked historically was projected into the future" — curve fitting with no certainty it keeps working.
- Both contain value, which is why his adaptations (TD Lines, TD Moving Averages) are **totally objective and mechanical**.

### 2.2 Qualified vs disqualified breakouts — the mindset shift

When breakouts occur, DeMark classifies them as **qualified** or **disqualified**. A disqualified breakout to the upside is an opportunity to **sell into strength**; a disqualified breakout to the downside is an opportunity to **buy into weakness**. Change your mindset: instead of trading in the direction of a trend line break ("with the flow"), consider trading *against* it — or at minimum don't be sucked into a breakout everyone has already participated in. "If nothing else, it would preclude you from entering those trades in the direction of the market."

### 2.3 Multi-timeframe alignment

When intraday charts first appeared (1980–81, via Bunker Ramo — one-minute charts, still drawn manually), Tom was amazed that the daily/weekly patterns repeated on every timeframe. The refinement technology enabled: **identify a daily or weekly breakout or trend exhaustion and align it with higher and lower timeframes in phase/sync** — weekly in sync with the daily, then honed to your preferred intraday periods. If all timeframes work together, "your potential for success is enhanced considerably."

### 2.4 The five indicators at a glance

1. **TD Lines** — an objective, mechanical standard for trend line construction, so two people (or one person on two days) identify the same points, plus qualified/disqualified breakout tests and price projections.
2. **TD Moving Averages** — not conventional MAs: because markets trade in ranges most of the time, a conventional MA whipsaws. TD MA 1 **goes silent** in a trading range (nothing on the screen) and reinstates on a breakout. It "hugs the market on the upside at the lows and on the downside at the highs."
3. **TD Absolute Retracement** — retracements off *absolute* (all-time or properly re-initialized) highs/lows: static, permanent, consistent results. Two sibling approaches: **relative retracement** (objective anchoring of the swing high/low) and **TD Arc** (time + price, anticipates retracement breakouts).
4. **TD Sequential** — the setup/countdown exhaustion counter; speaks best in **sideways or orderly-trending** markets.
5. **TD Combo** — same setup as Sequential but a stricter countdown that begins during the setup itself; built for markets that move "perpendicular" — straight-line trends. Created because Tom was possessive of Sequential and feared wide usage would erode it.

## Part III: TD Lines (Lesson 3)

### 3.1 The problem and the construction

Created in the early 1970s out of frustration with subjective trend lines. Tom's speech gimmick proves the problem: he handed identical charts (soybeans, Treasury bonds) to 10–15 people in the front row of a Chicago Mercantile Exchange / Board of Trade audience and asked them to draw trend lines — **8 out of 10 drew totally different lines** on the same market.

**TD points (the anchors).** A market top is a **high preceded and succeeded by a lower high**; a market bottom is a **low preceded and succeeded by a higher low**. These turning points are the market's genuine supply and demand points. The most important demand point is the **most recent low surrounded by higher lows**; the most recent supply point is the **most recent high surrounded by lower highs**.

**The line.** Connect the most recent TD point to the prior most recent TD point and **extend the line into the future**; update continuously as new TD points form. Breaking the two most recent supply points (a **supply line**, DeMark's downtrend line) to the upside precedes moves higher; breaking the demand line (uptrend line) to the downside precedes declines.

**Levels (tuning the lookback).** The default anchor described above is **Level 1**: one bar on each side qualifies the point (e.g., for a supply point: a lower high the day prior and a lower high the day after the peak). For longer-duration lines, widen the window: **five bars prior and following, or more (e.g., 15)**. On a weekly chart Tom illustrates **Level 8**: a low that is lower than the prior eight lows *and* lower than the subsequent eight lows. The same breakout, qualifier, and confirmation rules apply at every level.

### 3.2 Qualified, confirmed, and disqualified breakouts

The logic (applies to trend lines, moving averages, RSI — any breakout): **a breakout has more sustenance if it is unexpected.** Traders are creatures of habit who expect trends to continue; if they've already positioned for the break, the potential buying/selling is exhausted before the break occurs.

**Qualified upside breakout** of a supply (downtrend) TD line:
1. The bar *before* the breakout closes **down** (the breakout is unexpected — shorts are skeptical and give the market leeway).
2. The breakout bar trades **above the TD line and closes above it**. The recognition tends to arrive at the close — capitulation by the wrong-sided crowd.

**Confirmed upside breakout** — the follow-through bar must:
1. **Open above** the qualified breakout bar's close (spillover of demand),
2. **Trade above the high of the prior three bars**, and
3. **Close up** on the day.

**Qualified downside breakout** of a demand (uptrend) TD line — the mirror:
1. The bar before the breakout closes **up**.
2. The breakout bar breaks below the TD line and closes below it.

**Confirmed downside breakout**: the next bar has a **down open**, trades **below the low of the prior three bars**, and **closes down** versus the qualified breakout bar's close.

A **disqualified** breakout is one that fails these tests — e.g., an upside breakout preceded by an up close (everyone already bought; no longevity), or a qualified break that reverses the next day. **Do not act on a qualified break alone — wait for confirmation.**

### 3.3 Price projections — the market's symmetry

Every breakout carries a **companion projection**, based on the market's innate symmetry: *the movement on one side of a line is often reflected on the other side.*

- **Downside break of an upsloping demand line**: measure the **extreme high above the line** (from the peak down to the trend line) and **subtract that distance from the breakout point** → downside price objective.
- **Upside break of a downsloping supply line**: measure the **lowest low beneath the line** up to the line and **add that distance to the breakout point** → upside price objective.

Worked example from the lesson (S&P daily): a series of Level-1 supply lines; a peak of approximately **440 in early March**; an upside breakout on **March 15**; taking the distance from the lowest price beneath the broken TD line up to the line and adding it to the breakout gives the upside projection. There is no certainty the projection gets hit, and a reverse breakout (e.g., the demand line then breaking down) flips your position and starts a new projection in the opposite direction.

### 3.4 The Greenspan story (1973–74)

Two institutional brokers arrived at Tom's firm with an economist carrying the then-obligatory briefcase. In the cherry-wood conference room the economist polled the room of CFAs on where interest rates were headed. Tom, staring at a crude **Wall Street Journal** chart, connected a high surrounded by two lower highs back to the previous such high, extended the line, applied his projection arithmetic, and answered **the opposite of the entire room**. Four to five months later rates reached his level; the broker called so the economist could ask how it was done — econometric models? "None of the above… a *Wall Street Journal* chart… I drew a line from the peak to the second peak… took the lowest price beneath that trend line and added it to the breakout." Both hung up dumbfounded. The economist was **Alan Greenspan**.

## Part IV: TD Moving Averages (Lesson 4)

### 4.1 Why conventional moving averages fail

Moving averages are "widely accepted, widely used, but also widely abused," with no consistency of approach. Because markets operate in a trading range **76% to 82% of the time** (Tom's long-run estimate, occasionally rounded up to 84% in speech), a conventional MA intersects price continuously — buy/sell signals whipsaw you to death in the range and leave you without a position when the real breakout finally comes.

### 4.2 TD Moving Average 1 — the self-silencing average

Use whatever lookback you like; Tom uses **13-day and 21-day** MAs. The construction is two one-sided lines:

- **Upside (bullish) line, computed on lows**: plots while the market keeps recording **lows above the prior N bars' lows** (for a 13-day MA, the prior 12–13 days' lows). When price **fails to make a qualifying new low for four consecutive price bars or more**, the line **suspends** — nothing on the screen. It **reinstates** when price again records the qualifying breakout (a low above the prior N-bar window).
- **Downside (bearish) line, computed on highs**: plots while the market keeps making **highs below the prior N bars' highs**; four consecutive bars without a qualifying new high suspends it, until a new high beyond the prior window reinstates it.

Behavior: the active line hugs the trend (the lows-based line under rallies, the highs-based line over declines). The low may occasionally intersect the lows-based line, but **closing below it requires substantial weakness** — conventional MAs would have flagged false reversals long before. When the line suspends, that is the tool telling you a trading range is likely: don't let a "trend" indicator force you out of a position in what is only consolidation.

Chart examples: **QQQ (Nasdaq 100) 2011–2012** — the blue ascending line tracked January–March 2012, suspended after four bars without a new high (correctly ignoring what would have looked like a top), reinstated as the market resumed; into the eventual peak the four-bar failure suspended it again, the market declined, and the mirror lows-line suspended when new lows stopped; the market broke down in early May and trended lower. **SPY 2019–2020** shows the same intermittent behavior. Tom's corollary: "The trend is your friend — **unless the trend is about to end**. And that's where we operate."

You may smooth the line: a **one-day average** of the N-day calculation, or — their preference — a **three-day average of the 13-day MA**.

### 4.3 TD Moving Average 2 — the dual 5/13 system

Two MAs in combination: a **5-bar and a 13-bar** average, moving in tandem — 5 above 13 in an uptrend, 5 below 13 in a downtrend. Two refinements:

- **Re-cross reinforcement**: in an uptrend, when the 5 corrects back (below the 13) while the 13 is still rising, the 5 crossing **back above** the 13 signals the uptrend is resuming. In a downtrend, the 5 rallying back above the 13 and then falling back down through it **reinforces the downtrend**.
- **Projection into the future**: project the 5-bar **three days forward** and the 13-bar **eight days forward**. This avoids much of the congestion/whipsaw of trading ranges and significantly reduces the number of (false) indications.

Tom was doing this in the early 1970s on the **Dow Jones Industrial Average with an 89-day average projected 55 days into the future** (both Fibonacci numbers) — done manually and shared in speeches; the chart services of the day lacked the software to implement it. His claim: the modern **Ichimoku "cloud"** is a takeoff on this original projected-moving-average idea. Projection gives a better fit than the conventional alignment of an MA with current price.

## Part V: TD Absolute Retracement (Lesson 5)

### 5.1 Three approaches to retracement

1. **Absolute retracement** — project off the **absolute (all-time) price high or low**, or an **intermediate level that has been properly initialized**. Results are *static, permanent, and consistent*. This is the lesson's focus.
2. **Relative retracement** — what most people attempt, usually subjectively. DeMark's mechanical version: from the current low, take the **highest price since the last time price was at or below that low**. Example: you're at 10; find the last time price was at or below 10; the highest price in between (say 20) is the anchor — use the 20→10 range. No arbitrary picking of "a high 20, 30, or 40 days ago."
3. **TD Arc** — incorporates **time and price**, anticipates bottoms (and relative-retracement breakouts) before conventional calculations flag them; it can foretell *when* as well as where. (A further tool, **Trend Factor**, was mentioned but not detailed in this series.)

Conventional retracement use is one-sided (high-to-low after the fact). Absolute retracement works **in advance** from known extremes.

### 5.2 The downside levels

From a significant/all-time **high**, the support levels are:

| Level | Calculation | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **38.2%** | High × 0.382 (= 100 − 61.8) | First support; a ratio Tom says he originated ("no one else had come up with the number until I did that easy calculation") |
| **50%** | High × 0.50 | One-half the distance between the 61.8 and 38.2 marks; major support for severe declines |
| **61.8%** | High × 0.618 (the golden mean) | Usually where **initial** support is found |
| **23.6%** | High × 0.236 | **Final** support area — "the inflection point where a company goes from operational into bankruptcy" |

Base the calculation on a **stable reference price**: the high itself, or the **high day's close**, or (if the high day gapped) the relevant gap close — whichever the day's price activity makes the stable choice. Use **daily charts** (not weekly or monthly) for the calculation.

**IPO application**: after a hot IPO tops out, the 61.8% and 38.2% levels of the high are "very high success rate" support zones. If a mid-decline rally occurs — e.g., price rallies 38.2% off a low near the 50% mark — the calculation **re-initializes**: per the improvement Tom's son **TJ** developed ~20 years ago, **any rally of 138.2% off a price level within a decline resets the projection**, and you then compute 61.8% etc. off the new (initialized) high rather than the absolute high.

### 5.3 Historical validations

- **1987**: Dow high **August 25, 1987** (with Sequential and Combo both printing 13s — one on the day, one a few weeks later). 61.8% × the high/high-day close projected the **low 1600s**; the **October 19** low close and **October 20** lower low satisfied the projection "almost precisely to the price tick."
- **1929**: projecting off the September 1929 high, the subsequent low landed at **50% of the absolute price high** — the level that has caught Bitcoin's declines several times (± a few percent) and many other markets.
- **2000 → 2002**: also a 50% retracement of the rally.
- **Gold and silver 1980**: peaks **January 20 and January 18, 1980** respectively; both found support at the **50%** level and rallied.
- Rule of thumb: 61.8% is where initial support usually appears; **without a companion indicator confirming the level, expect the market to proceed to the next level down.**

### 5.4 Upside projections and the companion-indicator rule

Off an **all-time low**, upside objectives are multiples of the absolute price itself (not high-low differences): **138.2% (100 + 38.2), 150%, 161.8%, 200%, 223.6%, 261.8%, 361.8%** — markets often recover to 161.8% of the absolute chart value. Tom finds the tool **most valuable on the downside**.

**The critical caveat**: use absolute retracement as a **companion indicator**. It attests to the *viability* of a top or bottom flagged by TD Sequential or Combo; a retracement level without a 13 speaking at the same time is vulnerable — the market tends to fall (or climb) to the *next* level.

## Part VI: TD Sequential and TD Combo (Lesson 6)

### 6.1 Origins

Two threads converge. First, the market mechanics of Part II: bottoms form when "the last seller has sold," tops when "the last buyer has bought"; tops take extended time with multiple upside thrusts before exhausting, while bottoms are typically a big move down, a rally, and a retest. Second, a formative trade: early in his career (employer's rules barred him from personally trading equities or fixed income), Tom gravitated to commodities — pre-financial futures, pre-currency futures, before listed stock options (1973–74). At a **Walston** brokerage (watching the "clackers" in the bullpen), he impulsively bought silver at **$2.60**. It went **down limit $0.10** — one trade at the next open, then again, limits widened to $0.20, and after **four down-limit days** he escaped at the bottom. Silver immediately made new highs. His resolution became the entire philosophy of Sequential: "I can't trade on impulse… I can't buy into strength. **I've got to buy into weakness — but with some justification.**"

He studied daily charts (Victoria Feed and Grain service), tested closes versus closes, tried 8-bars-versus-4 (Fibonacci), and settled on **nine and four** (comforted that 9 + 4 = 13). Back-testing found the pattern at the **1966** and **1970** lows, the **September 1929** high and **November 1929** low — 13s at every major turning point, a record "almost unblemished" in that era. "We thought we had discovered the Holy Grail."

### 6.2 Setup (identical for Sequential and Combo)

- **Buy setup**: **9 consecutive closes each less than the close four bars earlier.**
- **Sell setup**: **9 consecutive closes each greater than the close four bars earlier.**
- The setup is tracked with green numbers 1–9 on the chart.
- **Permanence**: once a 9 prints, it is **never removed**. Everything in the DeMark library is permanent — an explicit rejection of the technician's "number two pencil" habit of adjusting trend lines and averages to best-fit history.
- Note the counting convention: the setup requires *consecutive* qualifying closes; the count is by price bars (any timeframe — 1-minute to monthly).

**What a 9 means by itself.** Most often a market reaction follows the completed setup: after a **buy 9**, a turn to the upside for **at least two to three price bars**; after a **sell 9**, a sideways-to-down "hiccup" for **two to four price bars**. This happens even on a one-minute chart.

**TDST (the setup trend line).** The extreme price of the setup — the **highest price of the nine sell setup** (resistance) or the **lowest price of the nine buy setup** (support) — defines the TDST. If, after a buy 9, the upward thrust **exceeds the TDST** and the break is **qualified and confirmed**, you can be confident the buy countdown will run to 13 — so don't fade the 9. If price remains **below** the TDST, "more often than not, the decline that was there previously will resume." Symmetric logic applies after a sell 9.

### 6.3 Countdown (Sequential)

Once the 9 completes, countdown begins **on the ninth bar** (red numbers 1–13):

- **Buy countdown**: a bar qualifies when its **close is less than or equal to the low two price bars earlier**.
- **Sell countdown**: a bar qualifies when its **close is greater than or equal to the high two price bars earlier**.
- Accumulate **13** → **trend exhaustion**. A 13 does not mean instant reversal; it flags the market as stretched and vulnerable.

**Qualifier 1 (hard and fast, introduced mid-1970s).** The countdown must not be "elevated" (a **hunchback/malformed** formation — e.g., a 13 printing at $20 while countdown 8 printed at $10). For a buy countdown: **the low of countdown bar 13 must be less than (must intersect) the close of countdown bar 8.** For a sell countdown: **the high of bar 13 must be greater than the close of bar 8.** If a 13 completes without the qualifier, the chart prints an **asterisk or plus sign** showing where the 13 would have occurred — full disclosure; users may take it, "but we don't recommend it."

**Qualifier 2 (elective).** For a buy countdown: **the low of countdown bar 8 must intersect (be less than) the close of countdown bar 5**; sell side mirror (high of 8 above the close of 5). This enforces that the move is genuinely making lower lows (higher highs) rather than looping sideways.

### 6.4 Recycles and cancellation

- **Recycle**: in a persistent trend, countdown can be interrupted by a **new setup in the same direction**. If the count runs to **17 or more consecutive closes** beyond the close four bars earlier (i.e., the new 9 matures without a 13 intervening), the previous setup and its partial countdown are **discarded**; the dominant (current) nine becomes operative and countdown restarts. Recycles were rare when markets ranged (the 1970s) and are common now that trends persist — modern users must watch for them.
- **Cancellation**: if, during a buy setup/countdown, the market reverses and records a **9 in the opposite direction**, the entire buy countdown is **cancelled** and you are immediately in the opposite setup/countdown phase.

**Watching charts build is recommended**: even if you never trade intraday, following the numbers accumulate intraday is "a great way to learn how markets evolve" — nines, 13s, reactions, recycles, cancellations.

### 6.5 After the 13: three disciplines

- **Time metric (temporal)**: after a 13 (say a sell countdown 13), the market should respond — decline — **within 12 price bars** (bars, not days: could be 12 minutes or 12 weeks). If it doesn't, the market is consolidating and will probably **resume its prior trend**. The 13 has effectively failed.
- **Risk level (price)**: scan the **entire countdown period** for its **extreme price** (for a sell 13: the highest peak price of the countdown, not necessarily the bar scored 13). Take that bar's **true range** — high to low, **adding any gap** (low-to-prior-close difference) — and **add the range to the high**: that is the **risk level**, the maximum expected rally while the 13 works. (Mirror for a buy 13: lowest low of the countdown period minus that bar's true range.) Treat it as the admission point: only if price **closes above** the risk level **and follows through the next day** do you concede the 13 has failed.
- **Money management in thirds** (after a sell 13, i.e., building/holding a short): **sell (short) one third when the 13 prints; a second third if price reaches the risk level; the final third on a price flip to the downside** — a close less than the close four bars earlier, then a bar with a **down open** versus the prior close, a **low below the prior three bars' lows**, and a **close below the prior bar's close**. Most of the time the risk level is never even reached.

### 6.6 TD Combo — the stricter sibling

Combo was Tom's "**defense against wide usage of Sequential**" — created in the 1970s when big funds were successfully trading Sequential 13s (the 13s were also flagging corporate buyouts and accumulation/distribution patterns) and he feared dilution of the edge. **Sequential speaks in sideways markets** (it accumulates counts through a range and identifies exhaustion there); **Combo was built for markets moving perpendicular** — straight-line, thrusting trends.

- **Setup**: identical to Sequential (nine consecutive closes vs the close four bars earlier).
- **Countdown**: can begin **during the setup period itself** — Tom realized the first (inception) setup bar and other setup bars already possessed countdown-qualifying characteristics, so the countdown clock starts earlier and from that point forward.
- **The four countdown conditions** (sell countdown stated; buy is the mirror):
  1. **Close greater than or equal to the high two bars earlier** — the same core comparison as Sequential.
  2. **Each countdown close successively higher** — countdown 2's close above countdown 1's close, 3 above 2, … through 13. (Sequential permits sideways or down closes while accumulating; Combo does not.)
  3. **Each countdown close is an up close** versus the prior bar's close.
  4. **Each bar's high is greater than the prior bar's high.**
- Conditions 2–4 are what make Combo "finesse" — stricter, and more accurate in pinpointing the *maximum* extension of a move before exhaustion.

### 6.7 Worked examples from the lesson

- **Gold, January 1980** (the culmination of post-Bretton-Woods inflation, with the Hunt brothers squeezing silver): a 9 sell setup printed; about three trading days later Sequential-style qualifying closes began appearing — four to seven closes above the high two bars earlier, mostly up closes with one down close; then **silence for three to four days**; the breakout to the new high where **countdown 8** printed was the initiation of the final leg up; the count ran 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and the **13 printed one day before the final high and close at the end of January 1980**. Silver's 13 came one day earlier still. Both were timely notices that the market was about to top.
- **Dow Jones into January 2020**: a Combo **sell setup in December** with the countdown initiated during the setup; every countdown day had successively higher closes, above the high two bars earlier, a higher high than the prior day, and an up close — the four Combo conditions in action. The **13** printed; the **risk level** (extreme countdown high plus that bar's full range) marked "the maximum expected for the market to rally while waiting for the 13 to work on the downside." The market rallied **to the risk level, failed there, and the decline followed** (the COVID crash).
- **Dow Jones, March 2020**: a **9 buy setup**, then further downside into a **Combo 13 buy countdown** — every countdown close successively lower, each close below the prior day's close, each low below the prior day's low, each close less than the low two bars earlier. It identified **the exact day of the low**. (This is the same engine that called Bitcoin's highs and lows to the day on a five-day chart six separate times — a story from Lesson 1 — and the 2022 episode: after the January 24 low and the 61.8% rally, the setups "perfected" by February 24, were undercut by a fraction, and a Sequential 13 printed at the Monday open.)

### 6.8 The 2022 "perfection" episode (Lesson 1, applied)

A practical demonstration of combining the rules. After the **January 24** low, the S&P rallied **exactly 61.8%** off the low and stalled — precisely where the retracement math said it should. The setups then ground sideways for a month (January 24 to February 24) waiting to **perfect** — for "perfection," the low of bar 8 or 9 must undercut the lows of bars 6 and 7. Perfection arrived, the market exploded — but perfecting created **irregular upside reversals** (lows made on up closes), and since a genuine low is made on a **down close** (an up-close "low" is short covering), Tom judged in advance that **February 24 would have to be undercut**. It was — QQQ by less than one point, NDX fractionally — and at the Monday open a **Sequential 13** printed even as he watched for the stricter **Combo** ("the icing on the cake" — requiring three successively lower closes). His stated confidence in the undercut call: 98–100%.

## Part VII: Practical Guidance, Warnings, and Misreadings

- **Probable, never certain.** "No market indicator provides complete certainty; all we can ever hope for is a favorable probability and strong risk controls" (Jamie McDonald). The word to keep in mind is *probable*.
- **People misinterpret the work.** Jim Cramer credited a DeMark "buy" that was only a **Sequential 11** ("It was only 11. It didn't produce a buy… he bought it. He thought it was. A lot of people misinterpret the work"). Art Cashin publicly cited a DeMark buy signal; Tom's response: "We didn't give that. Someone interpreted it." Conversely, the week of the interview Cramer promoted the indicators on air as Sequential turned positive at Monday's open while the stricter Combo confirmation was still pending.
- **Silent is not wrong.** On Bitcoin since its streak of six exact calls, the indicator "missed a couple — but it didn't give you false signals. It just was **silent**."
- **Use the whole stack.** Absolute retracement levels need a Sequential/Combo 13 to attest viability; breakouts need qualification *and* confirmation; a 13 needs its qualifiers; multi-timeframe alignment (weekly → daily → intraday) "enhances your potential for success considerably."
- **Discipline over emotion.** Sequential and Combo "are disciplines": they force you to buy into weakness and sell into strength — the antithesis of the crowd — with defined risk levels, time metrics, and thirding rules. The mechanization is the point: whether or not you adopt the indicators, replacing subjective chart-reading with objective rules keeps you from losing money to your own moods (the closing theme, echoed from Dave Floyd's companion Real Vision course on technical trading).
- **Learn by watching.** Follow charts as they build, even intraday, to internalize how nines, 13s, recycles, cancellations, and reactions actually develop.

## Appendix A: Key Rules and Parameters

### A.1 Market context (DeMark's working assumptions)

| Claim | Value |
|---|---|
| Time in trading range | 76–82% (sometimes quoted to 84%) |
| Uptrend / downtrend share | ~12% up / ~6% down (asymmetry: buying is cumulative, selling is one decision) |
| Reference point for daily change | **Today's open**, not yesterday's close |
| True low | Made on a **down close**; a low on an up close is short covering |
| Reaction after a completed 9 setup | 2–3 bars (buy 9) / 2–4 bars (sell 9), any timeframe |
| Response window after a 13 | **12 price bars**, else expect trend resumption |

### A.2 TD Lines

| Rule | Specification |
|---|---|
| TD point (supply) | High with a lower high one bar before and one bar after (Level 1) |
| TD point (demand) | Low with a higher low one bar before and one bar after (Level 1) |
| Line construction | Connect most recent TD point to the previous TD point; extend forward; update continuously |
| Longer horizons | Widen the window: 5 bars, 8 bars, 15 bars per side (weekly charts for long term) |
| Qualified upside break | Down close on prior bar; breakout bar closes above the line |
| Confirmed upside break | Next bar: up open above qualified close; trades above prior 3 bars' highs; up close |
| Qualified/confirmed downside break | Mirror: up close prior; close below line; then down open, below prior 3 lows, down close |
| Upside projection | (Lowest low below line − line) added to breakout point |
| Downside projection | (Highest high above line − line) subtracted from breakout point |
| Action rule | Never act on qualified alone; wait for confirmation. Disqualified upside break = sell-into-strength opportunity |

### A.3 TD Moving Averages

| Variant | Rules |
|---|---|
| TD MA 1 (lows line) | Active while new lows exceed prior N bars' lows (13- or 21-day; prior 12–13 bars for 13-day); silent after 4 consecutive bars of failure; reinstates on qualifying new low. Smooth with 3-day average of the 13-day (their preference) |
| TD MA 1 (highs line) | Mirror: active while new highs are below prior N bars' highs; suspends after 4-bar failure; reinstates on breakout high |
| TD MA 2 | 5-bar and 13-bar MAs in tandem (5 above 13 = uptrend; below = downtrend); re-cross of 5 back through 13 reinforces trend resumption; project 5-bar 3 days and 13-bar 8 days into the future to cut whipsaw |
| Historical variant | 89-day DJIA average projected 55 days forward (early 1970s); Ichimoku described as a later takeoff of projected MAs |

### A.4 TD Absolute Retracement

| Context | Levels |
|---|---|
| Downside off an all-time/significant high | 38.2%, 50%, 61.8% (initial support), 23.6% (terminal/bankruptcy zone) |
| Upside off an all-time low | 138.2%, 150%, 161.8%, 200%, 223.6%, 261.8%, 361.8% of the absolute price |
| Anchoring price | The high/low itself, or the high/low day's close, or gap close — the stable reference |
| Chart timeframe | Daily (not weekly/monthly) |
| Re-initialization (TJ's rule) | A rally of 38.2% off a mid-decline low, formally **138.2% off a price level**, resets the projection off the new high |
| Companion rule | No confirming 13 at the level → expect the move to extend to the next level |
| Relative retracement | From the current low, anchor = highest price since price was last at/below that low |
| TD Arc | Time + price; anticipates bottoms/retracement breakouts before conventional tools |

### A.5 TD Sequential

| Phase | Buy side | Sell side |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | 9 consecutive closes **< close 4 bars earlier**; permanent on chart | 9 consecutive closes **> close 4 bars earlier** |
| TDST | Lowest low of the buy setup = support | Highest high of the sell setup = resistance |
| Countdown (starts on bar 9) | Close ≤ **low 2 bars earlier** → 1…13 | Close ≥ **high 2 bars earlier** → 1…13 |
| Qualifier 1 (mandatory) | Low of bar 13 < close of bar 8 (else asterisk/plus shown; not recommended) | High of bar 13 > close of bar 8 |
| Qualifier 2 (elective) | Low of bar 8 < close of bar 5 | High of bar 8 > close of bar 5 |
| Recycle | New same-direction 9 during countdown; at **17+** consecutive qualifying closes, old setup/countdown discarded | Same |
| Cancellation | Opposite 9 setup cancels countdown entirely | Same |
| Risk level | Lowest low of countdown period − that bar's true range (incl. gap) | Highest high of countdown period + that bar's true range (incl. gap); fail only on close beyond + next-day follow-through |
| Exit thirds (after sell 13) | — | 1/3 at the 13; 1/3 at the risk level; 1/3 on the downside price flip (close < close 4 bars earlier; then down open, low < prior 3 lows, close < prior close) |

### A.6 TD Combo

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Setup | Identical to Sequential |
| Countdown start | May begin **during the setup** (from the inception bar onward) |
| Countdown conditions (sell side; buy mirrored) | (1) close ≥ high 2 bars earlier; (2) each countdown close successively higher (1→13); (3) each countdown close an up close vs prior bar; (4) each high above the prior bar's high |
| Best environment | Straight-line, "perpendicular" trending markets (Sequential best in ranges) |
