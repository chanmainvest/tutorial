**HOSTS:**
- **Horace** (teacher): Walks through the structure and the data.
- **Stella** (student): Asks the questions retail actually asks.

**[INTRO]**

[VISUAL: Animated logo "Side Lesson 13 — IPOs and SPACs"]

**Horace:** Stella, you're scrolling X on a Tuesday morning and a
trending name says "RoboNova IPO opens for trading at 10:30 AM
ET, priced last night at $20." You have ten minutes. What do you
do?

**Stella:** I open my brokerage app, place a market buy when it
opens, and pray.

**Horace:** Right. And that — that exact behaviour — is the trade
that the IPO machine is engineered to extract from. Today we are
going to look at what actually happens between the time RoboNova
prices at $20 and the time you place that order. Then we are going
to look at the data on what happens to people who place that order.
Spoiler: the average outcome is negative. The median is worse.

---

**[SEGMENT 1: WHO GETS THE POP]**

[VISUAL: image/side13_ipo_first_year.png]

**Horace:** This chart is the average first-year price path for US
IPOs from the 2010-2024 cohort, normalised to the offer price equal
to one. The deal prices at one. By the time it opens for public
trading — which is what *you* see on your screen — it is at one
point one eight. That eighteen percent is the average first-day pop.

**Stella:** Eighteen percent in one morning?

**Horace:** In about an hour, between when the deal prices and when
the bell rings. And that eighteen percent goes almost entirely to
the institutional clients the underwriter allocated shares to the
night before. The book-building roadshow, the pricing meeting, the
whisper number — all of that exists to identify which favoured
clients will get the gift.

**Stella:** And retail?

**Horace:** Retail buys at the open. Retail buys at the one-point-
one-eight, not at the one. Look at the rest of this chart: the line
drifts up modestly into month two, sags through the summer as the
ninety-day lock-up clears for direct-listing names, falls harder
into months four through six as the 180-day lock-up flood hits the
traditional IPOs and the first earnings disappointment lands, and
finishes month twelve at zero point nine zero of offer.

**Stella:** So the allocated investor is up ten percent — bought at
one, sold at point nine zero, plus they had the eighteen point
pop earlier.

**Horace:** Allocated investor finishes year one at the offer
price's roughly negative ten percent, but they bought at the offer
price. Their P&L is the gap between point nine zero and one — minus
ten percent of capital — *plus* the value of any shares they sold
into the day-one pop. Most of them are still net positive after the
trade. The retail buyer at the open bought at one-point-one-eight.
At point nine zero terminal, retail is down roughly twenty-four
percent against a market that did roughly plus ten over the same
window.

**Stella:** Wait, twenty-four percent?

**Horace:** Approximately. Point nine zero divided by one point one
eight is roughly point seven six — a twenty-four percent loss off
your purchase price.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: WHY THE POP EXISTS]**

[VISUAL: Title card "Allocation as Currency"]

**Horace:** The pop is not a bug. It is a feature. The underwriter
deliberately prices the deal twenty percent below where the order
book would clear. They do this because they want to give favoured
clients an instant paper gain.

**Stella:** Why would the company let them do that?

**Horace:** Because the company wants its institutional shareholder
base to include long-term holders rather than flippers, and the
underwriter is the gatekeeper to that distribution. The company
leaves money on the table — billions, on the biggest deals — in
exchange for the underwriter's assurance that the book is full of
buy-and-hold institutions. Whether that assurance is worth the
discount is a separate debate; it is why direct listings exist.

**Stella:** Why don't more companies do direct listings then?

**Horace:** Two reasons. One: direct listings only work for very
well-known brands — Spotify, Slack, Palantir, Coinbase — that don't
need a bank to drive demand. A no-name biotech cannot do a direct
listing because nobody would show up to the auction. Two: the
underwriting bank's relationship is worth more than the discount
to the founders, who are getting access to research coverage,
trading services, and future M&A advice. The ecosystem
self-perpetuates.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: THE ONE-YEAR DRAG]**

[VISUAL: line returning to the first-year chart, focusing on
months 6-12.]

**Horace:** The retail buyer at the open is fighting four
structural headwinds across the first year:

**Stella:** Lay them out.

**Horace:** One. Window-shopping — companies time IPOs when their
sector is hot and their numbers look great. Both conditions
mean-revert. Two. Lock-up overhang — insiders cannot sell for ninety
to one-eighty days, but the market knows the supply is coming.
Three. Sell-side initiation — every analyst at the underwriting
bank is required to wait twenty-five days, then they all write
"buy" reports. The first non-buy from a major bank is often the
moment the stock breaks. Four. The first earnings cycle as a
public company — the gap between the roadshow numbers and the
first 10-Q is where reality lands.

**Stella:** And this shows up in the index data?

**Horace:** Renaissance IPO ETF, ticker IPO, has compounded at
roughly nine percent a year since September 2013. VTI over the
same window did roughly fourteen percent. That's a five-percent-per-
year drag on a *systematic, diversified* IPO strategy. Hand-picked
retail IPO portfolios are statistically worse, because selection
bias pushes the picks toward the most-exciting-and-therefore-most-
overpriced names.

**Stella:** And the trade that *feels* contrarian — buying the
shiny new name on day one — is actually the most crowded trade
in the market that morning.

**Horace:** Right. The real contrarian trade is the opposite end of
the lifecycle: the index deletion, the sector the model portfolios
just dumped, the unloved compounder. That is where the passive
machinery overshoots and leaves a mispricing on the floor. Day-one
IPO buying is the maximally engineered trade dressed in contrarian
clothing.

**Stella:** And on size?

**Horace:** Most IPOs price below a billion dollars in market cap.
Many of the rest are unseasoned even when the headline cap is
bigger. I do not buy sub-billion companies on the open exchange —
wide spreads, manipulable float, thin disclosures, the dominant
participants are not investors. That is the casino tier. The rule
does not care whether the casino tier arrives as an OTC ticker or
as a hot IPO with a Goldman roadshow. Same number, same rule.

**[SEGMENT 4: SPACS]**

[VISUAL: image/side13_spac_blowups.png]

**Horace:** Stella, look at this distribution. This is the
twelve-month return after the deSPAC merger for the 2021 SPAC
cohort. Median negative fifty percent. Top quartile negative
twenty-five. Bottom quartile negative eighty-five percent.

**Stella:** That's not even a market. That's a slaughterhouse.

**Horace:** Six hundred and thirteen SPACs went public in 2021
versus two hundred and forty-eight traditional IPOs. So this is
the dominant flow of the 2020-2021 vintage, not a corner case.
And the structure produces the result.

**Stella:** What's the structure?

**Horace:** Four pieces. One: the sponsor promote — twenty percent
of the post-IPO equity, paid for with nominal capital. That dilution
hits the public shareholder on merger day. Two: redemptions — by
the time the merger vote happens, sophisticated investors have
pulled their ten dollars back out and kept the warrants. The cash
the SPAC originally raised is sixty to ninety percent gone. Three:
the PIPE — Private Investment in Public Equity, priced at eight or
nine dollars per share to backfill the redemptions, further diluting
the public common. Four: forecasts. SPACs can include forward
projections in the proxy statement, which traditional IPOs cannot.
This produced unprecedented fantasy projections in 2020-2021 —
pre-revenue companies projecting hundreds of millions in EBITDA
five years out.

**Stella:** Who wins?

**Horace:** SPAC arbitrageurs — hedge funds who buy at ten dollars,
redeem at ten plus T-bill interest, and keep the warrants for free.
Their trade is essentially risk-free. The losing side is the retail
investor who buys the post-merger common at fifteen dollars in the
secondary market. That is the bag.

---

**[SEGMENT 5: DIRECT LISTINGS]**

[VISUAL: Title card with five tickers: SPOT, WORK, PLTR, COIN, RBLX]

**Horace:** Direct listings — Spotify in 2018, Slack 2019, Palantir
2020, Coinbase and Roblox 2021. No underwriter buying the deal. No
allocated insiders. The exchange runs an opening auction; the first
trade is whatever the order book clears at.

**Stella:** Better outcomes?

**Horace:** Mixed. The mechanism eliminates the eighteen-percent
gift to allocated clients. But it does not change the *evaluation*
problem. Spotify was down thirty-four percent within nine months of
listing, then recovered. Slack got bought at thirty percent below
its listing price. Coinbase was down eighty percent at the bottom of
crypto winter. Palantir is the one runaway winner.

**Stella:** So even the better mechanism doesn't beat the base rate.

**Horace:** Right. The mechanism makes the deal fairer. It does
not make the company a good investment.

**[SEGMENT 6: THE PLAYBOOK]**

[VISUAL: cut to interactive/side13_ipo_lab.html in the browser.]

**Horace:** Three rules. Rule one: wait six months. Let the lock-up
expire. Let one earnings cycle land. The expected return from the
first-day open to the six-month mark is essentially zero — you are
not giving up upside by waiting; you are skipping the noisiest
window.

**Stella:** Rule two?

**Horace:** Evaluate the stock the way you evaluate any other
stock. Read the 10-Q. Run the Week 21 DCF. If the metrics work and
the price embeds a reasonable growth path, buy it. If the price
still embeds the IPO-day excitement, pass.

**Stella:** Rule three?

**Horace:** If you absolutely cannot help yourself and you must
play the IPO window — size it like a lottery ticket. One to two
percent of the portfolio per name, max. Tranche four, opportunistic
sleeve. Never on margin. Never replacing your indexed core.

*(types into the lab: pop = +18%, lock-up drift = -10%, horizon =
12 months, capital = $10,000)*

**Horace:** Look at the two columns. Allocated investor finishes
at $10,620. Retail day-one investor finishes at $9,000. That gap
— sixteen hundred dollars on ten thousand — is exactly the spread
between buying at one and buying at one point one eight, and
holding through the same drag.

**Stella:** And the rational-versus-irrational point?

**Horace:** Right. The rational version of you reads the S-1 and
waits six months. The irrational version of you sees the X post,
opens the brokerage app, and pays the eighteen-percent pop to
favoured strangers. The whole point of having a written process is
that the rational version is the one who places the orders.

**[OUTRO]**

[VISUAL: Summary card with three bullets:
- Day-one buyers pay the +18% pop
- Average first-year IPO drag: -10% vs market
- SPAC 2021 cohort median: -50% post-merger]

**Horace:** Three takeaways. One: the eighteen-percent first-day
pop is a coupon paid by the company to allocated institutional
clients. Retail buying at the open pays the post-pop price. Two:
across pooled US data, the average first-year IPO buyer
underperforms the market by about ten points; the Renaissance IPO
ETF shows the same drag systematically. Three: SPACs are
structurally worse — sponsor promote, redemption, dilution, fantasy
forecasts — and the 2021 cohort's median twelve-month post-merger
return was around minus fifty percent.

**Stella:** So the playbook is wait six months, read one 10-Q, then
treat it like any other stock.

**Horace:** That is the playbook. The IPO window is for traders,
allocators, and sponsors. The post-IPO market is for investors.
Pick which side you are on.
