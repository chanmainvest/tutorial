**[INTRO — 0:00]**

**Stella:** Welcome back to the show. This is Week Six of the
chanmainvest tutorial, and today we're doing the chapter that
every investing course either skips entirely or gets badly wrong:
gold.

**Horace:** And the reason most courses get it wrong is the same
reason most investors get it wrong. They argue about gold like
it's a stock. It is not a stock.

**Stella:** Right. So let's set the frame. Five questions for
this episode. One — what *is* gold, given that humans have been
calling it money for five thousand years? Two — when has the
inflation-hedge story actually held up, and when has it failed?
Three — who's been buying it for the last fifteen years and why
does that matter? Four — what's the right toolkit, and what's
the difference between an ETF, a futures contract, and a coin?
Five — how much should you actually own?

**Horace:** And the surprise twist for anyone coming in from a
goldbug background or a Bogleheads background is the same
answer: neither extreme is right. Gold is not the only honest
money. Gold is also not a useless rock. It's a specific kind of
asset with specific properties, and once you understand them,
the allocation question answers itself.

**[BLOCK A — Five thousand years of gold as money — 1:30]**

**Stella:** Let's start at the beginning. Why do we even *call*
gold money?

**Horace:** Because the experiment has been running, more or
less continuously, since the Bronze Age. Gold rings circulated as
units of account in Egypt and Mesopotamia in 2500 BC. The first
true coins were the Lydian electrum coins, struck around 600 BC
under King Croesus — go to the British Museum, you can see
them. The Roman aureus anchored Roman commerce for three
centuries. Florence's *fiorino d'oro* was the reserve currency
of Renaissance Europe. Newton, as Master of the Royal Mint in
1717, set the gold-silver ratio that put Britain on a de facto
gold standard for two hundred years. Bretton Woods in 1944 was
just the latest version of the same idea — dollar pegged to gold
at thirty-five an ounce.

**Stella:** And then 1971.

**Horace:** Fifteenth of August 1971, Nixon closes the gold
window. For the first time in five thousand years, the global
monetary system has *no* metallic anchor. Pure fiat. We're
fifty-five years into that experiment now.

**Stella:** Fifty-five years feels like a long time.

**Horace:** It does until you realize that every previous fiat
regime in history ended in either restoration of metallic
backing or hyperinflation. That doesn't predict our future.
But it should make anyone who treats fiat money as the natural
state of the world a little less confident.

**Stella:** And the J.P. Morgan quote.

**Horace:** Pujo Committee, December 19th, 1912. Sworn testimony
to the House Banking and Currency Committee. He was asked
whether the basis of credit is money or property. He said —
and this is verbatim — "Gold is money. Everything else is
credit." That's the framing for this episode.

**[BLOCK B — Belief, no cashflow, and the modern wrinkles — 4:00]**

**Stella:** OK so what is gold, the asset?

**Horace:** A store of value priced by belief. It produces
essentially no cashflow. The price is whatever the next
marginal buyer pays.

**Stella:** That sounds dismissive.

**Horace:** It's not. The same sentence describes the US
dollar. A hundred-dollar bill produces no cashflow. Its value is
whatever the next person down the chain accepts it for. It's
priced by belief. The only difference between gold and the
dollar is *how old the belief is*. Gold's been money for five
thousand years. The pure-fiat dollar in its current form — post
1971 — is younger than the Beatles. Bitcoin is younger than the
iPhone.

**Stella:** Voltaire had a line about this.

**Horace:** "Paper money eventually returns to its intrinsic
value — zero." 1729. He was talking about the John Law assignats
in France, which were about to collapse. Every store of value
rests on belief. The honest question is durability.

**Stella:** And one important caveat — durability of belief is
not the same as guaranteed return.

**Horace:** Right. Gold's belief is robust. That doesn't promise
the next ten years look like the 1970s. Goldbugs who assume
they'll get the 1971-to-1980 return again because "gold always
wins in inflation" are extrapolating one regime onto another.
Treat the historical record as a constraint on what's plausible,
not as a forecast.

**Stella:** OK so if gold has no cashflow, what does that mean
for returns?

**Horace:** It means gold cannot compound the way equities do.
Stocks deliver about seven percent real per year over a century.
Gold delivers about one and a half percent real over the same
window. It's *money that holds its value*. It is not capital
that grows.

**Stella:** Quick clarification — you said *essentially* no
cashflow. There's a wrinkle.

**Horace:** Two wrinkles, both modern. First — gold can be lent.
The London bullion market publishes the Gold Forward Offered
Rate, GOFO, and the implied lease rate is the difference
between SOFR and GOFO. HSBC, JPMorgan, UBS — the bullion banks
borrow allocated bullion from large holders to satisfy
short-term delivery and refining demand. They pay a lease rate.
Usually small, point-one to two percent. Briefly went over five
percent during the COVID delivery chaos in early 2020. So gold
*does* have a small yield in the wholesale market.

**Stella:** Can a retail investor get that?

**Horace:** No, not directly. You need to be holding tonnes in
an LBMA-recognised vault. And the major retail gold ETFs —
GLD, IAU, GLDM, PHYS — explicitly *prohibit* lending in their
prospectuses. That's a feature, not a bug.

**Stella:** Why a feature?

**Horace:** Because the lease rate is paid by the bullion bank,
and if the bullion bank fails, your "yield-enhanced gold" is
an unsecured credit claim, not a bar. We'll come back to this
in the paper-versus-physical block.

**Stella:** What's the second wrinkle?

**Horace:** Basel III. June 2021 in the EU, January 2022 in the
UK. The Net Stable Funding Ratio rules came into force, and
they distinguish between allocated physical gold — which counts
as a high-quality liquid asset for a bank — and unallocated
paper gold, which carries an eighty-five percent required stable
funding charge. Translation: the cost of running the
unallocated paper-gold market went up materially. Several
analysts — Alasdair Macleod at Goldmoney, Ronan Manly at
BullionStar — argue this is one of the structural reasons gold
held up better than the real-rate model alone would predict in
2022 to 2026. Whether you buy that thesis or not, the regulatory
shift is real.

**[BLOCK C — When the inflation hedge works — 7:30]**

**Stella:** Bring up the real gold price chart with the macro
panel underneath.

**[VISUAL: image/week06_gold_real.png]**

**Horace:** Top panel — real gold price from 1971 through April
2026, all in 2026 dollars. Bottom panel — headline CPI year-
over-year and the real ten-year Treasury yield, with annual
gold returns shown as bars.

**Stella:** Three peaks pop out in the top panel.

**Horace:** Three real peaks at roughly the same level. 1980,
2011, and 2024 to 2026. All three sit between twenty-two and
twenty-five hundred in 2026 dollars. That repetition is
striking. One interpretation is there's a marginal-demand
ceiling at which gold gets expensive enough relative to other
inflation hedges that the buyer steps away. That's *my*
interpretation — three data points, not a proven law.

**Stella:** Long flat stretches in between.

**Horace:** Lost decades. 1980 to 1999 — real gold price down
almost eighty percent. 2012 to 2018 — down forty. Holding gold
through those windows tested every goldbug's faith.

**Stella:** Now the bottom panel — the real-rate relationship.

**Horace:** One sentence: gold rises when real interest rates
fall. Real rates down, gold up. Real rates up, gold down. The
mechanism is opportunity cost — if I can earn two percent real
on a Treasury, the no-yield asset is expensive. If I can only
earn negative one percent real, gold's zero yield suddenly looks
competitive.

**Stella:** And the three peaks line up with that.

**Horace:** Each one. 1971-80 — real rates collapsing into
stagflation. 2001-11 — Greenspan and then Bernanke pinning real
rates near zero. 2019-26 — COVID emergency easing.

**Stella:** But 2022 to 2026 doesn't quite fit.

**Horace:** It's the cleanest counter-example. Real ten-year
yields rose from negative one percent up to over plus two —
textbook setup for gold to fall. Gold rose anyway, about seventy
percent in real terms.

**Stella:** What broke the model?

**Horace:** Three things. Record central bank buying — we'll
cover that in the next block. Geopolitical reserve
diversification after the freezing of Russian FX reserves in
February 2022. And US fiscal deficits running near seven percent
of GDP outside of recession. Ray Dalio wrote an essay on
LinkedIn in 2024 — *Why I Like Gold More Than Bonds Now*. His
framing is gold is "non-credit, non-fiat money," and demand for
it rises when the credit-money system *and* the geopolitical
system are both under stress. Even if real rates are also rising.

**Stella:** Real rates are the main variable, not the only
variable.

**Horace:** Right. Don't be a one-factor investor.

**[BLOCK D — Regime by regime — 11:00]**

**Stella:** Bring up the regime chart.

**[VISUAL: image/week06_gold_vs_stocks.png]**

**Horace:** This is a different approach than the one cumulative
line you usually see. We've split the half-century into five
regimes and started every line at one dollar at the beginning of
each regime. So you can see the *shape* of returns inside each
window. Four lines per panel — S&P 500 in red, gold in gold,
sixty-forty stock-Treasury in blue, and a sixty-twenty-five-
fifteen blend — that's sixty percent stocks, twenty-five percent
Treasuries, fifteen percent gold — in green.

**Stella:** Walk me through the panels.

**Horace:** 1971 to 1980 — stagflation. Gold up about sixteen
times in nominal terms, five times in real. S&P essentially
flat in real terms. Sixty-forty actually *lost* real value. The
sixty-twenty-five-fifteen ended ahead of sixty-forty by a wide
margin. The whole gain was in the gold sleeve. *This* is the
regime gold is paid for.

**Stella:** 1981 to 1999.

**Horace:** Volcker disinflation and the great bull market. S&P
compounding at roughly fourteen percent real per year. Gold
loses eighty percent in real terms. Sixty-forty runs neck-and-
neck with equities at lower vol. The sixty-twenty-five-fifteen
*underperforms* sixty-forty by the gold drag — about eighty to
a hundred basis points a year. *This* is the regime gold makes
you pay.

**Stella:** 2000 to 2011.

**Horace:** Lost decade for stocks. S&P roughly flat for the
window. Gold up fifteen percent compounded. Sixty-forty beats
one-hundred-percent equities. Sixty-twenty-five-fifteen beats
sixty-forty.

**Stella:** 2012 to 2019.

**Horace:** Stocks run, gold flat to down, sixty-forty comes
back. Sixty-twenty-five-fifteen lags sixty-forty modestly.

**Stella:** 2020 to 2026.

**Horace:** Stocks roughly double, gold roughly doubles, sixty-
forty has its worst year on record in 2022. The
sixty-twenty-five-fifteen has a noticeably softer 2022 because
gold rose while stocks and bonds both fell. That's the regime
in which a permanent gold sleeve earns its keep.

**Stella:** Full-period summary.

**Horace:** S&P has the highest end-value by a meaningful
margin. The blended sixty-twenty-five-fifteen has a *higher
Sharpe ratio* than either sixty-forty or one-hundred-percent
S&P, with a much shallower max drawdown. The price for the
smoother ride is ending with somewhat less wealth than pure
equities. Most investors should accept that trade.

**Stella:** That sounds like a free lunch.

**Horace:** It's not. It's a *realized diversification benefit*
on the specific 1971-to-2026 sample. There's no guarantee future
regimes deliver the same benefit. The case for the gold sleeve
is "robust across many historical regimes," not "guaranteed in
any future one." Harry Browne built the Permanent Portfolio in
1981 on this kind of reasoning. Ray Dalio refined it as
Bridgewater All Weather in 1996. Christopher Cole at Artemis
restated it again in 2020 with the Dragon Portfolio paper. Three
serious investors, three different frameworks, all converging
on the same idea — gold and equities are different exposures
that complement each other across regimes.

**[BLOCK E — Central banks since 2010 — 13:30]**

**Stella:** Show the central bank chart.

**[VISUAL: image/week06_central_bank_gold.png]**

**Horace:** This is annual central bank net gold purchases from
1971 through 2025, in tonnes. Bars below zero are net selling.
Bars above zero are net buying. Notice the regime change at
2010.

**Stella:** Brown's Bottom is in there.

**Horace:** 1999, Gordon Brown sells about half of UK gold
reserves at two-eighty-two an ounce. The textbook example of
the official sector selling at the lows. They were a net seller
every year from 1990 through 2009. That regime ended in 2010 —
every year since, the official sector has been a net *buyer*.

**Stella:** And then the 2022 spike.

**Horace:** 2022 — one thousand and eighty-two tonnes. Largest
annual purchase since 1950. The trigger was the freezing of
three hundred billion of Russian central bank dollar reserves
after the invasion of Ukraine. Every non-aligned central bank
suddenly had a vivid reason to want a reserve asset that no one
else can switch off.

**Stella:** And it didn't stop.

**Horace:** 2023 — one thousand and thirty-seven tonnes. 2024 —
similar. 2025 still tracking above nine hundred tonnes. The
PBoC has reported gold reserve increases for eighteen
consecutive months through mid-2025. For context, the entire
annual newly-mined gold supply is about thirty-five hundred
tonnes. Central banks alone are absorbing twenty-five to thirty
percent of new mine supply.

**Stella:** And how does this change the strategic picture?

**Horace:** The 2010s gold cycle was driven by Western retail
and ETF flows, and it exhausted itself when real rates rose in
2013. The 2020s gold cycle is driven by *non-Western central
banks* who do not care about real rates because their
alternative — holding more dollars and Treasuries — was
demonstrated in 2022 to carry political risk they hadn't
previously priced. That marginal buyer doesn't switch off when
real rates rise. Might slow down. Unlikely to flip back to net
seller.

**[BLOCK F — Silver, platinum, palladium — 15:00]**

**Stella:** Bring up the gold-silver ratio chart.

**[VISUAL: image/week06_gold_silver_ratio.png]**

**Horace:** Quick word on the metals that aren't gold. Gold's
purity as a store of value comes from a property the others
lack — gold has essentially *no* industrial demand. Less than
ten percent of annual flow goes to industry. Everything else is
jewellery, investment, and central bank reserves. Monetary or
quasi-monetary demand. That's why gold tracks belief and real
rates rather than the manufacturing PMI.

**Stella:** Silver's different.

**Horace:** Silver was as much "money" as gold from the late
Roman Empire through the nineteenth century. Most of the world
ran a *bimetallic* standard. Two political events ended that —
Newton's 1717 mint ratio in Britain and the US Coinage Act of
1873, the "Crime of 73," which demonetised silver. William
Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech in 1896 was the last
serious political fight to bring it back. He lost. Industrial
demand has carried silver's price ever since — today about
fifty-five percent of silver demand is industrial.

**Stella:** Solar PV?

**Horace:** Solar PV is now the single largest industrial
silver demand category. Electronics, brazing alloys, jewellery,
silverware and coins. The chart on screen is the gold-silver
ratio — ounces of silver to buy one ounce of gold. Roman
through 19th century, it sat in a twelve-to-one to sixteen-to-
one band, roughly the ratio of crustal abundance. Modern era,
it has ranged from seventeen-to-one at the 1980 Hunt brothers'
silver squeeze to over a hundred and twenty-five-to-one at the
COVID March 2020 spike. Currently around eighty-to-one in
April 2026.

**Stella:** Practical takeaway?

**Horace:** Two things. The ratio mean-reverts but slowly and
noisily — silver bulls treat hundred-plus extremes as a signal
to overweight silver versus gold, and historically those
extremes do work off, but the wait can be years. And for a
portfolio diversifier, gold is the cleaner tool. Silver has
more torque for a tactical industrial-cycle bet.

**Stella:** Platinum and palladium?

**Horace:** Roughly eighty percent industrial. Autocatalysts —
palladium for petrol vehicles, platinum for diesel and
hydrogen. Their prices track auto-production cycles, emissions
regulation, and South African and Russian mine supply. They are
*not* stores of value in any meaningful sense. Palladium fell
sixty-five percent in nine months in 2023 when the EV-driven
de-rating finally caught up with the price. Treat them as
specialty industrial-cycle bets, not gold substitutes.

**Stella:** When the lesson talks about a permanent metals
sleeve —

**Horace:** It means gold. The others can have a place in a
tactical book, not in the permanent one.

**[BLOCK G — A note on industrial commodities — 16:30]**

**Stella:** Quick word on industrial commodities — oil, copper,
wheat — because the lesson title used to mention them.

**Horace:** Different asset class. Don't put them in the same
mental bucket as gold. Three reasons. They mean-revert toward
the marginal cost of supply. Their real long-run trends are
*down* — Jeremy Grantham at GMO has the standard research
papers on this. And they correlate with the business cycle —
owning a basket is roughly a leveraged short on the dollar
plus a leveraged long on global GDP. It's a factor exposure,
not a diversifier.

**Stella:** And the futures-ETF problem.

**Horace:** The contango trap, the USO case study, the whole
roll-yield mechanic — that's a separate lesson. Week 39
covers it in detail. For *this* lesson the takeaway is just
that physical-bullion gold ETFs do not share that problem,
and that's the specific reason gold is investable as a
passive long-term holding while most other commodities are
not.

**[BLOCK H — Bullion ETFs vs futures vs coins — 17:30]**

**Stella:** OK toolkit. For a US investor, what should they
actually buy?

**Horace:** Three names worth knowing. GLDM at ten basis
points is the cheapest US physical-gold ETF. GLD at forty is
the most liquid with the deepest options market. IAU at
twenty-five sits between them. SGOL at seventeen if you want
Swiss vault custody, BAR at eighteen, IAUM at nine. They all
hold allocated bullion in vaults.

**Stella:** Canadian listings?

**Horace:** Worth flagging. Sprott Physical Gold Trust — ticker
PHYS, dual-listed NYSE Arca and TSX, allocated bullion,
*physical redemption available* in four-hundred-ounce bars.
Royal Canadian Mint Gold ETR, ticker MNT on the TSX, receipts
backed one-to-one by allocated bullion in RCM vaults, also
physical redemption. Both of these matter for two reasons —
the redemption right is the strongest possible form of "I
really own this gold," and both qualify for a US tax
workaround we'll get to in a moment.

**Stella:** What about gold futures?

**Horace:** Different exposure. GLD owns physical bullion in a
vault — no roll cost, no contango, only drag is the management
fee. Gold futures — slash GC at CME, MGC for the micro
contract — are dated. You hold them, then close, deliver, or
roll into the next contract every two months. Gold's futures
curve is normally in mild contango — cost of carry, storage
plus insurance plus financing — so the roll costs you something.
On gold it's modest, one to three percent annualised in normal
regimes, occasionally higher. Far smaller than the five to ten
percent annual contango drag on oil.

**Stella:** Why use futures at all?

**Horace:** Two reasons. Leverage — a slash GC contract has a
notional of about two-hundred-and-forty-thousand dollars but
only requires about eleven thousand in margin. And Section 1256
tax treatment — sixty percent long-term, forty percent short-
term capital gains regardless of holding period, marked to
market at year-end. For a buy-and-hold sleeve, a bullion ETF
wins. For a tactical or leveraged position, futures or options
on GLD are better.

**Stella:** Miners?

**Horace:** GDX is leveraged gold with operational and country
risk. Underperformed GLD across most multi-decade windows since
inception. Use it tactically if you want a high-conviction view
on gold moving fast. Don't think of it as the same exposure as
bullion.

**Stella:** Coins?

**Horace:** American Gold Eagle, Canadian Maple Leaf,
Krugerrand, Philharmonic. Three to six percent premium to spot,
plus storage and insurance, plus one-to-three percent bid-ask on
resale. The all-in friction wipes out the GLDM expense advantage
over any horizon under ten or fifteen years. Physical is the
right answer *only* for off-grid jurisdictional risk or estate
planning across multiple legal systems.

**[BLOCK I — Paper vs physical, allocated vs unallocated — 19:30]**

**Stella:** Talk about paper gold versus physical gold.

**Horace:** Three custody models in the bullion market.
Allocated and segregated — specific bars with serial numbers
belong to you, named LBMA vault, audited annually. You have a
*property right* to the metal. If the custodian fails, the bars
don't enter the bankruptcy estate. That's what GLD, IAU, GLDM,
PHYS, MNT all use. Allocated but pooled — your weight is yours
but not specific bars. Slightly weaker, generally fine for
retail. Unallocated — you have a *book-entry credit* on a
bullion bank's balance sheet. You're an unsecured creditor of
the bank. If the bank fails, your "gold" is a bankruptcy claim,
not a bar. Most over-the-counter gold trades unallocated. It's
what makes the wholesale market liquid. It is *not* the same
product as owning a bar.

**Stella:** When picking a gold ETF, what should I look for?

**Horace:** Three checks. One — custody form, allocated and
segregated only. Two — does the trust permit lending out the
bullion? GLD, IAU, GLDM, SGOL, PHYS, MNT all explicitly
prohibit it. Some smaller "yield-enhanced" gold products do
lend, and the yield comes from counterparty exposure to bullion
banks. There's no large mainstream US ETF that lends its gold
en masse — the lending risk lives more in unallocated wholesale
products and small-issuer yield-enhanced structures aimed at
hedge funds. Three — audit cadence and auditor. Annually
minimum, by Inspectorate International or Bureau Veritas, with
a public bar list.

**Stella:** General rule.

**Horace:** Pay the few extra basis points for allocated, unlent,
audited bullion. The cost difference between a clean ten-basis-
point ETF and a hypothetical "yield-enhanced zero-fee" product
is trivial relative to the counterparty risk you take on by
accepting the yield.

**[BLOCK J — Sizing — 21:00]**

**Stella:** Show the gold ownership chart.

**[VISUAL: image/week06_gold_ownership.png]**

**Horace:** This is gold as a percentage of global financial
assets, 1971 through 2025. Long-run average around five
percent. 1980 peak around thirteen. 2000 trough around half a
percent. Current April 2026 reading about one-point-seven.

**Stella:** So we're underweight gold globally.

**Horace:** Materially. If global allocations mean-reverted to
the historical five-percent average — supply roughly fixed —
the implied gold price is roughly two-and-a-half to three times
current levels. Call it seven to eight thousand dollars an
ounce. Not a forecast. A sensitivity calculation. Goldbugs cite
it often. If you got a 1980-style panic-allocation move back to
thirteen percent, it would imply fifteen thousand plus. Again,
illustrative.

**Stella:** Three reference points from named investors.

**Horace:** Harry Browne, *Fail-Safe Investing*, 1981. Permanent
Portfolio — twenty-five percent gold, twenty-five stocks,
twenty-five long Treasuries, twenty-five cash. First systematic
case for a large permanent gold sleeve from a quant-minded
retail framework.

Ray Dalio at Bridgewater. All Weather, refined through the
2010s — gold roughly seven and a half percent inside a risk-
parity framework. In his 2024 LinkedIn essay *Why I Like Gold
More Than Bonds Now*, he argued for ten to fifteen percent given
current fiscal and geopolitical conditions.

Rick Rule, former CEO of Sprott US Holdings — recurring
interview guidance, "ten percent of your investable net worth in
gold and silver, two-thirds gold one-third silver, as
catastrophe insurance, and you should *hope* the insurance
never pays off." Rule frames it explicitly as insurance, not
return.

**Stella:** Three honest sizing answers for a US retail
investor?

**Horace:** Zero is the Bogleheads answer. Internally consistent
if you believe inflation stays tame and real rates stay positive
forever. Sixty-forty backtest from 1981 to 2021 supports it. The
2022 result does not.

Five to fifteen percent is the institutional consensus —
Browne at the high end, Dalio at the low end, Rule at the
middle, and the global market portfolio argument also at about
five. Right answer for most investors who want a portfolio
that performs across multiple regimes.

Twenty percent or more is the goldbug answer. Internally
consistent only if you have strong conviction on monetary
regime change. Most people running this allocation have not
honestly stress-tested the 1980-to-2000 panel from the regime
chart.

**Stella:** Why not zero?

**Horace:** Because 2022 cracked the regime that made bonds the
diversifier in sixty-forty. Gold was the diversifier in 2022 —
flat to up while stocks and bonds both fell. That's the regime
gold gets paid for.

**Stella:** Why not twenty?

**Horace:** Because the 1980-to-2000 lost-decade scenario
becomes a portfolio-level disaster at twenty. An asset that
loses eighty percent of its real value over twenty years at
twenty percent of book is a career-ending hole. Five to fifteen
at the same scenario is a manageable drag.

**[BLOCK K — Tax, non-US, and one common myth — 22:30]**

**Stella:** Tax angle, briefly.

**Horace:** US — GLD, IAU, GLDM, SGOL, BAR, IAUM are all
grantor trusts holding "collectibles." Long-term capital gains
rate is twenty-eight percent, not the fifteen-to-twenty that
applies to ordinary equities. Three honest workarounds, none
automatic. Hold the bullion ETF in an IRA — cleanest. Use Sprott
PHYS or RCM MNT with an annually-filed QEF election on Form
8621 — converts to long-term capital gains, but it's real
paperwork. Express the position via long-dated equity options on
GLD — *but* these are equity options under standard rules,
not Section 1256 broad-based-index options. Wash-sale rules,
straddle rules, exercise and assignment rules all apply. Don't
assume options "automatically solve" the twenty-eight percent
issue.

**Stella:** And the common myth.

**Horace:** A bunch of US states — Texas, Wyoming, Utah,
Oklahoma, Louisiana — have passed legislation declaring gold
and silver legal tender for state purposes and exempt from
state sales tax. Texas opened a state bullion depository in
2018. The legislation is *real* but it operates only at the
*state-tax* level. Federal capital gains treatment is
unchanged — the twenty-eight percent collectibles rate still
applies regardless of which state you live in. Anyone telling
you "gold is tax-free in Texas" is mixing up state sales tax
with federal capital gains tax.

**Stella:** Non-US investors briefly.

**Horace:** Hong Kong — no capital gains, no GST, deepest
physical gold market in Asia, hold US-listed GLD or local
bank bullion. Taiwan — no capital gains, common vehicle is the
bank gold passbook, but those are *unallocated* claims on the
bank so the custody caveats apply. Mainland China — Shanghai
Gold Exchange and bank gold accumulation accounts; cross-
border ETF access restricted. And one important warning for
all three — US-listed ETFs including GLD are *US situs assets*
for US estate tax. A non-US-domiciled investor holding more
than sixty thousand dollars of US-listed securities at death
runs into the US estate-tax regime — up to forty percent on
the excess. Canadian-domiciled funds like PHYS and MNT, and
physical gold held outside the US, sidestep this.

**[OUTRO — 23:30]**

**Stella:** OK to summarise. Gold is not a stock. It has
essentially no cashflow, modulo a small lease-rate yield in the
wholesale market. It is priced by belief, just like every other
store of value — but five thousand years of belief is a
durable starting point, not a guarantee. Its real-rate hedge
has worked in three windows out of five decades, and central
bank buying since 2010 has added a structural marginal bid that
the 2010s cycle did not have. For US retail investors, GLDM
and GLD are the workhorses; PHYS and MNT add Canadian-
domiciled options with redemption rights and a tax workaround.
Five to fifteen percent is the institutional sizing consensus.
Express it through options when the tax math favors it — but
read the fine print.

**Horace:** And the meta-point — don't argue about whether
gold "has value." Every store of value is belief. The honest
question is durability. Gold has five thousand years. The
