# Trading Strategy with Peter Brandt — Course Synthesis

**Course:** *Charting Masterclasses from a Trading Legend* (Real Vision Academy, five-part series, May 2017), plus the series' closing process lesson.

**Instructor:** Peter Brandt is a classical chartist who has traded foreign exchange and commodity futures since 1975 out of the Chicago Board of Trade. He ran his own proprietary firm, Factor Trading, from 1981, and in 2010 had his trading records back to 1981 audited and attested at an average annual return of roughly **41.6%**, achieved on a win rate of only **41–42%**. He is the author of *Diary of a Professional Commodity Trader* (Wiley), which spent 25 straight weeks as the #1 finance book on Amazon. Episode 1 is a career interview hosted by Raoul Pal; Episodes 2–5 are Brandt teaching solo.

**What the course covers:** Brandt lays out his complete framework for chart-based speculation in five episodes: his life and how his method was formed (Episode 1, an interview with Raoul Pal); the risk-management principles, sizing rules, and performance metrics that "pay the bills" (Episode 2); what charts can and cannot do, and the specific classical patterns he trades in the Schabacker tradition, with worked market examples (Episode 3); the human element — emotions, character traits, and how they are managed (Episode 4); and trading as a serious business, including a walk-through of his trading week and two trade case studies (Episode 5). The through-line: chart patterns themselves are a minor edge; aggressive risk management, emotional self-management, process, and longevity are what compound a career.

## Part I — Philosophy First: Principles Before Tactics

### 1.1 The decision pyramid

Brandt's central organizing idea is that **tactics cannot stand alone**. He draws a pyramid with three layers:

1. **Philosophy of market speculation** — what trading is and what philosophy drives your participation.
2. **Principles and guidelines** — derived from the philosophy.
3. **Tactics** — specific actions, which must "hang" from the layers above.

His metaphor: *"A tactic is like something you put on a Christmas tree. Without the Christmas tree, what you put on a Christmas tree can't hang."* Novices run straight to tactics — 90% of the questions he receives are tactical — and thereby lose the broader construct of the business.

Worked example of the flow, from his own trading:

- **Philosophy:** capital preservation is the first priority of successful speculation. **Principle:** therefore, cut losses short. **Tactic:** never take a loss home on a Friday — he will not hold a losing position over a weekend. He sold euro/US dollar short on a breakout on December 15; by December 30 it showed a loss going into the weekend, so he exited — though his opinion hadn't changed.
- Second example — **philosophy:** net profitability comes from very few large trades (let winners run, cut losers short). **Guideline:** use trailing stops so a winner never round-trips back to entry. **Tactic:** his **three-day trailing stop rule** — a mechanism he deliberately declines to fully describe — which keeps him in a run until at least **70% of his initial target** has been reached, then takes him out when specific reversal conditions occur over three days.

### 1.2 Risk management is an umbrella

"Risk management" is a catch-all with two halves in Brandt's practice: **capital / money management** — account-level decisions such as whether to hold any positions into Brexit or a US election, the maximum loss for a single day, positioning before a Fed announcement, composite risk of highly correlated trades, and risk per trade — and **trade management** — decisions about each individual trade: entry strategy, stop placement, sizing, profit-taking.

### 1.3 The ten guiding principles

Brandt lists ten principles that guide everything he does:

1. **My judgment on markets is unreliable.** He may be convinced he knows where a market is going; markets will do what they do. Flowing tactic: always use stops, cut losses short, because he can be "absolutely 180% wrong."
2. **A trade has two dimensions: direction AND timing.** Knowing crude oil was going down is worthless if the timing is wrong. Both dimensions must work for a trade to succeed.
3. **Traders trade price and price alone.** Warren Buffett buys whole companies to live on their income; anyone who buys a stock intending to sell it within the next 20 years is not an investor, he is a trader — and traders trade price, not the name in the upper-left corner of the chart. "I don't care the name that's on the upper left hand side of a chart. I care about the price scale that runs down the right side… the sooner they become accustomed to the fact that they're time and price traders, the more… realistic [their] expectation of what they're doing in their life." His favorite illustration: in 2011 he called the absolute high of silver at **$50.00** within 24 hours, "almost with arrogance." A Twitter personality bet him **$1,000 that silver would never again trade below $25**; Brandt took the bet and won — the man never paid and disappeared from social media, having reportedly had his entire fortune in silver at a **$38 average**. The lesson: the man was in love with the macro story of silver — the *name* on the chart — while Brandt traded the y-axis.
4. **Trade identification is one of the LEAST important components of success.** Signaling — what to buy and when — is where 90% of novices spend 90% of their time, money, and energy, and it is not where the outcome comes from. (See the "edge pie" in Part V.)
5. **Longevity. Trading is a marathon, not a sprint.** Build an approach that could last an entire generation; don't think about this week's P&L but about a compounded return over decades that keeps you in the game for the major moves. Corollary: **"no position" is a position** — traders feel naked without a position, but flat is often exactly the right stance.
6. **My one job is to manage losses.** His Cargill-trained mentor defined his own job as "a loss taker. I take losses quickly. Profits? I let my profits worry about themselves." Tactical expression: limit risk on any given trade to a maximum of about **1–2% of total trading capital**. People who risk 5–10% per trade: "that's fine for people like George Soros," but for an active trader it is "bound for doom" — the math makes survival impossible.
7. **Protect the pile of chips.** Like a poker player in Las Vegas: when the chips are gone, you're out of the game. Related belief he trades by: **"my worst drawdown is the one that's yet to happen."** That assumption forces respect for sizing.
8. **Focus on sequential closed-trade NAV** (detailed in Part IV.4).
9. **Money management takes priority over market opinions.** Who cares what the chart says when the position is going against you? Hence his signature phrase: **"strong opinions, weakly held."** He can hold a strong view, but the moment the position turns against him the opinion must become weak. He is criticized on Twitter for being bearish one week and bullish the next; dogmatism, he says, is what produces large losses.
10. **The right trading metrics.** Annualized return and Sharpe ratio are "junk… they need to be placed on the junk pile." What matters: **gain-to-pain ratio, profit ratio, and Calmar ratio** (Part IV.7).

### 1.4 What he does NOT believe

- **Per-trade probability estimates.** "I don't believe in probability, because probability can only be measured statistically when you have a large number." With roughly 8 trades a month, no meaningful probability can attach to an individual trade; what he knows is his own long-run statistics: "there is no average year for me."
- **Running outside money.** He traded Commodities Corporation capital in the 1980s — effectively a free call option (trading 100 of his own contracts plus a 15-contract profit share with no downside) — but has refused outside money for over 20 years: "trading other people's capital would basically ruin me. It would screw up my head so bad that it would affect my own trading."
- **Day trading.** Odds are heavily against it — research and exchange reports he cites put consistently profitable day traders at about **1%**. Classical chart breakouts on daily or intraday charts are not a viable day-trading method.

## Part II — What Charts Can and Cannot Do

### 2.1 The three things charts give him

Addressing charting "naysayers" who call it voodoo, Brandt concedes the core objection outright — *"you're absolutely right. Price charts do fail. They fail much more often than they're correct."* What charts actually provide:

1. **Where a market has been, where it is now, and perhaps the path of least resistance** — valuable information in itself.
2. **Evidence of accumulation or distribution** — when a pattern sets up just perfectly, it can reveal strong-handed interests building or unloading positions at a price level.
3. **The real edge: highly skewed, asymmetric reward-to-risk opportunities** — "a point in the history of price where I can place a bigger bet than normal with a limited risk." That is where the value of charting comes from.

### 2.2 The honest limitations

- **Charts are not a comprehensive market analysis tool.** "Most markets most of the time defy understanding from a classical charting point of view." Demanding a constant chart-based opinion on the S&P is, in his word, "lunacy."
- **The two-second rule.** "I'll look at a chart for two seconds. And if I don't see something in two seconds of looking at a chart, I move on."
- **Valid patterns are the exception, not the rule.** In a given market — the S&Ps, soybeans, London cocoa — **two valid chart patterns in a year** may be all there is.
- **Reliability is cyclical.** Roughly **four months of the year chart patterns are reliable; the other eight months they are relatively unreliable.**
- **No global macro narratives from a single chart.** Reading one gold chart and deriving what oil, the dollar, and stock markets must do: "What if you read the chart wrong? Then your whole global macro narrative is wrong."
- **Charts morph (morphology).** His sweet spot is a pattern **about 20–26 weeks long** (range 10 weeks to a year), and when he dissects one he finds it is assembled from numerous shorter patterns that *failed* — a head-and-shoulders can turn out to be merely the left shoulder of a larger one, or a piece of a rectangle. The chartist's challenge is the **patience to wait out the morphing and the discipline to pull the trigger** when a pattern finally completes and works. And a failure is itself information: "the fact that it failed may be a story unto itself" — sometimes the setup for the real trade.

### 2.3 False breakouts: then and now

In the late 1970s and 1980s a breakout was fairly reliable — "you could almost bet that the chart pattern would fulfill the implications that it had." Today he sees **far more false breakouts, premature breakouts, and erratic breakouts**. His adaptations: pay **less attention to candlestick spindles/wicks** (largely high-frequency traders running stops); pay **more attention to weekly charts**; and pay special attention to **closing-price charts** — the close is the single most important price of the day, and the Friday close the most important of the week.

### 2.4 Dogma is the enemy

His repeated cure for chart-dogmatism is the same phrase: **strong opinions, weakly held.** Traders who dig in become "convinced they're right," and that is normally where large losses come from. He is deliberately flexible — negative on a market one week, positive the next — because the position, not the opinion, is what must be protected.

## Part III — Brandt's Charting Method and Pattern Catalog

### 3.1 The frame: Schabacker lineage, weekly for opinion, daily for timing

- His training is **Richard W. Schabacker** — the 1933 manuscript that founded classical charting and from which Edwards and Magee later derived. "I would consider myself to be a Schabacker disciple," and he treats Schabacker's conditions for each pattern as **cardinal rules**. Most charts posted on social media, he laments, draw patterns that "just do not meet the conditions" — wrong head-and-shoulders, wrong trend lines, wrong wedges.
- **Weekly charts are the basis of his opinion; daily charts are for timing and tactics.** "It's difficult to do tactics based on weekly charts. Tactics belong on daily charts."
- Simplicity as doctrine: a **white background** (he knows nobody does this anymore) and **as few lines as possible**. Overcrowded charts he calls **"Star Wars charting"** — "so many lines, I get dizzy." Ten chartists shown one chart will produce about fifteen opinions; clarity is the defense.
- **Markets traded:** roughly **50% global futures** (all continents), **40% spot FX** in the major currencies (Australian dollar, British pound, etc. — no exotics like Brazil or South Africa), **10% global equities**.
- **Secondary inputs he does use:** **CFTC Commitment of Traders reports** — not week to week, but when positioning is near **record historical extremes** it becomes a factor; a simple **14-day moving average**; the **ADX indicator** ("just about the only indicator that I pay attention to"); and **analog years** — markets behave similarly across eras, so if a market starts acting like one from 10–20 years ago, study that precedent to anticipate what may come next.
- **Blacklist:** Elliott Wave, cycles, indicators in general, Gann, Fibonacci retracements, seasonals. His logic on indicators: "an indicator is really a derivative of price. So why should I study a derivative of price if I can study price itself?"

### 3.2 The master distinction: diagonal vs horizontal patterns

Brandt files **every** chart pattern into two categories:

- **Diagonal patterns** — boundary lines drawn on a slant: trend lines, symmetrical triangles, rising and falling wedges, channels.
- **Horizontal patterns** — the important boundary lines are flat: head-and-shoulders (top and bottom), right-angle (ascending/descending) triangles, rectangles.

**He trades horizontal patterns and avoids diagonals.** His reasoning is statistical, not aesthetic: his lifetime win rate is ~41–42%, but he estimates that had he traded only diagonal patterns, his win rate "would be somewhere down in the high 20s or low 30s" — and "in terms of probability theory, there is just a massive difference in outcomes between a 30% win rate and a 42% win rate." Diagonals, even when valid, are **far more likely to morph** and produce tricky trading. His worked examples:

- **Trend lines** (Dow Jones Transports, soybean oil): "You go through a chart book with a ruler and a pen in a cage with an ape, and sooner or later you're going to have a trend line that makes some sense." Even multi-touch trend lines "more often than not… morph out into something else." He is not criticizing trend-line users — he is declining the lower probability.
- **Channels** (Canadian dollar futures): well-defined boundaries, yet as diagonal constructions they "tend to morph… much less reliable."
- **Symmetrical triangles** (euro/yen cross; US dollar/Norwegian krone): the euro-yen triangle broke out down and failed, broke out up and failed, broke out down again — and only eventually became a symmetrical triangle that actually worked, after repeated morphing. A trader who proclaimed the dollar-Norway downside breakout "would have eventually proven to be quite wrong."
- **Wedges** (rising wedge in the Dow Jones Utility Average on a semi-log chart back to 2001; a diagonal trend line in the French CAC): they can produce successful trades, but "making any sort of adamant statement about a market based on a diagonal chart construction is really, really skating on very thin ice." (He uses arithmetic scales for daily-chart work; semi-log only for long spans like this.)

**Why do chartists lean on diagonals?** Two reasons he names: (1) commentators "don't want to come out and say, I have nothing to say about markets" — most of the time markets defy chart understanding, so the need to always point at something pushes people onto slanted lines; (2) more dangerously and subtly, traders want to **justify a bias or an existing position** — "they're not doing it out of deception," but they hunt for a chart case that aligns with what they already hold. (The flags-and-pennants exception is covered in 3.6.)

### 3.3 Head and shoulders (the reversal pattern he anchors on)

**Head-and-shoulders top** — Brandt's idealized reading is a story of **distribution** by large players: a major trend has been ridden by "very smart, deep-pocketed traders"; trends eventually change, and the pattern shows how they respond to rallies and dips. Price rallies to the **left shoulder** and breaks; large traders attempt to manipulate markets and spread rumors (fully legal in futures markets, he notes); the market rallies to a new high — **the head** — "traps the little trader, and allows the large trader to dump their final positions"; price breaks back to the **neckline**, rallies to the **right shoulder**, and the reversal takes hold. Worked example: a head-and-shoulders top in **US dollar/Japanese yen** — left shoulder, break, head ("a little more complicated" than the idealized diagram), right shoulder, then a substantial downtrend. **Why it counts as horizontal:** the neckline is a flat boundary line.

**Head-and-shoulders bottom** — the mirror image: shorts cover on the left shoulder, rally to the neckline, the head, rally to the neckline, right shoulder, trend reversal. Worked example: a textbook H&S bottom in **soybean meal** — bought the breakout, added on the way up, the market exceeded his target, and the **three-day trailing stop rule** finally stopped him out of the entire position on May 31 (the lesson cites a pattern completion of April 11 and his breakout purchase on November 11; the spoken dates are partially garbled in the source).

**Continuation head-and-shoulders** — the same formation occurring at the *midpoint* of a major trend rather than at a top or bottom. His example: a continuation H&S in **gold** from early 2008 through Q3 2009; when the flat-topped formation completed around **$1,020**, gold ran to **$1,900** — "a very substantial pattern."

### 3.4 Right-angle triangles

Two varieties, both defined by one flat boundary line that prices ultimately break:

- **Descending triangle:** the market rallies, meets substantial supply, falls back; each subsequent rally meets **more aggressive selling at progressively lower prices** while support holds at one flat level — until the flat support gives way and the market "falls away quickly." Worked example: a descending triangle in **gold** that began in summer 2016 and completed on **October 4, 2016**, producing a decline from about **$1,300 to about $1,130**.
- **Ascending triangle:** the mirror image — the market meets overhead supply, pulls back, and during the formation **buyers become more and more aggressive while sellers sit scattered at the same level**, until the selling dries up and buyers take control. Worked example: the **US dollar/Chinese yuan**'s **20-month ascending triangle**, which he calls "probably going to go down in history as one of the classic textbook examples" — after the breakout he thought there was a strong probability the dollar-yuan would never, in his lifetime, return to the levels of the triangle's construction.

### 3.5 Rectangles

**Definition:** a trading range bounded by **two horizontal (or near-parallel) lines**, upper and lower. A rectangle can be:

- a **reversal pattern** — an uptrend (or downtrend) stops, price trades sideways between the boundaries, then the trend reverses; or
- a **continuation pattern** — the range forms mid-trend and the preceding trend resumes on breakout.

His examples:

- **Natural gas, reversal:** a broad rectangle from **April 2011 to September 2014**; its downside resolution launched "the great bear market in the energy markets" from June 2014 down to the February 2016 bottom — "one for the ages."
- **US dollar/Turkish lira, continuation:** the dollar ground higher, drifted sideways in a rectangle, and broke out in **October 2016** into a substantial rally — coinciding with the apparent coup and political instability in Turkey, which Brandt treats as incidental: "I basically don't pay attention to the news. I try to keep myself cloistered away from the types of things most people are on the edge of their chair waiting for CNBC to record."
- **British pound/US dollar ("cable"), continuation:** after the sharp post-Brexit-vote decline of June 2016, the market "drifted sideways in just a perfect, perfect rectangular pattern" and broke out on **October 3**. "That's the kind of move I'm looking for."

**Size expectation:** he is not looking for 50–60% moves in the underlying — he targets **moves of roughly 5% to 20% of the underlying value**, which horizontal patterns are excellent at identifying.

### 3.6 Flags and pennants ("half-mast" patterns) — the exception to the diagonal ban

Brief patterns that form **within a very strong, sustained trend**: a pause, "a tight area of congestion," before the trend resumes. Charts make it easy to see when a market is trending hard versus going nowhere, and when a strong trend pauses briefly, the pause can be traded.

- **Pennant** example: after the post-election "Trump blast-off" in the **Dow Jones Industrial Average**, price went sideways from about mid-December to early February — not a rectangle, not a triangle — a tight congestion he defines as a pennant. The breakout line here was technically diagonal, **but the diagonal ran in the direction of the trend** — and that is the distinction he draws: breaking up *through* a downtrend line against the trend is unreliable; a brief pause whose resolution line supports the prevailing trend is a different, tradeable animal.
- **Flag** example: in **US dollar/Chinese yuan**, a down-sloping drift immediately after a straight-line advance — on its face a diagonal pattern, but because it followed a sustained, "almost dramatic" move he was willing to trade it; plus a second pennant from July through October 2016 that resolved upward. "Flags are more dependable than a typical diagonal pattern."
- **Crude oil** (from the process episode): after a descending-triangle short played out and covered at its target, he watched a **four- or five-day flag** form and laid an order to re-short on its completion, looking for crude "to perhaps go eventually much lower."

### 3.7 Pattern duration and selectivity

- **The sweet spot: horizontal patterns between 10 and 26 weeks in duration** ("that's in my wheel house"). Patterns persisting **multiple years** may be fine for an overview but become **useless for trading**, because execution must be geared to daily charts. (He will still reference a giant multi-year structure as context — see the Eurodollar trade in Part VII — but never times an entry from it.)
- **If a pattern looks like it might be one but he isn't sure: "I ignore them. I want to see patterns that are just undeniably clear."**
- Expect only about **two valid patterns per market per year**; everything else is noise or morphology in progress.

### 3.8 Breakouts, validity, and targets

- He is a **breakout trader**: he enters at the precise point on the chart that "should lead to an immediate increase in the value of a long position" — no interest in markets chopping in broad trading ranges, every interest in markets capable of an **immediate thrust**.
- **Valid breakouts lean on closes** (see 2.3): closing prices, weekly charts, the Friday close — with the explicit acknowledgment that false breakouts are more common now than in the 1970s–80s and that he has had to adapt.
- **He always has a target in mind** at entry — his anticipation of where the market will go — and lets a trade run to at least **70% of that target** before his tightening rules can take him out. Expected magnitude: **5–20% of the underlying**, not 50–60%.
- **A failed breakout is data, not noise** — failed patterns feed the morphology story and can hand him his next trade (see 2.2).
- **Two attempts, then leave it alone:** stopped out of a setup and the market reverses? He will date "a girl twice, and from there on it's somebody else's date." He tried dollar/yen twice, was wrong twice, and a third try "would have been a charm" — but rules beat charm: "I have to be rules based. I have to be disciplined."

## Part IV — Risk Management: The Numbers and the Rules

### 4.1 The cautionary tale that frames everything

At a February webinar for Factor members, someone asked: *"What would I do if I were short the stock index markets from a level where the Dow was 10,000?"* Brandt was flabbergasted — the per-contract loss on Dow futures from 10,000 would have been **over $50,000**, a short against more than half the Dow's rise since its 1930s Depression low. "It's big losses such as this, which occur all too frequently, that really end the career of traders." His defense: **a stop in the market, always** — he never puts a position on, ignores it, hopes, gets stubborn, and sinks so far underwater that hanging on becomes the only option — and **he always exits a loss on a Friday**.

### 4.2 Position sizing — the actual numbers

- **Risk per trade:** capped at roughly **1–2% of total trading capital**; typical initial risk is **70–80 basis points**, with a normal range of **30–40 bps up to about 150 bps** on the best setups, and periods when he deliberately down-sizes across the board.
- **Why 5% is doom:** working with Mark Ritchie Jr. on "very sophisticated Excel programs" examining risk, the conclusion is unambiguous — an active trader risking 5% of capital per trade "is going to be history… carried out on a cot," because the win rate required to survive at that risk level is unreasonably high. And the self-deception trap: a trader who says "I only risk 100 bps" but becomes obsessed with one market and takes that same 100 bps **ten times** has quietly lost 10–15% of capital on a single idea.
- **How sizing is computed:** entry and the invalidation point ("where I'm wrong") are defined first, so the **risk per contract is known — and he backs into position size from the risk budget**: "If I'm going to risk, let's say, eight-tenths of 1% or 1% of my capital on this trade… how many contracts do I do?" Size is an output of risk, never the other way around.
- **His mental yardstick:** roughly **6 to 12 futures contracts per million dollars** of designated trading capital (with an analogous per-million unit for FX positions; the exact FX figure is garbled in the source).
- **Leverage actually used:** margin equity of only about **10–20% of the capital he designates as AUM**. He argues leveraged markets are *easier* to control risk in: 24-hour markets, deep liquidity (the December Eurodollar at 10,000 bid / 20,000 offered in the middle of the night), and cash flexibility because his whole AUM is never tied up in a cash purchase.
- **Asymmetry is not the goal:** occasionally he will risk **70 bps to harvest a 35 bps profit in two or three days** if the setup is right — he is not optimizing reward-to-risk per trade; he is optimizing "I know where I'm wrong in a market, and I know what my downside is."

### 4.3 Stops and active trade management

- **A stop exists on every trade, always** — placed at entry, never added later as an afterthought.
- **Advance stops to break-even as fast as possible** — sometimes the same day, sometimes a day or two later: his **"active and aggressive trade management protocol."**
- **Trail stops as the trade works**, tightening them "extremely tight" once the move approaches the target — the **three-day trailing stop rule** is his profit-capture mechanism after the 70%-of-target threshold (Part I.1).
- **"Popcorn trades."** The pattern he works hardest to kill: a trade pops immediately, works for days or weeks, then rolls over and returns all the way to entry. He uses "various statistical methods" to manage these; the stated objective is that **no trade that has been profitable should ever come all the way back to entry**.
- **Best trades announce themselves:** "the best trades are those trades that I put on and they're in profit by the end of day one and they never look back." Those are the trades that write his year.
- **The Friday rule and the two-day rule** (from his mentor): never take a losing position home on a Friday; never go home with a losing position two days in a row.
- **Contingency orders:** when he enters on a breakout, the entry stop order carries attached orders — a protective stop if filled, and a profit-taker at target — submitted as one bundled "contingency order." "I do not like being… naked on the order side."

### 4.4 Sequential closed-trade NAV

His most unconventional practice. Most traders watch account value including open equity; Brandt considers obsession with open-trade equity **destructive** — "my open profits are not mine… they're play money. What matters is what's in the bank." He charts only his **net asset value on closed trades, in sequence**: each plotted point moves by the rate of return of the next closed trade. His example: long soybeans with a $3,000 open profit per contract; a government-report day knocks it to $2,000; he exits. Did he make $2,000 or lose $1,000? Traders call that a bad day; he cannot see how "taking money off the table and putting it into the bank can represent a bad day." The four steps he uses to keep the closed-trade NAV line rising are exactly 4.2–4.3: (1) enter at breakout points likely to move immediately; (2) get stops to break-even quickly; (3) let profits run despite the temptation to grab money after five straight losses; (4) protect popcorn trades from full round-trips.

### 4.5 Counterparty and liquidity risk

- He keeps **way too much money in cash** by design — everything liquid, instantly available for the next trade.
- Multiple clearing firms and FX dealers, with a **maximum of about 20% of his designated AUM at any one of them**, movable "on a pretty quick basis."
- He has been burned and names names: he lost money in the **MF Global** collapse, lost "a considerable sum" with **Refco** ("Revco" in the transcript) when the then-largest futures commission merchant went under, and lost money with **Paragon Financial**. Raoul Pal's point, which Brandt endorses: people forget counterparty risk entirely while trading.

### 4.6 The mathematics of drawdowns and win rates

This is the statistical heart of the course:

- **Win rate ~41–42%.** "My default expectation is I'm going to be a loser on this trade… I think that's a remarkable mindset to be able to trade that way." **Of the next 50 trades, he knows 6–10 will produce his net bottom line — and 42 will have to cross themselves out.** "I'm letting 42 trades be a washout so that eight trades can give me my bottom line."
- **The shape of a year:** about **3–4 months are meaningfully profitable (500–1,000 basis points)**; about **4 months tread water** (good trades followed by slaps in the face); about **4 months are white-knuckle**, in which he believes — as he believes every year at some point — "I'm never going to have a winning trade again."
- **The underwater curve.** He shows the Factor underwater curve back to 1981 and the S&P 500's since 1986 to make the same point: **a trader (and even a buy-and-hold index investor in a great bull market) is either in a drawdown or recovering from one the vast majority of the time.** Counter-intuitive but statistically inevitable: "it's two steps forward, one step back." The implication is everything in this Part — keep drawdowns shallow so recoveries are fast.
- **His worst peak-to-valley giveback ever, including mark-to-market, is 35%.** Not comfortable — "there's a lot of people that have suffered far worse" — but survivable, and a direct product of the rules above.
- **The standing assumption: "your worst drawdown is yet to come."** He doesn't look forward to the saying; he trades like it's true. Companion proverb: **"You meet old traders and you meet bold traders, but you never meet an old bold trader."**

### 4.7 The metrics that matter

Annualized return and Sharpe ratio: "basically junk… worthless" as measures of trader skill. The three he tracks instead (he credits the gain-to-pain ratio to his friend **Jack Schwager**'s book):

- **Gain-to-pain ratio:** sum of all monthly returns ÷ sum of the absolute values of negative monthly returns. **1 = quite good; 2 = excellent; 3+ = world class.**
- **Profit ratio:** the same construction per trade — cumulative return of all winning trades ÷ absolute value of the cumulative return of all losing trades, each trade measured against total trading capital. His own runs **over 3, 3.5**.
- **Calmar ratio:** average annualized return over 3 or 5 years (textbook is 5) ÷ worst drawdown in that same period. **1 = good; 2 = very good/outstanding; 3 = world class.** He also cites a **MAR ratio above 1**.

**The audited record:** in 2010 he had trading records back to 1981 reviewed by an auditing firm, which attested the performance after cross-checking brokerage statements against bank statements "and more importantly, what I paid the IRS": an average annual return of about **41.6%**. He refuses the genius framing: *"I don't attribute that to my genius or my smarts or my talents as a trader… I attribute it to the attention I have paid to risk management."*

## Part V — The Human Element

### 5.1 The edge pie

Brandt's definition of edge: "a very small, sometimes seemingly insignificant advantage that a trader can exploit over, and over, and over again to gain a thin margin over the marketplace. If I need to better define an edge than that, then chances are you don't have an edge." Every successful trader he has known has one **unique to themselves** — and no two of the great Commodities Corporation traders (Ed Seykota, Paul Tudor Jones, and the rest of that "birthing place of fabulous traders") trade the same way.

His own edge, as a pie chart shown on screen, ranks four contributors: **1. Risk management** (the biggest slice), **2. Managing emotions** (a close second), **3. Process**, **4. Trade identification / signaling** (the smallest slice by far). Early in his career he thought charting *was* the edge; four decades on, he is "really not sure that charts offer traders an edge… they may offer a small edge, but an edge that's really insignificant relative to the kind of performance that I want." His stated edge, offered elsewhere in the series: "First, very aggressive, very active risk management. Second, the size differentials" — the 30-to-150-bps spectrum of trade risk.

### 5.2 Character faults that sabotage traders

His inventory of traits he has watched destroy trading performance — several of which he owns in himself: **compulsiveness** (left in front of a screen, "I could talk myself out of a good trade and into a bad trade many times a day"), **worrying**, **perfectionism** ("perfection is the enemy of good"), **stubbornness** (the direct route to holding losses far too long), **overconfidence** and equally **under-confidence**, **over-aggressiveness** ("bulldogs that never know when to leave the ring"), **over-possessiveness** (refusing to credit others' ideas), **being unfocused**, **closed-mindedness**, and **greed** — "they just want every last penny on the table."

The famous simplification — fear and greed — he waves off: "I wish that was the end of it. Fear and greed are just the start." And the raw adage: **if you really want to know yourself — the good, the bad, the ugly, warts and all — become a trader.** Greed, false hope, indecisiveness: "the market's going to teach you… if you are open to learn about yourself, the market is a great teacher." Richard Dennis's line he quotes with approval: **"successful trading is an upstream swim against human nature."** For a discretionary trader, the worst enemy is found by going into the bathroom and looking in the mirror.

### 5.3 The traits that make traders

What he has observed in accomplished traders: **patience** (they "wait for their pitch" without needing always to be in), **diligence** (attention to detail — "sometimes it's the small things that really have the big payout"), **industriousness**, **analytical depth** (they don't take the first answer), **creativity** (seeing outcomes others would call crazy), and **curiosity** (about markets, about technical analysis, about what central banks do). The work is to manage the faults and accentuate the strengths — which requires brutal self-honesty, "and quite frankly, not an easy thing to do."

### 5.4 Brandt's own coping mechanisms

- **Avoid the screen during market hours.** He enters orders in two windows when markets are quiet or closed — **4:00–4:30 PM Mountain Time** and around **5:00–6:00 AM** — and stays away from intraday ticks: "my strength is in looking at charts from a bigger picture… if I bog myself down into the markets intraday, eventually my emotions are going to get the best of me."
- **Fear of missing trades.** He hates missing a move ("that drives me crazy") and beats himself up over it; the remedies are a written **checklist** for catching trades, honest review of whether his reasons for trading or not trading were right, and acceptance that the two-attempts rule means he *will* miss trades.
- **Risk aversion and gun-shyness.** He is more risk-averse than he should be — after two or three straight losses he can go gun-shy. His fix is mechanical: a **sizing grid** that starts at a defined bet level and adjusts, so "systematic reasoning and logical thinking" override the emotional urge to avoid risk, and he forces himself to take trades at full planned size.
- **The meta-rule: manage, don't overcome.** After 40+ years he still feels fear, greed, and self-doubt; the question is only whether they will be allowed to dictate trades. "We manage our emotions… we don't overcome them… It's a never-ending battle."

## Part VI — Process: Trading as a Business

### 6.1 Six business practices

Trading for Brandt is "a dead serious business" whether done full-time, part-time, or as recreation, and his boyhood equation (pick the right market, place the bet, collect the money) gave way to a craft practiced with discipline. On the "art or science" debate he is dismissive: "the debate is a bunch of nonsense. **Charting is a craft. I'm a craftsman as a chartist.** Trading is a business." His six practices:

1. **Know your product.** He knows his approach "as well as I know anything on earth. I know its heartbeat. I know where it's good. I know where it's bad. I know its weaknesses… I know my product." Every successful trader he knows is, above all, a good problem solver — the method is built through years of trial, error, brick walls, and being "slapped on the head by markets when I've been careless."
2. **Take it deadly seriously.** He has watched lazy, haphazard traders who trade then go have fun. That is not the job.
3. **Build for longevity.** His standing advice: build an approach that "can stand the test of time," not one tuned to this week.
4. **Maintain a comprehensive business plan.** Far beyond buy/sell/price: a framework for understanding markets; the "lenses" for reading price behavior; capitalization levels per trade; selection and entry guidelines; risk and trade-management guidelines; sizing **per unit of money** ("I don't just say, how many contracts can my money buy? No. I always think in terms of sizing per unit of money. I think in terms of risk"); and exit conditions.
5. **Systematic review and critique.** He grades **every trade A through F** on trade selection, entry, sizing, exit, and correlations, as honestly as possible; he grades **each year** of trading; and he tracks the three key metrics (Part IV.7) on a rolling basis.
6. **Keep a board of advisors.** He recommends unpaid "board of directors" roles to young traders: other traders who understand your method and will give critical, honest reviews — not of what you bought, but of *how you go about the business*. His own board, formed early, still reviews him **quarterly**, "and they don't always tell me the things I want to hear."

### 6.2 "I am an order enterer"

Strip away the glamour — the reality is the opposite. "A trader is an order enterer… I enter orders in a very quiet, procedural, systematic, repeatable way." The test he applies to every order is the **wall-pin test**: blow the chart up to wall size; for every trade you've ever taken in that market, push in a green pin for a buy and a red pin for a sale; step back across the room and ask, *do they all make sense? Can I look at this a year after the fact and say that trade made sense?* He knows some "really good trades" lost money and some "absolutely stupid trades" made money — the pins judge process, not outcome.

### 6.3 The trading week

His week is clockwork — he can say what he will be doing a year from today at a given hour. All times Mountain Time:

- **Friday afternoon (from ~3:00–3:30 PM, as markets close): the week begins.** He scrolls the weekly charts of his full universe — **about 75 markets**: every liquid major futures market worldwide plus primary and secondary FX crosses (sometimes multiple contract months and continuation charts), noting markets that pop: setups forming, trades he's in, trades on watch. Result: the **long list of 15–20 markets**. He starts with markets he already has positions in, since those automatically get deeper review (typically about six trades in flight at week's end).
- **Saturday:** the long list again, adding **daily charts** to the weeklies; no orders yet. "I'm mulling through my mind. What if this happened? What if that happened?" — building a mental picture of the orders and strategies each market might warrant.
- **Sunday (until ~2:00–2:30 PM):** the long list is cut to the **active short list** — markets now eligible for actual orders or price alerts, no longer just a watch list.
- **Sunday afternoon/evening (~3:30–4:00 PM): order entry.** For every market on the short list: if he's already in, review last week's protective stop and target ("I always have orders in to take profits when my targets are hit"); if not, define the trigger conditions — a horizontal pattern in the 10–26-week sweet spot and the specific breakout that would fire the entry. He typically enters Sunday evening with **actual orders working at the exchanges in five to eight markets**, plus **price alerts** (email + iPhone text) on levels that matter: targets, stops, entry triggers, crucial chart levels.
- **Monday (~5:00 AM):** day-session orders go in for markets he won't leave working overnight — **grains, livestock** — because thin overnight sessions are where he fears being picked off by high-frequency operations. Where a stop could be run in thin conditions he uses **stop-close-only orders**, judging in the last minutes of the session whether he wants to still be in at the close ("the closing price is the most important price of the day").
- **Monday close through Thursday:** "the boring part of the week." Each afternoon (~2:30 PM) he reviews the short list, daily and weekly charts, and fine-tunes the order flow: adjust an entry price, move a protective stop, tighten a trail. "Orders for me are constantly in flux… trading really is a function of managing my order flow."
- **Friday afternoon:** the merry-go-round starts again.

### 6.4 Order architecture and HFT defenses

- **Contingency order bundles** (entry + attached stop + attached target) so discipline executes even when he isn't watching.
- **Day-only orders** for HFT-vulnerable markets; **stop-close-only** stops where overnight stop-running is a risk.
- **Alerts** as a monitoring substitute: he does not watch screens; he defines in advance the prices worth knowing about and lets email/text tell him.
- Rationale: thin overnight sessions are "subject to being picked up by high-frequency trading operations and algos."

## Part VII — Two Complete Trade Walk-Throughs

Brandt closes the series by assembling every component — identify, enter, size, know-when-wrong, know-when-right, exit — in one winner and one loser.

### 7.1 Eurodollar futures short — a career-type trade (+500 bps)

- **Context:** like everyone, he expected the Fed to eventually raise rates from zero — but "I need to see something on a chart. It's just not some idea that the Fed's going to raise rates." He waited through 2015 and most of 2016 for chart confirmation.
- **Monthly chart:** a massive **eight-year rectangle** from 2009 to 2016 — the ZIRP era of flat rates. Longer than his ideal duration, but horizontal. He will not trade off a monthly chart; it only told him "all hands on deck."
- **Weekly chart:** the rectangle's lower boundary was penetrated in **August 2016** — time to hunt shorts. (A diagonal channel on the weekly December 2018 contract was noted but, being diagonal, not traded.)
- **Daily chart:** a **descending triangle** formed — "now we're cooking": monthly rolled over, weekly broken out, daily horizontal pattern. It broke out on **November 9**; he shorted.
- **Sizing:** from entry and invalidation he computed risk per contract, then chose roughly **0.8–1.0% of capital** risk for the position, backing into the contract count.
- **Management and exit:** stops trailed down judiciously through December; knocked out at the end of December. The trade banked **just over 500 basis points — about 5% of his account**.

### 7.2 May copper — the typical trade (−28 bps)

"Not all trades are like that… my win rate's 40%, so they can't be." May copper built a **rectangle from October**; on **February 10** it broke out and he bought the breakout — triggered off a pre-set alert. Stops were moved up a couple of times, and on **February 16** — six days later — he was stopped out for about **28–29 basis points**. "A much more typical trade for me is one in which I lose 20 or 30 basis points… I start off being willing to risk 70 or 80 basis points, clamp down my risk, and get bumped out with a small loss." Winning trades simply last a lot longer than losing ones.

### 7.3 The short list in action (same week)

- **Crude oil:** short from a **daily descending triangle** (a failed weekly breakout attempt); covered "on almost a low tick of the breakdown" at its target, and now laying an order to **re-short on a 4–5 day flag**.
- **Euro/US dollar:** a **24-going-on-25-month rectangle** whose bottom broke in December/January — he was short (with a side note on a "January effect" he wrote up for Real Vision publications), watching a possible **daily head-and-shoulders bottom** that could launch the rectangle's eventual breakout. On "jumping the gun" on patterns before completion he is candid: "I can't probably tell you that I'm miles ahead."
- **Short-term European rates:** a "massive head-and-shoulders top" in the one-year Eurodollar-type contract (Europe's 90-day rate proxy) said European yields were bottoming after years of zero and negative rate policy; he was **short the euro** on the pattern's completion. Early-stage and given little room: "if the market jerks the wrong way, I'm out, because this is a perfect example of **money management being the boss of market analysis**… by protecting my capital, I can play the game long term."

## Part VIII — Strong Opinions, Attributed

- **Technical vs fundamental:** "I absolutely believe that a fundamental is going to move any given market over a period of time. I don't have to know what that is." A beautiful six-month chart pattern tells him which way the dominant fundamental is *driving* — "I know what it's doing."
- **The dominant factor theory** — from his mentor Dan Markey: for any market or composite there is **one dominant driver** (perhaps a year for a commodity, five or ten years or more for an economy); every other fundamental factor "just adds noise and confusion"; the jiggles between point A and point B come from other things. Brandt's corollary on Twitter culture: post anything about the dollar and someone asks "what does it mean for gold?" — "All I know is I can't enter a US dollar order in the gold pit."
- **On macro knowledge:** "I make no attempt to understand a company or an economy. I don't know what central banks do. I don't know when the Fed does things, and I don't want to know." He watches Real Vision's macro minds not to learn their views but "to try to get a sense for how they think."
- **On day trading:** "Day trading is a losing proposition," with cited research that only about **1% of day traders** are consistently profitable; classical chart breakouts on intraday charts are not a reliable way to try.
- **On indicators:** "An indicator is really a derivative of price. So why should I study a derivative of price if I can study price itself?"
- **On signal services vs education:** most chart vendors sell "trade identification… a signaling system," and "I don't think selling signaling systems is really a way to educate people." His Factor weekly report instead reflects what he is actually doing in his own account, emphasizing four things: Schabacker-style charting for today's markets; risk management ("risk management pays the bills"); the mental side; and process.
- **On copy-trading gurus:** "I don't believe that you can read somebody else's approach to selecting trades and have it be successful for you… no two [great traders] trade the same way." Novices hunting the magic bullet are chasing the wrong thing.
- **Raoul Pal's contribution, endorsed by Brandt:** the most successful macro traders of all time — **Paul Tudor Jones, Stan Druckenmiller, Louis Bacon** — are all chartists first: "they filter every idea from a chart first," then decide whether it fits their framework.

## Part IX — A Life in Charts: The Career Story

**Minnesota to Chicago.** Brandt graduated from the University of Minnesota in advertising/journalism and joined one of the world's largest ad agencies (Needham, Harper & Steers) in Chicago, working on McDonald's and Campbell's Soup accounts before turning 25. A neighbor in Evanston — a soybean trader — invited him to lunch overlooking the wheat pit at the Board of Trade: "a hook in the mouth incident with big barbs." What hooked him was measurable performance: at day's end pit traders "know to a penny what they did during the day… They're not a yes man to a client." The modern futures world was then only about two years old — the Russian wheat failure of 1972–73 had started it, and there was no MBA fast track; everybody started at the bottom.

**The broker years (1975–1980).** Through a contact he joined Continental Grain — then the world's second-largest grain merchandiser after Cargill — at roughly half his advertising salary. He built his own book by creating hedging programs for his former advertising clients: Campbell's (owner of Orowheat, Pepperidge Farm, Godiva — brutally exposed to runaway commodity costs it couldn't pass through in the early-70s inflation), McDonald's, and later Homestake Mining, which had never hedged. That revenue funded his first four years while he "accumulated a grubstake of $5,000… $9,000" and lost it — "I burned out probably two, three, four accounts."

**The first trade — and its lesson.** A friend named John, a pit trader, was "so bullish beans" at ~$5.10; Brandt bought a contract, beans went to $5.20 then tanked to $4.90, and he bailed with a $0.20 loss in under a week. Days later he met John: "Wasn't that a fabulous move?" — John had been trading for a $0.02–0.03 scalp. The lesson: **"bullish" and "bearish" mean absolutely nothing** without a timeframe, an entry, an exit, a definition of "wrong," and a sizing plan — and your idea horizon must match your trade horizon.

**Charts.** In 1979 a friend bought him **Edwards and Magee's *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*** — "the book that changed who I was as a trader. I gobbled that book down." He kept charts by hand — graph paper from the art store, black fine-point pen, ruler — then bought Commodity Research Bureau chart books at the newsstand monthly because he was too cheap to subscribe. He gives trading five years to "really have a clue": "you're paying for a very serious education. A PhD in markets."

**Factor Trading (1980/81).** He left Continental Grain, handed his Campbell's business to his mentor, and started Factor Research and Trading Inc. as a proprietary firm on the 15th floor of the Board of Trade. Three lucky foundations he names: (1) bill-paying income from his hedging clients, so trading profits were never needed for groceries; (2) a safety net — a promise from ad-agency head Dick Needham that if he blew up he could return at a 25–30% raise; and (3) **the mentor** — a former top Cargill trader (Dan Markey) who became family, loaned them their first house, and "would just spill risk management" endlessly. From him came the job definition, the loss-timing rules, and the dominant factor theory.

**The Thanksgiving trade.** Early on, he caught a huge move in Swiss francs and D-marks — which he attributes to providence, not skill. He put on a big dollar/Swiss position Wednesday before Thanksgiving; London ignored the move Thursday; he talked a banker at his London clearer into giving him a D-mark position while "laying in bed Thanksgiving Day"; by Monday it was a winner. His own verdict: "It was way too big a position… irresponsibly big. But it went my way."

**Swing trading defined.** He settled early into being a **swing trader taking roughly 10% of the underlying value out of a market**: "my trading has always been between the 30-yard lines. I leave the goal posts to the 20 other people; I just want a chunk in the middle." Never top or bottom picking — "I'm a position taker." Early on he took 25–30 signals a month; he cut that to the current **5–8 per month, 50–100 trades a year**, holding days to weeks (a four-month dollar/Singapore position was the career outlier).

**Technology and the lake years.** Two changes in 1982–83 revolutionized the business: CBOT members could hold seats without being in the building (commissions fell from dollars per side to quarters), and the small satellite dish brought quotes to a personal computer — ending his $1,000-a-month AT&T phone bill. In 1983 he moved his family to a lake in northern Minnesota and traded from there — an upstairs position he credits for surviving the transition that killed many pit traders.

**Burnout and the ten years off.** Around 1995, 24-hour markets broke him: band-alerted overnight across five to seven markets while living on the lake, "you're not sleeping… I said, I can't do this anymore." He walked away entirely — no quote machine, money in stock mutual funds, a decade of nonprofit work, a move to Colorado. In 2006 he asked his wife: what about commodity trading again? Returning meant re-learning electronic order entry and trading against HFTs and algos — and asking honestly whether 1980s charting still worked. The answer: **+80% in 2006, +100% the next year.** Some things had changed (the false-breakout adjustments in Part II), but the patterns held: "charts basically show you how humans think, and that doesn't really change" — you can find working patterns on 300-year-old charts.

**The book.** Wiley pressed him for years; he agreed and titled it *12 Months in the Life of a Commodity Trader*. Five months in, lightning struck his Colorado home — no backup, manuscript and computer gone ("even risk managers miss some risks"). He retreated to his father-in-law's townhouse near Santa Barbara to catch up; old neurological problems (from a 1984 accident) flared and he ended up in Mayo Clinic. The book became *Five Months in the Life of a Commodity Trader* — published as *Diary of a Professional Commodity Trader* — and spent **25 straight weeks as Amazon's #1 finance book**, "the surprise of my life." He still won't put it in his ten recommended books: "I don't believe that you can read somebody else's approach to selecting trades and have it be successful for you."

**Twitter, Factor, and the exit plan.** Wiley plugged him into Twitter for the book promotion; between Twitter and StockTwits he gathered ~110,000 followers ("who are these people?"). In 2014 his journaling became the Factor weekly report — an education vehicle reflecting his real positions, not a signal service. And his announced plan: a 2020 exit from his public voice, after which he would throw away the daily charts, look at charts only on Sundays, **cut his size in half, and trade about 20 times a year**. What drives him now is not money: "It's the pursuit of excellence… trying to find a trade where I'd be able to say, I wouldn't do anything different on that trade. From start to finish, that's the trade I wanted." He pinches himself: "I will be 70 years old this year, and I've been able to support myself this whole period by being a market speculator? Really?"

**The worst trade.** His single worst result ever was an **eight-fold dollar loss in the crude oil market** — the price of not being alert on a trade. He has watched the full psychology a hundred times: a small loss becomes "I'll get out at break-even," becomes "I'll get out if it hurts a little less" — "and when they get out of it, please let me know, because that will be when the market turns."

## Appendix A — Key Rules and Frameworks (Quick Reference)

**Risk and sizing:** max risk ~1–2% of capital per trade (typical 70–80 bps; range 30–150 bps); 5%/trade = ruin for an active trader; sizing back-computed from entry and invalidation point; yardstick 6–12 futures contracts per $1M; 10–20% margin equity vs designated AUM; max ~20% of AUM at any one clearer; stops in from entry, to break-even ASAP, trailed actively; three-day trailing stop rule after 70% of target; protect popcorn trades from round-trips; never a loss home Friday, never two losing days straight; two attempts per market, then move on; contingency order bundles (entry + stop + target), never naked; alerts by email/text; stop-close-only and day-only orders where HFT is a risk.

**Charting:** Schabacker's conditions are cardinal; weekly charts for opinion, daily for timing, never monthly; trade horizontal patterns (H&S top/bottom/continuation, ascending/descending triangles, rectangles); avoid diagonals (trend lines, symmetrical triangles, wedges, channels — win rate would drop from ~42% to high-20s/low-30s); exception: flags and pennants inside strong trends; duration sweet spot 10–26 weeks (multi-year = context only); ~2 valid patterns per market per year, unsure = ignore; breakouts judged on closes (Friday close most important), lean weekly over daily, distrust intraday spindles (HFT stop-running); patterns morph and failures inform; move target 5–20% of underlying; always carry a target, run to ≥70% of it; secondary tools: COT at record extremes, 14-day simple MA, ADX, analog years; banned: Elliott Wave, cycles, indicators, Gann, Fibonacci, seasonals, "Star Wars" charts.

**Metrics:** gain-to-pain (Σ monthly returns ÷ Σ |negative monthly returns|; 1 good / 2 excellent / 3+ world class); profit ratio (same per trade; his >3–3.5); Calmar (avg annualized return over 3–5 yrs ÷ worst drawdown; 1 good / 2 very good / 3 world class); MAR >1; annualized return and Sharpe = junk.

**The statistical reality:** win rate 41–42% → default assumption: the next trade loses; of 50 trades, 6–10 create the year's profit, 42 wash out; year shape = 3–4 meaningful months (500–1,000 bps), ~4 treading water, ~4 white-knuckle; always either in a drawdown or recovering (true of the S&P since 1986 too); "two steps forward, one step back"; worst-ever peak-to-valley giveback 35%; assume your worst drawdown is yet to come; "you never meet an old bold trader"; audited ~41.6% average annual return, 1981–2010.
