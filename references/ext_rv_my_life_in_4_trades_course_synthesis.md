# My Life in 4 Trades (Real Vision) — Consolidated Episode Notes

**About this series.** In *My Life in 4 Trades* each episode invites one investor to tell their life story through four formative trades — the best and worst decisions that made them. The guests span macro legends (Raoul Pal, David Rosenberg, Dan Tapiero), commodities and vol specialists (Diego Parrilla, Tony Greer, Mike Green, Nancy Davis), fund founders (Anthony Scaramucci, David Rubenstein, Harris Kuperman, Sa'ad Shah, Ben Harburg), and practitioners of a different cut (Denise Shull on trading psychology, Dee Smith on geopolitical risk, Andrew Rosner on digital assets). Every episode's biography, the four trades with their setups/sizes/outcomes, and each guest's principles are preserved below; subscription promos and small talk are stripped.

Episodes are ordered to open with the classic macro names and close with the founder interviews.


## Raoul Pal — My Life in 4 Trades
Raoul Pal is the co-founder and CEO of **Real Vision** and author of the institutional research service **Global Macro Investor**, with about 34 years in markets at the time of recording (episode originally aired November 2022; republished February 16, 2024), interviewed by Maggie Lake. By format agreement, his two "best trades" are **other people's trades** he watched from a front-row seat; his two worst are his own. The declared through-line of his career: the journey **from information to knowledge to wisdom**.### Background: Windsor, India, and talking his way inPal grew up outside London near Windsor—"idyllic": a cul-de-sac of 10 houses with 15 kids, all captains of every team. He couldn't compete with them and concluded the only competition worth having was with himself ("I can't even bear board games"). At 11 the family uprooted to **India** (his father is Indian; the 1980 recession prompted the move) and returned 18 months later realizing they'd never fit in there either—the origin, he believes, of being comfortable outside his comfort zone and of treating life as a journey. He watched failure around him carefully "and made sure it didn't happen."His father was European marketing director for **Xerox**; Raoul was a child of the 1980s—red braces, Porsches, Thatcher-era wealth made finance feel like the cool future. He studied **economics and law**, graduated into the 1990 recession with **100 rejection letters** (he still has them), and got the decisive advice at a friend of his father's birthday party: work for a great marketing company like Mars and they give you free Mars bars; work for a bank and they give you free money—**"Same job—sales and marketing. One pays a lot more."**"I'm a big believer in manifesting your own destiny—I see it and make it happen." He fought in via **Telerate** graduate training, was promoted to salesman fast, and pushed onto a trading-desk track at **James Capel** in **equity derivatives**; his boss resigned six months later and he became the boss. The dealing room overwhelmed him at first until he discovered **charts** as his visual cue (he had trained traders in technical analysis before ever entering a bank). When Capel skipped the bonus after a great year, he moved his whole team to **NatWest**—just as NatWest hired 120 people from Morgan Stanley's elite equity-derivatives team and his head-of-desk job evaporated. Asked what he wanted to do: **"Hedge funds."** He handed over the list of the world's ten largest; his boss said "come to New York next week, I'll introduce you to all of them"—**Paul Tudor Jones, Louis Bacon, Long-Term Capital Management, "the whole lot."** "That was the start of my career." (His "secret weapon" was the unpronounceable first name: "Can I speak to Paul?" / "It's Raoul"—assistants passed him through on first-name basis, so everyone assumed friendship. "A genius piece of marketing.")He rode the derivatives wave—S&P futures, stock and index options—and as "the hedge fund guy in Europe" at **Goldman Sachs** serviced the greats: "my daily phone calls were Paul Tudor Jones, Stan Druckenmiller… Julian Robertson." He then ran the **global macro fund at GLG Partners**. He retired from money management—"I didn't like managing money for other people; the pension and fund-of-funds complex forces shorter time horizons and lower volatility—it becomes about gathering assets, not performance—and I was dead right: the industry imploded through lack of performance." He moved to Spain, wrote **Global Macro Investor**, called the 2008 crisis ("a lot of the people from The Big Short were clients"), and co-founded **Real Vision in 2014**—with the Bitcoin logo in its first-ever video.### Trade 1 (best—Louis Bacon's): Shorting South Africa through the financial rand (1998)Context: the **Asian Financial Crisis**. Thailand devalues, tipping a rolling sovereign-debt crisis across Asia; currencies, stocks and bonds collapse; European banks that lent into Asia are under stress; LTCM blows up. "Hedge funds are starting to figure out what's next—there's always a **daisy chain when it comes to leverage**: who's going to blow up next?" Moore Capital, Louis Bacon's shop, was Pal's biggest client by a long way.The entry, from Pal's seat on Goldman's equity-derivatives desk: a call from Moore's head of trading (Chris) mid-chaos—**"Raoul, sell South Africa,"** phone down. No instrument, no size, no price ("just f—ing sell South Africa… don't sell the futures contracts, sell the stocks… I don't give a s—" about the index). **Five days** of relentless selling into an illiquid market—"how much you done?" every ten minutes—collapsing the South African stock market. A month to six weeks later, the same messy process in reverse over three to four days. The position was on the order of **half a billion dollars** (Pal isn't certain of the exact size); the equity leg made about **8%**.Pal's reaction: "What did Louis just do? That seemed like a complete waste of time and effort." The answer: **"No—we made 58%. Best trades we did all year."** The real trade was **shorting South Africa's currency**. Across crisis Asia, central banks hiked rates to punish currency speculators, so borrowing currencies to short them cost 20%+—South Africa's shortable (commercial) rand cost "call it 27%" to borrow, making the classic short marginal, while the non-fungible **financial rand** couldn't be shorted directly at all. Moore's insight: **shorting South African stocks cost only about 0.5% to borrow—and the stock-borrow mechanics gave them a short position in the financial rand**, the currency nobody else could touch. **It fell 50%.** Nobody else had figured out the funding workaround; Moore saw the plumbing and executed superfast, before anyone understood what was happening.The lesson Pal kept: **"The game that you see being played is not the game. The world's best investors think two or three steps ahead, at a speed and understanding that is breathtaking."** The expression matters as much as the idea—shorting stocks was the *vehicle* for the currency position, not the trade itself.Same fingerprint, as a coda: when the UK auctioned **3G mobile licenses** for billions, Bacon instantly reasoned that every European government would copy the auction and bankrupt or severely stress every telco—and called Pal: "How the f— are we short telcos in Europe?" There was no tradable index, so Pal called **Jason Good at Deutsche Bank**; they launched the European sector-swap business, and **within about an hour Moore had sold a billion dollars of European telcos**. "He's really fast at assessing huge amounts of information and making the best expression of the view."The synthesis rule came from GLG boss (and still-mentor) **Noam Gottesman**, delivered when Pal got "carried away doing weird option structures": **"If you want to scratch your ear, you don't put your arm all around your head—you just scratch your ear. If you want to go long the euro, buy the euro."** The target zone: **"a sweet spot between seeing something nobody else is seeing yet, and still giving yourself a high probability of success"**—between the too-obvious and the over-engineered (a chronic option-trader failure mode).### Trade 2 (worst—his own): Overstaying the bear, 2009Setup: after calling the financial crisis with his **ISM-based business-cycle framework**—"made a lot of money for my clients; made my whole reputation on that"—Pal watched markets bounce from the spring of 2009 while his mind demanded the logical conclusion: banking insolvency, pension wipeouts, barter (Argentina 2004), a 1929–33 replay—"even though the market's down 50%, it can go down another 50%, and another 50%." Then came **QE, TARP, fiscal stimulus**; his own indicators bottomed and turned up. He constructed "a narrative that the business cycle would roll over again and make a new low. **I had no evidence to support that. It was emotion**"—plus post-2008 hubris: "I thought I was invincible… imagine the glory if I got it right again."He held his shorts, added, and recommended the short publicly—"in 2009 you either did really well or you got absolutely nuked. **I got absolutely nuked.**" Worst year he'd ever had, personally and for Global Macro Investor, and "a lot of clients got murdered by it."The confession generalizes: **"It's when you impose your will on the markets—what you think should be, not what is."** He distinguishes being early, being wrong, and the **emotional override of a sound framework** ("I'm currently quite wrong on bonds, but my macro framework tells me it's the best risk-reward position—that's not an emotional position"). Macro people self-identify with their bear calls because bear markets pay fast—"we use leverage, go for the kill, then walk away. The lesson everybody learns is when to walk away." The warning he'd received and ignored: in mid-2002 he wrote to **Paul Tudor Jones** about his post-9/11 bearishness and got back one line he has never forgotten—**"I agree, Raoul. Just be careful. Don't outstay your bearish welcome."** In 2009 he outstayed it, wanting to be right.The GLG coda that taught him hubris economics: after strong performance he negotiated a bigger cut of performance fees from Gottesman—"and I then had a terrible year." He offered to forgo the bonus; Gottesman paid it anyway; Pal declined as a matter of honoring the deal, and left. "Every time I've started to believe my s— smells of roses, I've had my nose rubbed in it."His **recovery protocol**: get back to intellectual honesty (it takes a while; your head isn't right); **take little risk and calm down**; admit the thesis was wrong; find a new thesis—**"but don't force it"**—the Kyle Bass trap of forcing the next narrative after one legendary trade. He was gun-shy in 2010, decent in 2011, and great in 2012 (the European sovereign crisis, "and I didn't overstay"). Tactical breaks count: at GLG, when a stretch stopped working, they'd **close everything out**, flat, and rebuild.### Trade 3 (best—the Tudor trader's): Limit-long Eurodollars, then Majorca (2001)Pal calls this, with the Bacon trade, the best macro trade he ever saw. The setup: **January 2, 2001** (around the illiquid turn of the year)—the Fed makes its first cut; **ISM below 50**; growth weakening; equity bubble and debt problems. The market prices maybe **75bp of cuts**. The trader's hypothesis, pure macro thinking: **the Fed never cuts just once**—into this, they'll go **300–400 basis points**. Knowing the path and the clock (recessions run roughly 12–18 months; stocks had already peaked), he chose the purest expression: not the "lazy trade" of shorting equities, but **long Eurodollar futures**—the best leverage and best risk-adjusted way to own falling rates out to year-end. He went "**massive, irresponsibly long**," limit-ordered into the illiquid window—**and flew to his holiday home in Majorca**.The management was the lesson. The trade worked almost immediately (the luck, Pal says, was the **speed of the first move**—an early profit meant no retracement ever took him to negative P&L, so he couldn't be stopped out). Then he **did nothing**. Colleagues asked when he was coming back to the office: "I've got one trade to do. The Fed is going to cut several hundred basis points and I'm going to clean up." By June he had the biggest P&L at Tudor—and then Eurodollars retraced 75bp on the "is the worst over?" bounce and he gave a chunk back. The summoning call: Tudor and **Mark Horton Berry** in London—biggest P&L of anyone, huge risk on, your bonus is going out the window and it's only June: "What do you want to do?" His answer: **"I need to double up."** Paul: "Fine—you know what you're doing. I'll double your risk limit, but you're playing with your own bonus here. If you get back to flat, you're out." Back on the plane to Majorca; **not a single other instrument traded all year**. Around November he closed the position—*before* the move finished (rates kept falling into 2002)—took the profit, **quit, and retired**.Pal's distillation: **"Take the purest expression of the view when you know the probability is so far in your favor that you have to be greedy."** Knowing when to be greedy is the skill—he cites Soros's *Alchemy of Finance* and Druckenmiller on backing yourself when you're in the groove—and he warns that **chasing the feeling** ("I want to do that again") bankrupts people.He ran the template himself: **"Buy Bonds, Wear Diamonds," 2019**—the Fed had over-tightened into a weakening economy (the pivot signaling through 2018), so the moment they cut, buy bonds. He held through the frustrating no-man's-land because forward-looking data stayed weak ("I'm not trading my emotion; I'm using my stuff"), and in January 2020, seeing the pandemic reach Italy, wrote the Global Macro Investor note titled **"Panic"**—same logic. Skill or luck? **"Both. The luck is usually the speed at which it happens"**—preparation creates the position; speed determines whether you can survive holding it.### Trade 4 (worst—his own): Bitcoin 2013–2020—right thesis, wrong trade managementContext: after the 2012 Eurozone sovereign crisis and Cyprus's bail-in ("a terrifying time"), Pal knew debt burdens hadn't gone away. A friend, **Emil Woods**, pointed him to Bitcoin. In **March 2013** he wrote what he believes was the **first macro-economic strategy paper on Bitcoin**: valuing it gold-style via **stock-to-flow logic at roughly $1,000,000 per coin** (gold ~$1,300) against a **$200** market price. Knowing his own fallibility, he applied what he calls the **"Raoul-is-an-idiot discount" of 90%**—still $100,000: "the best risk-reward I've ever seen in my entire career. It could go to zero, but it's an option." He bought with a five-plus-year time horizon—"close my eyes and see where it ends up."What he got right: the thesis and the nerve to size it. It doubled in two weeks, went 5x, crashed 85% back to his $200 basis—**"I've never lost a penny in Bitcoin"**—and he ignored it entirely (the account at **itBit** in Singapore, built by Woods and Charles Cascarilla, sat untouched; "I don't even know the password"). By 2017 he was up **10x at about $2,500**—and here the "worst trade" begins: **newly divorced and owing his ex-wife half**, and spooked by the **fork wars** he didn't understand ("when you don't understand it, you close it"), he sold. It went **another 10x, to $20,000**. (He did call the December 2017 bubble in real time—good process, but the position was already gone.)The rebuild: **Dan Tapiero** pestered him through 2018–19; Pal finally interviewed him on Real Vision and "he completely opened my mind." Thesis restored—"the next recession, Bitcoin's going to be something"—Pal watched the price coil in the wedge pattern he loves (touch bottom, top, bottom, top, then pull back into the middle of the range and explode). In **March 2020** it plummeted into the middle of the range: he bought with **all available cash** (closing Eurodollar futures and gold positions) at **about $6,500**—"irresponsibly long… by far bigger than my original bet." It ran to **$60,000**; he rotated into **ETH**, "a great call… made a lot more money."The post-mortem is the payload. When he finally did the math: had he simply **held the 2013 position**, he'd have made **5x more money**; had he **added a fixed amount each time price tagged the bottom of the logarithmic channel**, **25x more**. The missing framework—since absorbed—is **exponential adoption: logarithmic trends, Metcalfe's law**. A technology being adopted at scale does *not* mean-revert to a price level; it mean-reverts to **the bottom of its log channel**—"so buy the channel when it gets oversold, unless something changes the thesis." The error is ancient: "We're all mean-reversionists—boom equals bust—but it does **not** in technology. The 2001 Nasdaq crash was just noise around an internet adoption trend." He made the same mistake with Amazon—watched it at "a P/E of 100," called insanity, wanted to short it all the way up—"it wasn't a bookseller, it was a **network**. Once you understand that, you don't make the mistake. Same with Tesla."Rules he now trades by: **buy the massive selloff**—anything down 70% or more that is not going bust, especially in a secular theme; **hold a basket**, because "we know the direction of travel but not which names get there" (a spread of ETFs—ARK is one position among several—robotics, single stocks like **Reliance in India**); **set a 5–10 year horizon and expect violence**; judge everything as **risk-reward**: "Down 30%, up 10x—you'll take that all day. Discount me 50%—up 5x. Still amazing." And the meta-claim: the **exponential age** (AI, robotics, energy, genetics, blockchain) will not be stopped by the Fed. His gold line: four decades of "great inflation is coming, buy gold" happened, the debasement happened, and gold still went nowhere in real terms ("gold miners at all-time record lows, inflation-adjusted")—**"Am I going to own gold, or do I just buy AI?"**### Overarching principles (Pal)- **The game you see is not the game**—think two or three steps ahead, fast, and express the view in the best vehicle, not the obvious one (Bacon's rand; the telco shorts).- **The Gottesman ear-scratch**: simplicity beats cleverness. The sweet spot is non-obvious but high-probability; over-engineered option structures are where careers die.- **Don't impose your will on markets.** Distinguish early / wrong / emotional-override. Don't outstay your bearish welcome.- **When probability is overwhelming, be greedy**: one trade, purest expression, hold your nerve—early profit (speed luck) is what lets you survive the ride.- **Buy panic into secular adoption curves**; mean-reversion doesn't apply to scaling technology; manage positions to the log channel, not to a price target.- **Write it down before you trade**—advice an investor gave him at GLG: "Write your trade thesis, your entry, where you stop out, the risk-reward—risking 1% to make 5%—and where you could be wrong; then monitor it to hold yourself accountable. Doing that process alone will dramatically change your trading."- The best trade, finally: **quality of life**—friends, family, how you live rather than what you own. "Your comfort zone and where the magic happens—they don't overlap."

## David Rosenberg — My Life in 4 Trades
David Rosenberg is the founder and president of **Rosenberg Research**, formerly chief economist and strategist at Merrill Lynch (2002–09) and one of Wall Street's best-known bears. Recorded live at a Club Real Vision event in Lisbon on November 8, 2023 (published January 1, 2024), with Maggie Lake. He calls himself a "nose-to-the-grindstone, really tenacious son of a you-know-what"; the episode is most striking for how unsparingly he dissects his own failures as a manager, a father, and even a real-estate buyer.### Background: the shy kid from OttawaRosenberg grew up in Ottawa, a shy, poor student who loved hockey, in a scholastic family—mother a kindergarten teacher; father a civil engineer who worked for the federal government and taught at the University of Ottawa; two older sisters in education and nursing; a brother who taught at the University of Tel Aviv. "I was the black sheep." In grade 9 his final grade was 64% ("like a C-"), and the homeroom teacher at Hillcrest High School asked, "Are you sure you're a Rosenberg?" A grade-10 English/history teacher ("Mr. Hiva") "spoke a language I understood," ignited him, and taught him, retrospectively, the importance of communication.Grade-11 economics lasted one day: he came home describing the Keynesian model that assumes a closed economy with no government, and his father showed him his paycheck—"50% of this goes to the government. Maybe we should take something a little more practical." He dropped the course—and became an economist anyway.He enrolled at the **University of Toronto in 1979** in Commerce, intending to be an accountant ("economists are accountants with better personalities"). He did well in everything except the one course that mattered—**financial accounting**—because he couldn't make T-accounts balance. His professor, Gary Lee, said of students who can't balance books: "I tell them to go into economics." He switched, earned a master's in **international economics** graduating into the brutal early-1980s recession, and TA'd a housing and real-estate economics course. That professor's recommendation got him hired in **1984** at **Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC)**—the program-evaluation division doing cost-benefit analysis of federal housing programs. Three years, $32,000 a year—and stifled: powerful public-sector unions, layers of committees, "slackers of all slackers" who worked "11 to 2."His first great trade was a career trade: applying to every big bank on Bay Street, only the **Bank of Nova Scotia** bit—as an **intermediate economist at $28,000** (down from $32,000, lesser title, more expensive city). His start date: **October 19, 1987—the day of the crash**. His father's counsel, which Rosenberg calls his first learning lesson and still writes about: **"Sometimes in life you've got to take a step back to take two steps forward… Where do you see yourself in five or ten years? Not next year."**### The crash-day apprenticeshipHis bosses—chief economist **Warren Jestin** and assistant chief **Aaron Campbell**—spent day one walking charts up to the CEO showing the crash was a **liquidity event, not a fundamental one**: "No recession coming out of this; you want to start adding to risk almost immediately." They were right, and Rosenberg absorbed the distinction he still teaches: **liquidity crisis vs solvency/fundamental crisis**. Rule one from Campbell: never come to the office without reading **Barron's** and Alan Abelson's "Up and Down Wall Street."Within a month, senior management—expecting recession—halved the research budget; half the economics department was laid off, and a goodbye party for ten fired colleagues cemented his vow: "That is never going to happen to me. If my boss ever fires me, he'll have to feel like he's amputating his right arm." His mentor **Monica Smith** left for the World Bank, so he was running morning meetings within weeks—"sink or swim."His writing was too public-sector-academic: for weeks every report came back covered in red X's. His fix was monastic—he **photocopied everything Jestin and Campbell had written for the previous three years (15 boxes)** and over three weekends read to "get into their brain." One day a report came back with not a red mark on it. Jestin, suppressing a smirk: **"David, you finally get it."** "You shift from total lack of self-confidence to self-confidence, and it just builds momentum." He became **the first economist on Bay Street to make senior economist before age 30**.### Trade 1 (best): Starting the daily newsletter at Bank of Montreal (1998)After seven or eight years at Scotia with no promotion path ("Aaron and Warren weren't going anywhere"), a headhunter moved him to the **Bank of Montreal** as the economics department's number two. Chief economist: **Sherry Cooper**, a big Bay Street name.At a client event, **Bill Downe**—then building BMO's treasury operation, later BMO's CEO—sought him out around 10:30 p.m., single malt in hand. The verdict: everybody loves your morning meetings, but "you speak really fast—it becomes a game of broken telephone." The directive: **"Start your own newsletter. Get your thoughts from the morning meetings down on paper every single morning, blast it out. We'll market it… send it to clients and to the media."** Rosenberg's internal pushback was one lousy excuse: *what if it flops?* His conclusion, now a staple of his teaching: **"The fear of failure is a lousy excuse not to undertake a new endeavor. The most successful people I've met had a lot of failures—those were learning lessons. You don't really know what success is until you've had those."** Downe neutralized the organizational obstacle: "Leave Sherry to me.""**Rosie's tidbits**" went viral. The sales desk began taking him to institutional clients; he was in the Globe and Mail and the National Post, doing TV. In his last year there (1999) he **outranked his own boss in the Brendan Wood survey** (Bay Street's ranking poll—garbled as "Bretton Woods" in the transcript). It created friction he had predicted: "The one thing you never want to do is outrank your boss."What the trade gave him: a daily mandate for what he loves, the psychological gift of **cadence** ("If I get it wrong on Monday, Tuesday's around the corner"), and the asset that mattered more than any single forecast: **daily distribution**—the note traveled with him and made his career in New York. It never went to his head, thanks to depression-era parents who "ingrained humility" in all four kids.### Trade 2 (worst): The condo option he never exercised (2002)August 2002: a call from **Bob McCann**, one of Merrill Lynch's top executives: "Would you ever consider moving to New York?"—"For which job?"—"That's all I wanted to hear." That November, **Stan O'Neal** replaced CEO **David Komansky** and the post-tech-wreck research bloodletting cut deep: senior names on every research team were axed, including Merrill's chief economist **Bruce Steinberg**, "by far the biggest name." The HR email landed at 11:45 a.m. as Rosenberg sat at his Toronto desk—his parents were in town for lunch—and a New York number lit up instantly: "Do you remember the conversation we had in August?"Why him: months earlier, **Patrick Brady**, head of equity sales and trading in New York, had noticed the desk reading the daily—by then renamed "**Morning Market Memo**" ("I love the alliteration")—and said: "This guy writes about everything. He's a must-read for us."The move cost his family. His wife **Rachel** resisted: their nine-year-old had auditory-processing issues and a hard-won Toronto specialist (the boy has since become a doctor). McCann misread the hesitation as a money play; head honchos flew in with the package: **fly home to Toronto whenever he wanted on the firm's dime, plus a free one-bedroom condo in Battery Park City**. Rachel's verdict: take it or be "marginalized… a laughingstock" on Bay Street—and "give it two years." He stayed **seven** ("I told you, Rachel—I was never good at math").The worst-trade mechanics were pure real estate: the Merrill arrangement came with an **option to buy the condo for $400,000**. He passed. **It's worth about $2 million today.** "I had an option to buy a five-bagger—and didn't." Maggie Lake's gloss: "In New York City real estate, you never say no."### Trade 3 (worst): Sacrificing his team at the altar of the II vote (2002–09)Merrill ran a **"managing producer"** model: Rosenberg managed an economics team, but his job was production—revenue, profile, and above all the **Institutional Investor rankings** (a top-three, All-Star ranking "brings in a ton of investment banking revenue and gives the firm tremendous bragging rights"). He and strategist **Rich Bernstein** were the firm's two macro voices. He diagnosed why his predecessor had faced the knife—insufficient friendliness to the sales force—and understood the machine: **II votes are allocated by PMs and CIOs largely through the salespeople who serve them**, so "the most important thing is availability." He made himself available **24/7 to the global sales force**—derivatives, FX, fixed income, commodities, equities—endlessly traveling, writing the daily from hotel rooms and black cars on his BlackBerry. Era-marker he offers without excuse: "Mezzanine CDOs leveraged over ten times—I felt like Henry Hill in Goodfellas."It worked: **runner-up (No. 4) in II by his second year, ranked by the third**, a "gold star" bonus every single year. And it failed: every year his **anonymous 360 reviews from his own team never scored better than about 3 out of 10** (best year: 4), with comments always the same—*never here, never around*. When he showed research chief **Candace Browning** the grades in 2009, her reply: "Keep doing what you're doing on the production side. I don't care if they give you a zero."His confession, unretracted: **"I mentored nobody… I never had team meetings. When I needed them, I emailed them. 'If I need you, you'll hear from me.' Maybe that's worse—they really missed me."** He contrasts Bernstein, who trained his successor (now the firm's chief strategist), while Merrill had to go outside and hire **Ethan Harris** when Rosenberg left in 2009. Root cause, in his words: **"When the only person you trust is yourself… I didn't put enough faith and trust in them. That was a huge flaw."** The system reinforced it—commission revenues, wealth-management and external report cards—but the counterfactual was available ("other people have done it; it can be done"). The remedy: give the team real responsibility, mentor them, accept that managing "takes effort, time, and trust." Owning it, he says, put him in far better stead when he started his own business.### Trade 4 (best): Leaving New York—Gluskin Sheff and the probabilistic mind (2009)By 2009 the three boys were 9, 12 and 16; he had become a weekend dad, sometimes skipping weekends. The memory that broke him: Sunday-night bedtimes with his middle son **Jacob** (now COO of Rosenberg Research)—"Daddy, you're going to New York tomorrow morning"—and the guttural sigh. "I neglected my family the way I neglected my team… My work became an addiction. There is such a thing as too much of a good thing—all of nature is about balance."No ultimatum: "I woke up and saw the reality… I did the Yogi Berra—I saw the fork in the road and I took it." Merrill offered a Toronto arrangement and a ton more money; he refused—the job required New York, and "I know myself. I know I'd be sucked back into the vortex" (Al Pacino in Godfather III: "just when I thought I was out, they pull me back in"). Then came **Gluskin Sheff**, the swanky Toronto wealth boutique: clients concentrated in Toronto, Montreal, Vancouver and Calgary—a job with no excuse not to see family—and a move to the **buy side** at nearly 50 after a career across Scotia, BMO and Merrill ("teach this old dog new tricks").Three returns from those ten-eleven years—**"I learned more at Gluskin than in my previous 20 years combined"**:1. **The scenario framework.** At his first investor meeting (2009), co-founder **Ira Gluskin** heard the base-case forecast and asked: "What happens if you're wrong?… You don't have a Plan B—you don't have a plan." Rosenberg came back with **scenarios carrying probabilities**. He learned to move conviction along the distribution—an 80% base case nudged to 60%, adjacent scenarios swapped—"I didn't even change anything, and they'd be writing down furiously." The realization that became his signature: **"You're really dealing with probabilistic outcomes—what is the cost of being wrong benchmarked against the reward of being right."**2. **Meeting the real economy.** At Merrill he met only the PMs and CIOs who cast votes. At Gluskin (minimums of $2–3 million) he met the clients themselves—mostly **entrepreneurs**—and absorbed their cycles of failure and success. "They gave me the faith and confidence that I could actually start my own business." ## Dan Tapiero — My Life in 4 Trades: Dan Tapiero
Dan Tapiero is the founder and CEO of 10T Holdings, a growth-equity fund focused exclusively on the digital-asset ecosystem, and previously a European bond and currency trader for Julian Robertson at Tiger Management, head of macro research for Michael Steinhardt, and co-founder with Stanley Druckenmiller of the gold business GBI and of AgCoa, which became the largest private farmland REIT in the US. His family gave him awareness of banking (a grandfather was a banker in London) and a fascination with foreign currencies from extensive travel. The career spark was reading: George Soros's **The Alchemy of Finance** — "I probably read the first 100 pages of it 10 times" — and Jack Schwager's **Market Wizards**, where the **Bruce Kovner** profile clicked: a humanities major who read widely, formed a big thesis about the world, and "instead of writing a paper about it and getting a grade, he would put an investment on." His boss told him to sit somewhere on the floor and report back in a few months, so he invented his own curriculum: mornings on the trading floor, afternoons reading four to six hours. He did a month on the treasury desk and a stint on the CMO desk of **Mike Vranos**, "the CMO king of Wall Street" — famously sitting down uninvited in the empty seat next to Vranos ("Who the F are you?... Ed told me to sit here"). They wanted to make him a CMO structurer; he wanted to trade. On the treasury desk he needled a Harvard-trained bond trader — "why aren't you at a firm like Soros?" — and six months later that trader became macro execution head at **Tiger Management** and hired Tapiero as the **European bond and currency trader** at age 24, after some 20 rounds of interviews culminating with **Julian Robertson** himself. Tiger was 90–95% equity (the Tiger Cubs — Lee Ainslie, Steve Mandel and the rest), and Tapiero was in a quirky three-or-four-person new macro group, working the 3 a.m. European shift nobody else wanted. The timing put him on the front line of the **ERM crisis**.### The apprenticeship trade: shorting the Irish punt, 1992His first currency trade for Robertson was the **break of the Irish punt out of the Exchange Rate Mechanism**. The pound had collapsed against the Deutsche Mark, but the punt — forgotten — stayed pegged, even though virtually all of Ireland's trade was with the UK. The peg meant a massively overvalued currency crushing the Irish economy; the thesis was that Ireland would fall into depression if it held. At 24, Tapiero was suddenly the number-one or -two client on the buyside for Credit Suisse, JPMorgan and Goldman — "when I called somebody up, they did something," power he notes was "not a permanent position." A crafty English salesman claimed "a direct line into the central bank," and sizes went $25 million, $50 million, $100 million. When Tapiero reported that, Robertson said: "Let's do $400 million" — more than existed in the punt market; the salesman placed the entire position with one counterparty, probably the biggest commission of his career, at very large spreads. They pressured the peg, forward points blew out, the market messaged "what's going on in the punt?" — and one Saturday morning the Financial Times front page read **"Irish punt devalues 10%."** "I thought, oh my goodness, we just made $40 million... overnight." He is scrupulous about credit — "I was the mechanic changing the tire," finding liquidity and facilitating; the trade belonged to Julian and his two bosses — and he cites **Rudy Dornbusch** (the MIT economist who later served as an outside analyst when he worked for Steinhardt): macro events "never happen when you expect them to, then they happen all at once, and more quickly than you thought." He also insists it was a fundamental valuation bet, not a speculative "attack" on Ireland.### Trade 1 (worst): Japanese government bond calls, 1994 — 50% of a year's gains in a weekContext: 1993 was the bond rally that minted the golden age of macro — rates collapsing across Europe, the US and Japan; Tiger was long Spanish bonds (up 20 points in a straight line); US two-year yields fell from 8% in 1991 to about 3%; Japan's post-bubble rates dropped 500–600 basis points. In his personal account (permitted, since you "can't front-run a bond future"), Tapiero rode **long JGB call options** to a return he describes as "thousands of percent" — on a meaningful base — at leverage he estimates around **50-to-1**. He was 25, with the Central Park apartment and the Miami weekends, "maybe a little too much of a confidence boost... a little too early."The data stayed weak through 1994, so he held — but the **Fed began hiking in March 1994**, and within a week or two in mid-1994 the calls went **to zero**. He lost **about 50% of everything he had made the previous year — in one week**. "I did not say a word for like, felt like three months... nearly catatonic," re-working the trade "a hundred different times," and made essentially no trades for the rest of the year. The lesson he mined from it is the core of his methodology ever since: **"Your view and your analysis is only important versus what the market has priced."** Everything he believed about Japan in March 1993 was *more* true in March 1994 — rates had no reason to fall another 400 basis points, and it was all priced in. (Context: 1994 was a massacre — Steinhardt went from +50–70% in 1993 to −30%; the two-year went from 3% to 8% by March 1995, the largest bond correction since 1929; Mexico blew up and Bob Rubin restructured its oil revenues. And his Japan view was eventually right — rates did go to zero — "you were just early.") The structural lesson: **defined-risk instruments saved him** — the calls meant 50-to-1 leverage could only cost premium. "It made me realize the importance of having a little bit of that downside protection always, even if you have maximum conviction in your analysis."### Trade 2 (best): the Thai baht devaluation, 1997 — risk 1 to make 20After Tiger he was head of macro research at Michael Steinhardt's firm (stepping in just before Steinhardt's 1995 retirement), then joined **Sofair Capital** (name as transcribed) — at the time the largest Asian hedge fund, about $1 billion, run by brothers Michael and Philip — on a negotiated deal: his own separate account plus responsibility for the macro book of the whole fund. Early 1997: the Asian "tiger miracle" economies had boomed into 1992–93 and weakened badly, yet their currencies **remained pegged to the dollar** — a vestige that outsourced their monetary policy to the US. The Fed's 1994 hikes had sent the dollar sharply higher against the yen, dragging the pegged baht and ringgit up with it, crushing their export competitiveness. To Tapiero this was "exactly" the ERM/punt setup again.He wrote his signature ~10-page thesis memos for every currency, argued for a **$100 million dollar-long/Thai baht-short** position, and put on a **one-year dollar/baht forward** where the only cost was a **1% negative carry** against a thesis of **20–30% devaluation**: "Risk 1 to make 20 or 30. That was the best trade I'd ever seen in my career up until that point." He worked 9 p.m. to 5 a.m. to live the market (his then-girlfriend, now wife of 24 years, "put up with that schedule"). As the thesis spread — he circulated the memos, other macro funds piled in and the forward carry rose to 2%, 3%, even 4% and they still came — the linchpin he tracked was the **Thai central bank's reserves**, down to less than two or three months' cover: past a certain level of capital flight, "it was game over." On **July 4th weekend, 1997**, in a T-shirt in the cubbyhole office he shared with Morgan Stanley Asset Management (Barton Biggs's shop), the tape printed **"Thai baht de-pegs."** The fund made roughly **$50 million within a week or two**, then spent the rest of the year shorting every other pegged currency that had to follow — the ringgit, the rupiah — "it was just dominoes." Two side trades: **Indonesian rupiah puts bought at 4 volatility that went up 18 times**. His Tiger-era friend, by then at Louis Bacon's Moore Capital, did the baht "really big for Lewis"; he assumes Soros and Robertson had it too, and notes Druckenmiller told him later he'd had the baht but went the wrong way on the ringgit and lost a chunk. Sofair was, he says, the only Asian fund up in 1997, a year when regional equities fell 30–50%. The meta-lesson: he is "very concentrated and focused on opportunities that are really clear, where there's a lot of asymmetry" — and history rhymes: front-row experience with one peg break made the next one recognizable.### Trade 3 (worst): the metaverse avatar company, 2008 — a straight-out fraudAfter years of great runs (he had pivoted to agriculture and grains in 2005–06, including a **7x in three months** grain trade with a friend while at SAC Capital — his biggest short-term hit), a trusted friend — the single largest outside owner of a "metaverse" startup where users met as avatars in virtual rooms — invited him in. Tapiero, flush from 2007, liked the big-picture idea, sat with the CEO, and asked the right question: **financials, audited books**. "He kept hemming and hawing." The friend vouched ("Dan, it's fine — it all good"), and Tapiero invested for a few percentage points of the company. **A month later** the CEO was unreachable, then admitted they were shutting down — they'd never raised the rest, and Tapiero's money had gone to pay the founder back before the announcement. "You just defrauded me." The money went to zero; he didn't sue ("I don't do that nonsense — that's not a way to live happily or successfully").The lessons: don't abandon your process in a new domain; **excessive trust in a single person is not due diligence**, even when he has millions of his own in the deal; overconfidence from prior gains breeds sloppiness. He flags that the same failure mode nearly recurred 30 years later: at 10T he passed on **FTX three times**, and on BlockFi and Celsius — yet still had **2 of 26 portfolio companies** (under 5% of AUM combined) go to zero, both cases of founders misleading, both instances where he "relied on somebody internally" and "didn't do as much work as I should have myself." His resolution: "It's my responsibility. I run the fund. I never blame anybody else" — while acknowledging the balance that you must have a team (Buffett doesn't do all the work alone), so **size the portfolio so no single fraud is cataclysmic**. And to founders: don't burn bridges by lying to investors. He also distinguishes losing money to fraud from losing money by being early — the first is a moral failure, the second is the nature of venture investing.### Trade 4 (his pick): starting 10T — "the biggest macro trade of all time"Tapiero's arc away from liquid markets began with business-building: **GBI**, the gold company he launched with **Stanley Druckenmiller**, and **AgCoa**, the farmland REIT that became the largest private one in the US before its 2013 sale. The logic: "the asymmetry of the bet in starting a business is much greater than in just doing a trade," and structuring a long-term bet inside an operating business suits his temperament — sit through volatility, build a diversified portfolio.**10T Holdings** applies that to digital assets: a growth-equity fund of about **24 companies**, which he believes is the only fund of its kind exclusively focused on crypto, blockchain and Web3. He calls the growth of the digital-asset ecosystem and Bitcoin adoption "the biggest macro trade of all time" — with the caveat of "crippling volatility" (assets that go up 20x then drop 90%), a market he estimates is "**10x more difficult than trading the euro**." Hence the structure: diversified positions and equity terms designed to "optimize the upside while having a control on the downside," the direct product of his 1994 call-option lesson. His grand thesis: "the internet was the digitization of ideas and information; this is the **digitization of money and value**" — and beyond that, the "**truth machine**" (a concept he discussed with Raoul Pal in 2019): a permanent digital ledger that cannot be distorted, whose applications might even screen out the frauds that burned him twice. The space demands an interdisciplinary mind — macro, currency, monetary policy, cryptography, coding, tech — and he places himself in the company of fellow macro migrants Dan Morehead (Pantera) and Mike Novogratz (Galaxy), all of whom realized the old macro world was "a horse and buggy situation" relative to the opportunity set in the new one.### Principles- **Price versus view:** analysis matters only relative to what the market has already priced; being fundamentally right and early is indistinguishable from being wrong for a long time.- **Asymmetry is the trade:** seek setups where risk 1 makes 20 (the baht); use defined-risk structures (options, equity with limited downside) even at maximum conviction.- **Concentration on clarity:** few positions, deep work, 10-page theses written down before the trade.- **Timing is not yours to control:** Dornbusch's rule — macro never happens on your schedule, then happens all at once.- **Own everything:** as the decision-maker, "all investments are my responsibility" — no blame-shifting, no lawsuits, structure so mistakes are survivable.- **Trust, verified:** friends and teams help you scale, but excessive trust in one person — with or without aligned incentives — is the recurring way good investors lose money.

## Dee Smith — My Life in 4 Trades: Dee Smith
Dee Smith is the founder of **Strategic Insight Group**, a self-described "private intelligence agency" doing non-financial due diligence for large investors — pension plans, private equity, hedge funds and family offices — after earlier careers as a composer-conductor, international museum-exhibition organizer with front-row seats to the Soviet collapse, and intelligence-adjacent work. From age six they traveled together; by nine or ten internationally; by twelve or thirteen he had been all over the world with her — Africa, Southeast Asia, Europe — planning itineraries he handed to her travel agent. She lived to 98, still traveling in her early nineties. Second, his father — an artist and captain of two ships in World War II — had been taken all over the American West as a boy, met the elderly Plains Indians who had fought the white man, been **inducted into the Oglala Sioux tribe at 15**, and amassed (partly through his parents' purchases) a collection of about **1,200 American Indian works of art and objects of life**: "I grew up in a house full of shamanistic objects." A recognized aptitude for music — more aptitude, he says, than desire — led to music-theory study at 12 with the head of the local university, and music, "a very demanding mistress... 24/7," crowded out everything else.### Trade 1 (best): walking away from music — the bookstore testThe decision came from an honest signal he recommends to everyone: **in bookstores he drifted to natural history, science, philosophy and history — never music.** "If you listen to yourself, you will tell yourself all sorts of things. Most people don't. Our society conditions you not to." It's not interesting" to anyone else. But the magic words were "we're working with Linda Schele" — "Oh, Linda Schele. We'll come over and we'll discuss it." Hence his founding credo: **"People think the near future is going to be like the recent past. It's not necessarily going to be."**### Trade 2 (worst): Latin America venture capital, wrecked by the pesoHe left exhibitions because the career had degenerated into constant fundraising for the nonprofit, which "kept me from doing the other things" he loved. The next trade: **raising money in Europe to invest in Latin America** in the mid-1990s — "that's what we'd call emerging markets now." Mexico was booming after **NAFTA**, and his projects — a chain of movie theaters, a large parking-garage program, an information service on Mexico — were "going great guns" until the **peso collapse of December 1994** (he misremembers the year as '95 or '96, but everything changed "literally in three weeks" in late December). He had an apartment in Mexico City, flew down Mondays and back Thursdays, loved the city — and lost a lot of his own money. His verdict: "It wasn't a bad trade in that I began to hate it. The market circumstances overwhelmed me... it wasn't really possible unless you had a very, very long horizon, which I didn't." The deeper takeaway reinforced his worldview: macro shocks are nobody's forecast, and when they hit, every plan is on hold.### Trade 3 (best): Strategic Insight Group — private intelligence for investorsAfter Mexico, he consulted everyone he knew, including **the head of the Defense Intelligence Agency**, who told him: "You know how to do this kind of research. You should start a private intelligence company" — information once available only to governments was becoming available in the private sector, with vast searchable databases being assembled. "It's a time like no other." Founded around 1996, the firm first did a wide range of work including US government subcontracting (geopolitical and counterterrorism projects), then focused where he saw the business: **investors**.The product is **non-financial due diligence** — "we don't do anything with numbers" — deep dives for someone putting serious money into, say, Malaysia or Peru: who are the counterparties, are they on the up and up, how corrupt are they, who are they connected to, how politically exposed are they, and what happens to their fortunes if the politics change; plus **scanning risk** for exogenous factors "not captured in balance sheets and income statements and pro formas." His favorite example: an agriculture project where every financial was fine, but nobody had noticed it depended on **snowmelt from a mountain range — and the snowmelt was going away**. On why AI can't replace the work: models trained on existing data find patterns that repeat past patterns; they can flag anomalies but "can't make that leap to what does this mean, what does it say about the future." The intelligence profession has a name for the opposite failure — **"failure of imagination"** — as in the Twin Towers, where earlier attacks were not taken seriously as evidence of what was possible. And a caveat on received wisdom: Occam's razor is fine, "but if this thing doesn't fall into that explanation you're looking at, you will miss it."### Trade 4 (worst): the intelligence-industry roll-up that COVID killedThe private-intelligence industry is a constellation of small specialists like his own firm, arrayed along a continuum from pure intelligence into security and cyber. His roll-up idea: pull together a group of complementary companies that could cross-sell to shared clients — "Intelligence Inc." It "was really going pretty well — I had it all lined up, I had all the companies, we had funding" — until the **pandemic**, compounded by the **interest-rate** regime change that froze debt-driven deal-making. Dead, but with a silver lining he considers decisive: had it closed six months earlier, the structure would have carried "a debt load that would be impossible." His generalization: "So many of these things... seem really bad, really disappointing. But as things work out, you may be better off when these things happen."### Principles- **Only control what you can control** — "a tiny, tiny bit of the world. Once these macro forces take over, nobody has any control."- **Expect discontinuity:** "I expect change... whenever things are too stable for too long, I'm uncomfortable." He cites brain science for why the rest of us aren't: assuming continuity saves the brain energy — adaptive for a species that evolved in stable worlds, maladaptive now (generative AI being his example of a change everyone timed at ten years that took one).- **The conspiracy-theory diagnosis:** people "would rather believe that somebody who's against them is in control of things than to believe that nobody is, and the bus is just careening on the edge of the cliff" — which is also why authoritarianism's "promise of order" sells, though history shows the world is not controllable that way.- **Stoicism:** worry only about what you can do something about; the ancient Stoics were "very articulate" about it.- **Doors and serendipity:** the Maya workshop, the phone call to the British Museum — "when a door opens, be willing not only to look through it but to step inside."- **Most people will talk to you** if you get in front of them with something interesting and useful — most people don't approach because they're afraid of rejection.- **Letting go is one of the big secrets of life:** he is glad he lacks the compulsive "have to do it" drive — it lets him release failures — unlike the eighty-year-old dealmakers he knows who keep flying and dealing and are miserable.

## Denise Shull — My Life in 4 Trades: Denise Shull

"If you play the long game, you'd be surprised what you can get."### Trade 2 (best): turning left onto Jackson — trading, and "Freud's Path to Profits"Her boyfriend at the time, a floor trader at the options exchange who had spent three years telling her she'd be a good trader, was stopped on the street — "my life changed because I turned left onto Jackson" — by a friend announcing his group was moving upstairs to trade with full-time intraday quotes. She became a trader, ran desks, and moved to New York in 1997 — the upstairs-prop world was, she stresses, "an absolute meritocracy" (the bar downstairs was literally called Jesse Livermore's): "If you were a decent person and making money, you were respected... If you were a decent person and not making money but trying, people helped you."At the prop firm **Electronic Trading Group** — every style under one roof, from an office head who was pure buy-low-sell-high ("Kmart's down a dollar, buy 10,000 shares") to an M&A-arbitrage trader who happened to be the most emotional man she knew, once on a magazine cover for his success — she worked out her own edge: "buy the things that were going up and sell the things that were going down," i.e., **momentum**, which she didn't initially know was called that. That took her to **Schonfeld** in Chicago, where she sat next to **Dimitri Balyasny** — then clipping 300 shares at a time, later founder of the multibillion-dollar Balyasny Asset Management — using a system that displayed relative strength by industry group (see oil-service names moving: buy the group). The decisive intellectual event, though, came in **2003**: taking classes at the tiny Mid-Manhattan Institute of Psychoanalysis, she was asked to let them publish her decade-old master's thesis, and in updating it absorbed the finding that changed everything — **you cannot make a decision without emotion**. A colleague (secretly working for the Market Wizard **Linda Bradford Raschke**) got her an editor, and she negotiated the fee for an article from zero to $250 — "enough to go out for a nice dinner in New York City with wine" — then $500. **"Freud's Path to Profits"** ran in *Stocks, Futures and Options* magazine in **December 2004** — and strangers began calling to ask if she would coach them. The consulting business was born from that single byline.### Trade 3 (worst): the Verso private placement — rationalizing past the gutHer trading friends ran private placements in tech and biotech and let her tag along; an early one turned $10,000 into roughly $40,000. Then the successful CEO of one placement launched a new company, **Verso**, and over **2003 she put in about $75,000**. She now calls it "misusing my psychoanalytic background": because people repeat their patterns, and the CEO had succeeded before, she was "sure that CEO would do it again." The stock went from around 50 cents to $5–6; **her friends sold; she didn't** — and at one point the position was worth **seven figures**. A board member was selling stock — "red flag, red flag, red flag" — and she accepted the board member's rationalization. She even consulted an intuition coach (the writer Flavia, whose article told of George Soros using back pain as an exit signal) around 2005–06 to decide whether to get out — but "the truth is I wasn't listening to my gut. **I was rationalizing because I didn't want to take the loss**" — money-wise, she specifies, not ego-wise. She did what everyone does: more research, meetings with the company, every earnings call. Verso **went bankrupt in 2008**. Total loss. The lesson, now embedded in her coaching — ask yourself "what do you really, **really, really** think?" — is that the first answer is usually the right one and usually isn't what you want to be true: "If I did that today, I'd be out of it in five seconds."### Trade 4 (worst): the *Billions* meeting — bait, switch, and erasureIn **2012** she appeared on Squawk Box, interviewed by **Andrew Ross Sorkin** — already, unknown to her, working on the pilot of what became *Billions*. In **August 2015** Sorkin emailed asking a favor: an actress playing a performance coach at a hedge fund would like to talk. Her intuition fired on the anomalies: instead of a simple introduction, Sorkin immediately looped in showrunners **Brian Koppelman and David Levien**; then Koppelman emailed at 11:30 on a Friday night and again at 10 Sunday morning — the deadest weekend of the New York summer. "They apparently really need me. And I was like, well, I don't need them — I should charge them thousands of dollars." Her lawyer (an old acquaintance of Koppelman's from the music business) told her to go in and "he'd make a deal." What she walked into was, she says, a **bait-and-switch**: the actress (Maggie Siff) absent for hours while the showrunners wrote down, verbatim, everything she said about traders playing out their childhoods in markets. The tells converged when she mentioned her book is deliberately structured as a fictional story with herself as a character — "Brian Koppelman's head blew off." As she later pieced together: a fictional form raises copyright exposure versus a self-help book, Koppelman had already been sued over *Rounders*, and he realized on the spot that Sorkin had used her book. Instead of the deal Showtime's business side seemed to want, she believes the creators' settlement with the network included **keeping her off CNBC permanently** — "erase me out of the financial world" — so viewers would never connect Wendy Rhoades to her. The fallout: all her CNBC relationships vanished, a pending *New York Times* profile collapsed ("you're just not that interesting," said the reporter), and she lost access to the traditional financial press — while, she says, a Supreme-Court-winning IP lawyer told her she had "more evidence than he'd ever seen" and a writer and a CNBC producer privately confirmed her account. Her framing of the mistake is the same as Verso's: **overriding her intuition in pursuit of the shiny object**. She knew what to ask for — a paid engagement, a simple letter of understanding; they were desperate (they'd even consulted Tony Robbins, who had once done work for, she thinks, Paul Tudor Jones — but "no idea how to write this female hedge fund performance coach"). "I trusted the lawyer. I should have listened to myself." And she decided not to let the erasure stand unanswered.### Principles- **"It's not the emotion, it's the action":** the science says you cannot decide without emotion — so the pop-trading prescription of clamping down, "make a plan, trade the plan, be disciplined," fails, because the suppressed emotion simply gets acted out later. The M&A-arbitrage guy screaming and jumping at his desk was the most successful trader in the building.- **Intuition is data:** you are always predicting a future emotion; your gut's first read — elicited by "what do you really, really, really think?" — deserves a hearing before the rationalization machine starts producing "more research."- **The worst trades rhyme:** Verso and *Billions* were the same trade — ignoring the anomalies (the looped-in showrunners, the weekend emails, the selling board member) because the shiny object was attractive.- **Perception is leverage:** "anything you want in life, you can get more of through a different approach to your so-called negative emotions." Fear, frustration and disappointment have purposes; a culture that mandates optimism and gratitude piles guilt and shame on people who feel them — her explanation for the mental-health crisis, and the message she takes from clients like Olympic snowboarder **Lindsey Jacobellis**, who understood why she'd snatched silver from gold in 2006 and won two golds in 2022.- **Play the long game:** the torn-up IBM rejection letter is the template — don't let someone else's profile of you decide what you attempt.- **Trading is "the hardest mental game on the planet"** — the market can go from 80 degrees to a blizzard in an afternoon, and most of its casualties are casualties of a wrong model of their own minds. Solving that is, she says, her calling.

## Diego Parrilla — My Life in 4 Trades: Diego Parrilla
Diego Parrilla is a Spanish mining and petroleum engineer turned options, volatility and tail-risk specialist, and managing partner at Quadriga Asset Managers in London. He started on the sell side at JPMorgan in London in the mid-to-late 1990s trading FX and commodities, then worked at Goldman Sachs and Merrill Lynch in macro commodities/FX, moved to the buy side (his own firm plus stints with BlueCrest and DiamondBack), and has lived the Madrid → New York → Singapore → London arc. He is the author of two books — the energy-markets bestseller *The Energy World Is Flat* (which first brought him to Real Vision around 2015) and *The Anti-Bubbles*. Hosted by Maggie Lake; recorded at a Club b by Real Vision event in Lisbon on November 8, 2023; published January 3, 2024.### The organizing framework: four stages of competenceParrilla deliberately structured his four trades around the **four stages of competence**, because he sees a trading life as a learning path:1. **Unconscious incompetence** — you don't know that you don't know. "The most dangerous one."2. **Conscious incompetence** — you know that you don't know.3. **Conscious competence** — you know what you know and what you don't.4. **Unconscious competence** — mastery; things come naturally, "you just know."Key points he draws from the framework:- **We learn in silos.** You can be unconsciously competent in sports but unconsciously incompetent in economics or politics. Much of his intellectual work is about **bridging those silos** — e.g., explaining market volatility through fluid mechanics, where nature and markets alike transition from a **laminar regime to a turbulent regime**. He considers analogies (he uses endless sporting ones) a powerful way to explain concepts: he can explain a market problem to his mother in cooking terms, and she will ask back exactly the right question — which would never have happened in market jargon.- **The emotional thermometer maps onto the stages.** Unconscious incompetence comes with **anger**; conscious incompetence with **frustration**; conscious competence with **acceptance**; unconscious competence with complete **indifference** — you make money, you lose money, there is no emotional spike. Your dominant emotion when a trade goes wrong diagnoses which stage you are actually in.- **Decision versus outcome matrix.** You control decisions, not outcomes. Good decision → good outcome, and bad → bad, are fine. The dangerous cells are a **good decision with a bad outcome** (generates frustration) and, even worse, a **bad decision with a good outcome** — like winning a round of Russian roulette: it teaches you to play again, "and eventually things happen."### Trade 1 — Unconscious incompetence: selling options for income in the dot-com era (late 1990s)**Context.** Early in his career, in the late-1990s dot-com melt-up, "everything is just going up, everyone was a genius." As a young, mathematically capable analyst he understood that options markets were paying him money for taking the risk of a collapse, so **selling options — particularly puts — looked enticing** because it seemed like it could only work one way. He was trading his own personal account.**What happened.** It worked — once, twice. Naive early success is, in his words, almost the worst thing that can happen to you: it builds confidence, you size up, "and eventually, boom, things happen." He took a hit that was a large amount for him at the time — lucky in that it was big enough to wake him up but not big enough to knock him out of the business.**The lesson (his central warning).** **Do not confuse option-selling strategies with income.** What people do is "build the house from the roof": they start with an income target — "I want $1,000 or $10,000 a month" — and reverse-engineer the notional and leverage needed to generate it. Selling options is an **insurance play and an expectancy game**; you collect premium for a while and then the tail arrives, especially if done levered and unhedged. If you must sell optionality, do it at the **right size** — which may not generate the income you wanted — and focus on the risk you are actually bearing rather than the payout you want. He has watched many friends walk into the "almost free money" temptation over the years and flags it as the trap he most wants viewers to avoid.**The FOMO connection.** The trade was fed by fear of missing out: on his desk, fellow analysts and associates were making multiples of their salaries trading on the side — "why am I bothering coming here?" He saw the identical movie again in the 2020–21 crypto mania ("guys, I've seen this") and notes these behavioral cycles simply repeat; the defense is awareness that FOMO is part of how we are wired.### Trade 2 — Conscious incompetence: the gold position and the boss's "sell it, then buy it twice" exercise**Context.** By now he was more experienced and had been given a small allowance of risk alongside the common book. He held a **gold position** he was deeply convinced about; the market refused to go his way, and the position reached the point where he went to his boss and said, "I'd like to **double the position** — because I think it turns from here." The boss asked why, then said: fine — but **sell the trade first, and then buy twice.** Parrilla thought he was crazy: why pay the extra commission and slippage? The boss insisted. He sold **10,000 ounces** — and then stood there and **did not want to buy them back.****What happened.** The moment he was flat, the question changed from "how do I defend my position?" to "do I actually want to put this position on again, fresh, today?" The answer, visibly, was no — and his boss was laughing, because he knew exactly what would happen. The doubling-up had been a purely **emotional decision**, and the forced exit is what a stop-loss does mechanically: it removes you from the trade and makes you re-justify it.**The lesson.**- **Honor your stops.** A stop is not a cost, it is the discipline that gets you out and lets you look at the opportunity with completely fresh eyes — including the fresh option of deploying the capital elsewhere, or holding cash. Skipping the "exit and reassess" step is cutting corners.- **Put discipline in writing:** set the orders in the system in advance, let them execute, then re-initiate deliberately if the idea still holds.- **Re-underwrite daily.** Some of the best desk traders he has met effectively **look at the world from scratch every morning** — "yesterday you were saying this, but things have changed." Maggie Lake notes commodity traders like Peter Brandt all describe the same reboot.### Trade 3 — Conscious competence: writing *The Energy World Is Flat* — the book as a trade**Context.** The third "trade" is the decision to write his first book. "If you want to learn, teach," he says, citing Cicero — and would amend it: **if you want to learn, write.** You are naive enough going in to think you know enough to share the message; the process reveals how much more structure the knowledge needed.**The Lego framework.** He compares ideas to **Lego pieces**: after years in energy markets he had an investment thesis and hundreds of ideas of every shape, color and size scattered on the table. Writing the book was the act of **ordering the pieces** — sorting them by color, by size, by shape, by every dimension. When you do that, gaps become visible the way a missing color is visible once you lay out the rainbow.**The Eureka moment.** His thesis was the "flattening of the energy world," covering crude oil, gas, coal, nuclear and more. Partway through, someone asked: **"How about renewables? How do they fit?"** He had no answer — until he ran the question through his ordered framework, "and magic happened": renewables didn't just fit, they became **one of the strongest arguments in favor of the thesis**. He compares it to **Mendeleev's periodic table**, which left holes for elements not yet discovered — and when those elements turned up, the framework was vindicated. The surprise was genuine: he did not know the "green" piece was missing until he laid everything out.**The lesson — have an explicit investment thesis for every trade.** The deep point is psychological detachment:- If your identity is the trade, new information feels like an attack; if your identity is a **thesis**, then "it's not you that is wrong, it's the thesis," and updating becomes low-friction. Framing a position as a thesis also forces the honest posture — *I'm right until proven wrong, and here are the risks that would prove me wrong* — instead of the ego posture of "you told me to buy it."- He keeps the writing discipline permanently (hence long newsletters — "you write for yourself first and foremost") and is always running three book-length "tables" of Lego pieces in parallel. **85–90% of the work is ordering and researching the ideas**; once that's done, the drafting itself is fast.- His drafting trick for a perfectionist mind: build the skeleton, write bullet points, and make **every paragraph self-contained** — first sentence carries the message, then the explanation, then a punch line. You could read his books from first lines alone; that constraint is what let him move forward instead of rewriting forever.### Trade 4 — Unconscious competence: building a career around what comes naturally**Context.** The fourth "trade" is career construction itself. At unconscious competence, you are so good at something it feels effortless — and you may not even recognize it as a skill ("do you choose your dog, or does your dog choose you?"). His example: math came so naturally he barely noticed it was an edge. His wife's is an uncanny sense of orientation — she cannot believe he gets lost.**The Goldman decision.** When he chose **Goldman Sachs over a tier-two bank offer that paid significantly more**, many people didn't understand it. For him it was obvious that the **NPV of joining the right team and franchise beat the higher salary** — "I would rather play for Real Madrid or Barcelona for less money than for a tier-two team." He thinks strategically and long term about career moves, and has reinvented himself several times — engineering to finance, sell side to buy side, Madrid to New York to Singapore and back to London.**Style fits personality.** His personality stress-test: he gets stressed by open-risk positions with stops he has to watch on a screen, but is perfectly suited to **long-dated options** — "I have three years to be right. The market can say this option is worth zero. Fine, that's your view — I know my downside." Others are the exact opposite and bleed from decay but are comfortable day-trading. Neither is wrong; the error is trading someone else's personality.**On teaching mastery.** Don't confuse being very good at something with being able to explain it — but true mastery should compress: Einstein ("if you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough") and Feynman ("explain it to me as if I were five"). He loves teaching and wants to be a professor eventually; when he teaches the four stages of trading itself, the progression is: (1) **wanting to be correct** ("why are you buying this?" — "because it's going up"); (2) understanding **expectancy** — probabilities and payoff symmetry; (3) **position sizing** — even a positive expectancy can be destroyed by wrong sizing; and (4) **portfolio construction** — his football-team analogy: value in the whole team, not just the striker or the goalkeeper, plus the **rebalancing alpha** a constructed portfolio harvests. Learning itself is **asymptotic**: you never reach 100%, and at the top it is a game of tiny differences — Djokovic versus Medvedev in one match can come down to a single ball on the line; in investing, it's sizing and the exit.### Parting advice- **Find the trading strategy that fits your personality.** Not everyone can play like Federer; some are Naturals like Rafa, some fighters like Djokovic. Take traits from the greats, but be yourself.- **If you feel unconscious incompetence in biotech but genuine love and skill for it, that's more likely to make you money than pretending to trade oil you don't understand.** At the same time, deliberately **expand your circle of competence** — with the caveat that the wider you go, the thinner the edge per area.- **Control risk first and foremost; watch position sizing; it's a game of staying alive** — protect capital and compound.- **The thermometer is your emotions.** The moment you feel anger, frustration, or even mere acceptance, there is work to do before the goal: emotional **indifference** — Federer not blinking at a net cord against him, Rafa focusing on process because outcomes are secondary and luck evens out.- On effort: **Toni Nadal's line — "working hard is a talent"** — is one he strongly believes in; but if hard work in an area never reaches flow, reflect on whether that is your arena at all ("knowing what you don't want to do" is also knowledge; his own motivation for some university exams was simply to take them once).

## Tony Greer — The Triumphs and Tribulations That Took Tony Greer to TG Macro
Tony Greer is a former Goldman Sachs commodity trader turned independent macro commentator, founder of the research shop **TG Macro** and a Real Vision Daily Briefing regular. He graduated from Cornell in 1990 — a self-described "wildly average student" — intending to follow his father, an over-the-counter trader who held one seat on Wall Street for 30 years at Dean Witter, where bids, offers and the competitiveness of a performance-based business were dinner-table conversation. His first decade was as a currency and commodity trader: Sumitomo Bank, two years at UBS including a posting to Zurich, and then through 2000 at Goldman Sachs' **J. Aron division**, where he ran the **Goldman Sachs Commodities Index** and the **gold book** and spent time on the exchange floor. Two best trades and two worst; hosted by Maggie Lake, recorded live April 5, 2022, published April 27, 2022.### Trade 1 (worst) — Quoting gold to the Swiss National Bank at UBS, 1993**Context.** At 25, Greer was the hungry kid on the UBS FX desk — first in, last out — and the bank sent him to **Zurich for six months** to work the Treasury desk trading currencies and commodities and helping risk management. Zurich in the European time zone was an eye-opener: you see the end of the Asian day and the start of New York, "a really good look at the global picture." Early in his gold-book experience, on a hectic day with gold rallying, he was managing his book long, being rewarded, having a genuinely good day.**The setup and the mistake.** Near the close, a salesman stood up: before the bell, the **Swiss National Bank** needed a quote on **a lakh of gold — 100,000 ounces**. Greer, a deer in the headlights, had not yet learned the first rule of quoting a book: **keep your positions small so you stay nimble and can provide liquidity.** He asked the desk for a read on whether SNB would buy or sell — big official producers are usually sellers, but an options trader guessed the SNB's options desk was **covering negative gamma, i.e., a buyer**. Greer quoted a **bid above the market**: if the market was, say, 25 cents bid at 75 cents offered on the handle, he bid roughly 50 cents through the figure. The SNB **sold him the 100,000 ounces** — instantly doubling his long to **200,000 ounces, or $200,000 of P&L per $1 move in gold**. The SNB asked for a fresh price on another 100,000; not knowing that was even possible mid-quote, Greer quoted **$1 lower**; the SNB passed — because it was still selling — and went **around the street hitting every other bid**. His 200,000 ounces went $5, then $10 underwater.**The four hours of hell.** From his good day to knowing his final P&L was roughly four to five hours. He thought only: *I'm going to get fired.* He asked the desk for help getting out — standard practice: "give me gold prices," everyone calls their counterparties — and liquidated roughly 300,000 ounces, finishing **at the low tick of the day**, after which gold traded up $3–4. He sat at his desk 15–20 minutes after the close contemplating his future. The loss was **seven figures**, and the Swiss-German-speaking office had little sympathy ("look at Superman over here").**The aftermath and the lessons.** The desk manager, to his surprise, was calm, acknowledged Greer had gotten **bad guidance on the desk**, and noted the desk had lost a million dollars in a day before — "the people involved in trading are actually more understanding than you think." The scar lasted a while, but the lessons became foundational:- **Know your counterparties:** "You've got to know the Swiss National Bank is a seller on a rally of $7, $8 in gold" — a big move when gold was trading in the mid-$300s and a $5–10 move mattered.- **The job of the gold book at a big bank is to provide liquidity, not to run maximum risk** — for the bank's clients, internal and proprietary traders. Even when you're bullish, **sometimes the right position is flat**, so you can quote two-way without a directional axe, buy or sell, and lay the risk off — and there is real edge to glean because your liquidity is often better than what you showed the client. Greer made it his life's vocation from then on to become a great desk dealer and quote markets properly.- **Own your P&L:** he stopped leaning on the (apologetic) salesman — if you carry the book, you carry the decisions.- **Every day is a new day.** He is a "terminal optimist" who has never woken up thinking the day would be worse; once the confirmation came that he wasn't fired, he moved on. "When you take your lumps… when you go to sleep that night, it's over. It's got to be over."### Trade 2 (best) — Short-dated S&P puts through the Great Financial Crisis, 2008–09**Context.** After Goldman, Greer ran an equity desk and a sales-and-trading operation at **Bank Hapoalim** (a large Israeli bank), where compliance restrictions meant he essentially could not trade. He left to join **Dahlman Rose**, a "maverick" natural-resources broker-dealer, working for his friend **Ernie Dahlman** and running a pod of five sales traders — and suddenly he could trade again, with loose restrictions. As the GFC cratered, memo after memo came down forbidding the desk to trade with household names on credit concerns — the same whisper network that became the Lehman story. Each memo made him **more bearish**.**The trade.** His strategy: during **retracement rallies**, buy **short-dated S&P puts** — strikes a week or two out — and sit persistent, "absurdly cheap" bids in the **out-of-the-money puts at nickel-and-dime prices** ($0.05–$0.10), accumulating whatever he could get filled on. The thesis: when the tape turned, **volatility would expand, direction would fall, and the puts would explode**. Rallies repeatedly petered out and craters closed on the lows right as his options expired — many expiring at **$1.50, $2, even $5** against his nickel entries, "absurd returns on my money."**The size and the outcome.** On leaving Hapoalim he opened a dedicated aggressive account with **$50,000**, wife fully on board. A couple of months in she asked how they were doing; "pretty good"; did he get it through six figures? — "it's a couple of them." He pulled the account up: **$475,000**. She said: keep going. Elsewhere he describes it as turning $50,000 into roughly $450,000 — made in about four months.**What gave him the conviction to stay contrarian while everyone was petrified:**- **The tape kept confirming the bear view** — rallies failing at obvious resistance levels.- **Market internals**: even on weeks the S&P rallied, the biggest stocks closed **flat on the week** — leadership was broken.- **LIBOR–OIS** ticking higher and higher — the interbank credit-stress spread saying "this trade is not over yet." (He notes it stopped working as a signal once the Fed distorted it.)- Watching **bottom-up stock pickers reiterate buys all the way down** while the market confirmed the opposite idea.He also credits being multi-asset: people who are only stock-centric never gave up the ghost. This period is when he got "much more serious" about systematic study — the origin of the process that became TG Macro. The kicker: the day he and his wife sat down and looked at the account together — the $475,000 print — **was the exact high-water mark**. He didn't know it; he was thinking about another $5 million and the cars and the pool.### Trade 3 (worst) — The same trade after the market turned: giving it all back**Context.** Same strategy, wrong regime. The market bottomed; Greer kept running the identical playbook **after things had changed**, and **upsized**: instead of $5,000–$10,000 lottery-ticket bids, he was **paying offers**, going home for the weekend **long $150,000 of puts**, later buying $25,000, then $100,000 of options in single clips — "and I've had even bigger positions than that have almost all of it expire worthless." The $50,000 that became ~$450,000 in about four months was **given back over about eight months**, down to a **$40,000 handle**, over the following year and a half.**The psychology, dissected honestly:**- **He confused a good trade with being smart.** "I was Zeus as a trader in my mind." A 10-bagger convinced him he saw what others couldn't.- **Cynicism at the bottom.** When the Fed turned on QE he dismissed it — "what the hell is that going to do… they're going to buy all these securities and they're going to what, disappear?" When **David Tepper** made what Greer now calls probably the greatest call in trading history — insanely bullish at the bottom because the Fed would rearrange the market and pump liquidity — Greer's read was: "this guy's getting on TV to pump the market so he can sell his stock." That misplaced conviction is what kept him re-loading the same trade into a new bull market.- **The capitulation call he ignored.** With the S&P around the 800 handle he asked a fund-manager friend what he thought. Answer: "Dude, it's going to get so that admitting that you have a stock at a cocktail party is going to be like admitting you have syphilis." That is what the end feels like — and that was the bottom.- **He mixed his job's income with his trading risk.** Commission business at Dahlman Rose was "raining from the sky," so a losing trading month felt fundable: "if I lose XYZ-thousand here, my commission month pays for it." His verdict now: "that is the stupidest thing I've ever thought in my life… **nothing matters except the P&L**" — your salary and your account are different scoreboards, and robbing Peter to pay Paul never works.- **He didn't loop in his wife.** She's a Goldman Sachs alumna, sharp, with good trading intuition and a track record of good outside-view advice. He regrets it: a partner in the turmoil might have said "make believe you turned 50 into 250, and let's stop now." Instead, alone, "I got myself into this mess and I'm going to get myself out." He wasn't hiding it, exactly — they just weren't talking about it — and the stress bled into everything: he was snapping at the five sales traders on his own desk.**The aftermath.** When the account hit ~$40,000 he closed it — "I'm not going to blow this 40 grand now" — and then **did not trade for four years: not one trade, not $1 of risk**. He consciously redirected the competitive energy into the once-in-a-lifetime commission franchise he was running, "earning every one of those dollars back the hard way instead of the easy way." The durable lessons: **there is no such thing as a one-off, all-in trade** — there is always another trade in five years, and the one after that, so every position must be sized and managed inside its own silo; and shake off the money quickly but keep the lesson ("you shrug off the pain and you remember the lesson").### Trade 4 (best) — Buying the oil lockdown lows, spring 2020**Context.** After **Cowen bought Dahlman Rose** (about four years into his no-trading stretch), his commission deal evaporated and he spent three miserable years at three different shops — "the pickup-basketball season of my career." Deciding that restarting his own trading would help him tune out the noise, he came back with a **written set of ground rules**: **no options** ("too toxic, too tempting"), **buy things for cash and sell them higher**, try not even to short, keep it simple. His whole method since: "I hunt trades that start in the **bottom left corner** of my screen and go to the **top right corner**, and I figure out where I can jump in in the middle." Getting his trading mojo back after the long absence rebuilt his confidence and spilled positively into his business — and this time he was determined to protect it.**The trade.** COVID lockdowns took crude to zero and toward a negative print. As a career commodity trader he knew instantly "this is just not sustainable — the world runs on commodities" under an artificial, one-of-a-kind lockdown. When oil was **$10 a barrel** on its way to negative and unleaded gasoline was around **48 cents**, he called friends on the **Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs energy desks** asking whether clients could buy their **lifetime supply of gasoline** at those prices — because he wanted to. When a friend mused that with a 40-gallon tank and oil at minus $2 the pump should hand him $80 back, Greer knew the market's mental model was broken: "there's a grave misunderstanding with what's going on" — the dislocation was the opportunity.**Management and exit.** He bought oil futures in the **"teen single digits."** It was genuinely difficult to hold. Following his new method, he took roughly **half off in the $70s–$80s** — a life-changing trade on the scale of his best S&P put days — and ran a **trailing stop on the balance**. With oil in the $80s–$90s he was mentally done, "and then **Putin invaded Ukraine and handed me a bid**," and he exited completely. The trade philosophy that separates this from 2008: **nothing is an all-in trade anymore; everything is "let's see how this goes"** — which is what smoothed out his psychology. He expects the commodity bull market that trade kicked off to run about **ten years**, and is now managing the P&L back into risk accordingly.### Principles and advice- **"If you're a beginner or intermediate trader — anything short of an expert — don't trade options. Don't lever yourself."** Let his tuition payments save you yours.- **Never risk more than 1% of capital on any trade.** Position sizes are now "really scientific," with tight parameters on risk.- **Take a partner.** The Goldman lesson for heavy-risk situations that erupt out of nowhere: grab somebody and ask "how should we look at this?" It kills the "put this trade in the drawer and hide it" instinct and smooths decision-making. "There's no reason to be alone in trading" — most of what he learned, right and wrong, came from talking to people; and home traders are alone by default.- **Study the market with open eyes instead of deciding the story first.** The old Greer read everything, formed a view, put positions on "and waited for the world to conform to what I thought." The new Greer studies the **closes — daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, yearly — intently, across indexes, sectors, macro and securities**, and since he started teaching macro, "my trading performance has exploded."- **The scoreboard is the P&L, and hubris is the tax on winning.** He calls himself money-hungry but says what he really cares about is the score — and that same competitive "I racked up more points than anybody" energy "is also the level of hubris that winds up taking you down a few notches once you get it wrong."

## Mike Green — The Dumbest or Smartest Man Alive: Mike Green's Been Called Both
Mike Green is Chief Strategist at Simplify Asset Management, and one of the most prominent theorists of passive investing's market impact. His path ran from Wharton through Bain and the Parthenon Group, a software company sold to Credit Suisse's Holt, small-cap value stock-picking at Moody Aldrich and Royce, running Canyon Partners' New York office (2006–2014, ~$5 billion), a Soros-seeded fund (Ice Farm Capital), managing Peter Thiel's capital, and the famous XIV "Volmageddon" prediction of 2017–18. His episode with Maggie Lake is organized around being called both "the dumbest man alive" and "the smartest person in this industry" — and what each label taught him about flows.### Background: a California farm, the 1987 crash, and a consulting-to-investing arcGreen grew up on a farm in Northern California, in a household where money and investing weren't discussed. Drawn to math, he started following the market at 14 or 15, read enough pre-crash commentary to convince his parents to move their portfolios to cash, and **faked being sick to stay home and watch the October 1987 crash** — "from that point, I was pretty much hooked." (Wall Street, the movie, completed the recruitment: "Charlie Sheen manages to score Daryl Hannah... that's what I want to do.") He did Wharton as an undergraduate, joined Bain & Company, left with senior colleagues to co-found **the Parthenon Group**, and in 1996 co-founded a software company, **Value Add Software**, which was sold to **Holt** — which was in turn sold to **Credit Suisse** — landing him in asset management. He picked small-cap value stocks at **Moody Aldrich Partners** (Boston), ran mutual funds at **Royce & Associates** (the largest small-cap specialist in New York), was recruited by **Canyon Partners** to launch its New York office in 2006 — building it to roughly 15 people and **$5 billion** by 2014 — then launched **Ice Farm Capital**, seeded by Soros ("not a trade per se, more of a life learning experience"), shut it down in late 2015/early 2016, managed **Peter Thiel's** capital, passed briefly through **Logica**, and joined **Simplify in 2021** to build its derivative-overlay ETF products.### Trade 1 (best): home builders in Q4 1999 — and "the dumbest man alive"At Moody Aldrich amid full dot-com glory — NASDAQ up (he believes) 86% in 1999 while small-cap value was roughly flat, "you felt like a moron compared to the peers across the street at Putnam" — Green bought **home builders in the fourth quarter of 1999** at roughly **0.8 times book**, a valuation "that reflected impending distress": the yield curve had inverted heading into 2000 (a classic recession signal) and cyclicals were under pressure. The names fell **another 35–40%** over the next couple of months, and in February 2000 a 50-plus-year-old client told the 29-year-old he was "**the dumbest man alive**" for not understanding the new economy (why Cisco deserved 100 times earnings) — that client, he adds with relish, "ended up going to jail." Green kept the insult as a career talisman for two reasons: it proves you are genuinely saying something contrarian, and if your analysis is sound, dismissal is opportunity. His thesis: the price implied a housing recession at least as deep as the early-1990s drawdown, which he judged highly improbable given relative shortages — and he predicted consolidation into large builders (public builders held ~18–19% market share when he started; ~50% today). The thesis proved 100% correct; the lambasting continued ("nobody will need houses — they'll inhabit the virtual space").Two lessons outlasted the win. First, the discipline question — "has anything changed?" — a 30% drawdown changes valuation, not analysis, and he added. But second, the discovery of **flows**: value managers were being fired, forcing them to sell home builders into a market whose marginal buyers (technology funds) wanted nothing they owned. On **March 10, 2000**, he watched NASDAQ fall 7.5% intraday while his book rose 6.5% — a ~1,300–1,400 basis-point intraday spread driven not by fundamental reappraisal but by fiduciary rebalancing after three-year review cycles. Small-cap value rose (he estimates) about **100% across the entire dot-com collapse** — and still his fund suffered redemptions, with clients exiting to "rebalance" back into the NASDAQ precisely because it had outperformed. His distilled rule: "None of it matters relative to the flows... It's when people began putting money back into small cap value that the trade began to work — and not before." Related craft: when two linked assets price opposite economies (NASDAA pricing boom, home builders pricing depression), identify what drives each and judge whether the divergence compresses.### Trade 2 (worst): Constellation Copper — checking every box, wiped out anywayThe China commodity thesis (demand exploding from ~2001 as MFN admission and post-1997-crisis payback kicked in; China structurally short copper; classic 14-year cycles needing ~6 new major mines) led him from Phelps Dodge into development-stage names. **Constellation Copper** — a near-production leaching operation he'd first bought small at Royce — needed capital at Canyon, so he structured a **convertible preferred** secured against the property, wrapping the bet in a fixed-income form Canyon could approve. He did everything right by the checklist: site visit, management meetings, feasibility study blessed. The recoveries in the **leach process** looked fixable ("just increase the acid"). But as acid application rose, recoveries didn't — the ore was **talc-rich, neutralizing the acid**, a localized chemistry failure baked into the company's refiltering assumptions. "That was my failure as an investor — even experts make mistakes in their own domain." Then the coup de grâce: China's Olympic-stimulus commodity boom sent **hydrochloric acid prices skyrocketing**, and with the GFC arriving there was "no greater fool" to buy the position. Total wipeout. His bookend story: in **June 2008**, at the commodity peak, a young hedge fund manager also invested in Constellation told him over lunch, "Mike, I think you might be the smartest person in this industry" — whereupon Green went to his desk, wrote the management team that he wanted to liquidate, reasoning that if *he* was the smartest guy and had no idea what was happening, "we're all, I think the technical term is, screwed." The liquidation was "among the most liberating experiences of my career" — he cut from **$750 million to about $150 million** — though Canyon kept him running part of the commodity book, which lost 50%.The aftermath taught the era's real lesson: a **liquidity crisis**. When he asked to redeploy post-TARP, the answer was "that money we took from you? We lost it all — and you're the only PM still liquid, so we need to take more to meet redemptions." He ended 2008 running **$27 million** — barely a business — which "liberated" him into derivatives; he returned almost **400% in 2009**, "the real story of 2009 was simply surviving." Along the way he collected the crisis's defining facts: Harvard selling private-equity secondaries at **$0.08 on the dollar** (while remaining obligated for the other $0.92), and Lehman clients discovering their "segregated" cash sat at **Lehman Brothers International** and vanished on the filing — "the conversion of cash instruments to non-cash instruments."### Trade 3 (worst): shorting LandAmerica Financial — and finding the Keynesian beauty contestConvinced housing would break, Green looked for adjacent expressions and shorted **LandAmerica Financial**, a title insurer, in spring 2006 — the logic being that title companies were taking liability-laden shortcuts in the mortgage bubble and the boom-driven business would shrink. He shorted around $75; the stock ran to **$125** — "it was basically schmucks like me who were too early" — the *Big Short* dynamic of locked doors and screaming managers, pure short covering (the same mechanics, he says, as the Tesla and Bed Bath & Beyond squeezes). He covered around $100, sweating "maybe I really just have this thing wrong," before it ultimately went to zero in the bust. The lessons stack: **being early is the same as being wrong**; and **prices rise while fundamentals deteriorate** — "the price is going higher, but this is actually not a positive" (his line on Bitcoin at $60,000). This is the **Keynesian beauty contest**: "It doesn't matter what my analysis of the cash flows is... that's just my opinion, man. If you bring billions of dollars to that opinion, you can move prices... What matters is how the money moves." His practical tool: **"who owns it?" is the first question for any security.** In 2020 he "functioned as a psychiatric counselor for bears," asking shorts who owned the names they were short — almost none had looked. "If Vanguard and BlackRock own your company, what event makes them sell? Nothing" — and their 3-basis-point funds profit by *lending* securities, which muddies the classic over-shorting signals that prime brokers used to keep clean.Inside this discussion sits the best trade of his life, entered almost by accident: a Canyon colleague wanted ~**$200 million** of **WCI Communities** (the Icahn-endorsed Florida condo builder, supposedly safe because no one walks away from $200,000 deposits). Green opposed the long but brokered insurance: buy the **ABX** subprime index, sized so a bankruptcy-level 5% index decline would reimburse the capital. The ABX didn't fall 5% — it went from par toward **65**, and the fund made **about $1.5 billion**. "Best trade of my life — and a trade I entered into without fully realizing the opportunity. I learned nothing from that trade."### Trade 4 (best): the XIV/SVXY trade — predicting VolmageddonWith Chris Cole, Green had ridden the "bull market in fear" of 2011–12 (French CDS as "the trade of the century," short Canada housing, the inevitable euro breakup) and watched retail vol-long tools like **VXX** proliferate; their pricing was so skewed versus pre-GFC norms that he became "one of the bigger vol sellers on the street." The same reverse-engineering logic ran his 2009 credit book: post-crisis mandates forced everyone to buy CDS protection, distorting its price, so he shorted CDS to synthesize long bonds with excess yield ("why buy the underlying when shorting the CDS gives me the bond plus return?"). The headline datum: as late as **June 2012**, a 10-year put on the S&P carried implied volatility above **40** — "every single day for the next ten years would have to behave like the aftermath of 9/11" to break even.By 2017 the crowd had flipped: XIV was up roughly **600%**, celebrated everywhere, with a former PM launching a fund around short volatility. At Chris Cole's invitation Green spoke at the **EQD (Equity Derivatives) conference in Las Vegas in May 2017**, arguing products like XIV would be destroyed. His math: the **beta of the VIX complex to the S&P** had climbed from a historic ~4 through 8, 12 and 20 — to about **22x** (over 24x on moves greater than 1%) — because VIX responds in *points*, not percentages, so a rising XIV requires ever-smaller S&P percentage moves to double volatility and zero out any inverse product. Reading the prospectus, he found a **force majeure clause**: an 85% single-day drawdown let the underwriter liquidate the product entirely. That was "a Big Short-type opportunity," and the vehicle was **two-year LEAP puts on SVXY** (ProShares' XIV lookalike) — options that priced a tidy lognormal world and missed the mechanic. At EQD, a VelocityShares founder shouted from the audience that Green didn't understand the product; Green predicted **zero on a 4% S&P decline** — on tape. Unusually, he warned his counterparties not to hold the risk he was buying against them ("I'm not trying to kill you"), both ethics and liquidity-sourcing; word leaked, his position became known, which cut both ways when the event came. The event — **February 2018's "Volmageddon"** — arrived, in his telling, when the Federal Reserve unexpectedly raised capital requirements against short-vol positions, triggering the scramble that cascaded XIV to zero; he watched it from the Thiel Capital trading floor. He later recognized he'd refought **Paul Tudor Jones's 1987 portfolio-insurance analysis** — read the investor letters, not the *Trader* documentary: markets cannot absorb the volumes a crash demands, which raises the probability of the crash, a waterfall feedback loop. And he saw XIV as a miniature of his **passive-investing** thesis — the path that began with a paper by Lasse Pedersen at AQR ("Sharpening the Arithmetic of Active Management").### Principles- **Flows over fundamentals:** analysis sets your opinion; money sets the price. Track who owns what, who is being forced to sell, and who the marginal buyer must be.- **"Has anything changed?"** — the homebuilders discipline: drawdowns alter valuation, not necessarily thesis; add if the facts hold, but respect forced sellers (including yourself when fired).- **Contrarian validation:** being called an idiot by the crowd is evidence you're early, not wrong — provided the work is genuinely done.- **Idiosyncratic risk is real:** even a perfect checklist (site visit, feasibility study, management meetings) dies on a soil-chemistry error; small-cap single names carry unresearchable failure modes, and in a crisis there is no buyer.- **Asymmetry on purpose, not by accident:** the ABX made $1.5 billion precisely because it was sized as insurance; the XIV puts were the deliberate version — a bounded premium for an identified, structurally forced cascade.- **Cassandra is structural:** brains are wired to ignore accurate warnings and attend to vivid ones, and "have fun staying poor" — the fear of losing relative status — "can get people to do all sorts of things," which is why bubbles run further than analysis implies.- **Kindling, then matches:** passive flows and distorted vol pricing are the kindling; the match is always something unanticipated (the Fed's 2018 capital change; in 2023, the unprecedented hiking cycle stretching someone until they "go postal" — he wants to be positioned for it).- **Love the puzzle:** he wakes at 4 a.m., calls markets "puzzle solving... what I was born to do," and notes with sadness that finance is being "morphed into something that you revile" — while taking excitement in what gets reborn from it (AI, self-driving, electrification, new energy).

## Nancy Davis — When a Little Luck and a Lot of Hard Work Pay Off
Nancy Davis is the founder and CIO of Quadratic Capital Management, manager of **IVOL**, the first long interest-rate-volatility ETF in the US market, and formerly head of credit derivatives and OTC trading on Goldman Sachs's prop desk, where she says she ran the largest options book in the world. Her May 2023 episode with Maggie Lake is a defense of the long-option, anti-consensus way of investing — plus the tale of a lucky green dress that bookended her career.### Background: barefoot in a magnolia tree, five grad classes, and the Goldman prop deskDavis describes her childhood self as "dirty and outside all the time... I spent probably a third of my life in a magnolia tree." Her father is a musician; she played many instruments and was the stand-up bassist for her high-school orchestra — "I'm about 5'3", so you picked the biggest instrument." Markets were foreign to her family ("no ties to Wall Street; I had never even been to New York until I started interviewing for Wall Street jobs"). On an academic scholarship in Washington, DC, she worked ~40 hours a week at a management consulting firm during her last two college years, and the job required learning **swaps** — her entry into derivatives, reinforced by five graduate classes (transfer pricing with swaps) taken through her honors program. She started trading options in her own portfolio in college, sent her resume in "on the internet" before LinkedIn existed, and — steered by an ex-LBO-banker professor's advice that "Goldman's the best" — joined **Goldman Sachs** right out of college, interview stress meetings notwithstanding ("I'm great at math. Bring it on"). Not from an Ivy League school, she was an outlier on the analyst and associate paths, but the **prop desk** suited her: a flat organization where "you had a number next to your name" and little politics — "a great place to be as a trader, especially for women — you can cut through the bullshit." In **2003 she was promoted to head of credit derivatives and OTC trading** for the prop desk, running the group about five years (about seven on prop, ten at the firm), before leaving for a JPMorgan hedge-fund strategy. The desk ran on superstition — **no red anywhere** (a rule she attributes to Eric Mindich), because red is the color of losing money and green of making money. She had both children at Goldman and returned after each maternity leave, and Goldman later invited her to speak at its London women's conference. Her confidence in the options world was unshakable: "I was like Madonna in the vol world."The **lucky green dress**: bought at TJ Maxx for her first Goldman interview, it reappeared in **March 2020** when the NYSE invited her to ring the closing bell — which happened to be the worst US equity down day since the 1987 crash, in stretchy green. The next day the NYSE closed its floor and went fully electronic: "I was actually the last person to ring the bell" in person.### Trade 1 (best): buying IG9 super-senior protection in 2011 — the other side of the London WhaleRunning credit at a 1940-Act mutual fund complex in 2011 — long-only, exposed like every credit fund to spread widening and rising correlation, with Greece imploding and rates at zero — Davis studied the tranche market and found **IG9 super-senior tranches absurdly cheap**: buying protection there paid off precisely when spreads widened and correlation rose, the exact scenario that would hurt the long-only funds. She campaigned to put the protection across the firm's portfolios — including appearing before boards to **rewrite the prospectuses**, since derivatives weren't permitted, persuading them it was fiduciary-sound (she still emails the former board chair). Several portfolio managers adopted it; several didn't — "all you can control is your own portfolio, then tell people and let them decide." She had no idea who was on the other side until she went to her boss and said, "I've heard these things about JPMorgan. I don't think they know about it. Can I go tell Jess Staley?" (she'd worked with him at JPMorgan's hedge fund). The day the London Whale story broke, his email arrived: "I have egg on my face. You were right. I can't believe it."The episode showcased her house style, sharpened earlier at the JPMorgan hedge fund, where she owned **Volkswagen options** because VW implied volatility — in single digits — was cheaper than dollar-yen, the cheapest single-stock vol available. She tried to talk the convert desk into converting positions into puts ("realized is 4 and it's priced at 6? A dollar a year involves 10 — this is 6. Are you crazy?"); they didn't listen, and VW's historic short squeeze paid her book. Her confession: "convincing other people is not my skill set. I have a lot of conviction — but convincing others is a skill I don't have."### The house philosophy: options as the core, not the garnish"I don't use any linear derivatives — futures, forwards, swaps go up a dollar but they go down a dollar. I only like the asymmetric ones: options. And I like to **buy** them, which is weird — 99% of the world sells options." Her thesis: everyone sprinkles options on a portfolio — selling them to expire, or buying them as hedges — "but nobody's actually trying to make money off the options. To me that's the obvious place to add alpha." A long option is her "**debit card**": maximum loss is the premium, and tenor — sometimes very long-dated — buys **staying power**, the Volkswagen lesson. It also answers her definition of the contrarian's dilemma: **the difference between being contrarian and being wrong is all timing**, and "with long options you can be wrong for a lot longer" than with linear derivatives — where, as the UK's LDI funds learned, a margin call on receiving-rates swaps (levered long bonds) forces you to sell your collateral at the worst moment. Trend-followers and CTAs, she notes, crowd the same linear liquidity.### Trade 2 (worst): Japan, the widow maker"I've tried to short Japan in all sorts of ways — payer swaptions in the rates market, JPY FX options, Nikkei, dispersion." Her verdict: "there's fundamentals, and then there's Japan." The central bank has manipulated markets for decades, deflation has persisted, and even now she marvels that Japan ran yield curve control with a positive ~50bp twos-tens curve (JGBs and JPY swaps) while the US curve stayed massively inverted — "what in the world? That makes no sense." Her only consistent success: **carry** — owning higher-yielding currencies against the yen; rates, "forget about it," and equities are distorted by official buying. What keeps pulling her back is the same trait as everything else she does: "It's cheap vol... owning cheap optionality is a little thing that keeps me coming back." Not in her 40-Act funds, she concedes, but "maybe in Nancy's private life one of these days."### Trade 3 (worst): gold — a fiat-currency trade, not an inflation hedgeAfter the financial crisis she was one of the QE-means-inflation people and held gold in her personal account from about **2009** — and "with the exception of certain periods, it really doesn't do anything." Her most clever construction: **long XAU (gold in FX form) versus the Swiss franc**, turning gold into a positive-carry position against a negative-yielding currency — until the SNB "messed that up" (contained, since it was options). She has since done the academic work and takes a deliberately provocative position: gold is "**definitely a fiat currency trade. It's definitely FX. It's definitely psychology — but it is not an inflation hedge**." Bring on the gold bugs: "I love all the hate that I get. I have done the work."### The stay-at-home chapter that proved the callingBetween firms, Davis chose to be a full-time mother for three and a half years (children aged zero to three, no nannies, no babysitters — "whatever I do, I want to do it really freakin' awesome"). In the two-hour preschool window she went to the **Greenwich Library and used its Bloomberg terminals to price structured products — mostly convertible bonds with embedded options — on the secondary market, and traded them**, while her friends had coffee or played paddle. "It was actually in that period that I realized: I really love options. When your free time answers the question for you, you've found your career."### Trade 4 (best): launching IVOL in 2019 — fixing the TIPS marketThe through-line of her career was named in her first year at Goldman, when the Treasury issued **TIPS**: "I remember thinking, that is so not going to work. They're bonds — **all TIPS are long duration**, so they all lose money when rates rise; a short-duration bond should just be called 'less long.' And the only index is **CPI, which the Fed doesn't even use**." Fixing the TIPS market became "a lifelong career passion." **IVOL** (listed 2019, with her ETF partner) holds roughly **80–85% in TIPS-type treasuries** and "fixes the problems with long options" — the first long interest-rate-volatility product in the US ETF market, packaging an OTC market that was previously inaccessible to ordinary investors into a single listed fund. Her related warning, vindicated by Silicon Valley Bank's failure: the aggregate bond index has no inflation protection, and **about a third of it is mortgages, which are short volatility** — homeowners hold the prepay option, so rising rates extend mortgage duration exactly when the bondholder is losing. The hardest part of entrepreneurship, she says, is distribution: "the ETF world is an **oligopoly**, really controlled by three large firms," and wealth-management platforms must approve issuers — some flatly saying "we only look at the largest issuers." Her answer is persistence: "If it's a listed security, why do you need approval?... The fund has paid out **30 basis points every single month since distributions began in July 2019**... I'm optimistic. I think we'll get there." She has now worked for herself since 2013 — longer than she spent at Goldman — and acts as an evangelist: "I have my peeps out there that I've convinced to leave their day jobs and start their own business... if you're a solution, you should do it."### Principles and perspective- **Buy optionality, sell nothing:** asymmetry is the edge; premium is a debit card with a known worst case and time to be right.- **Anti-consensus is the job description:** "when everybody's worried about one thing, the other side is usually cheaply priced in volatility."- **Contrarian vs. wrong = timing** — so engineer staying power into the structure, not the forecast.- **Conviction without conversion:** control your own book; don't measure yourself by who else listens.- **Perception is a choice:** the only woman in the vol-conference room can feel awkward — or "like a rock star... I deserve to be here." She chose the latter, and hired for diversity as she rose.- **Do what you love and it isn't work:** the Greenwich Library Bloomberg terminal is her proof; success follows the thing you'd do in your free two hours.- **Keep the swagger humble:** her family remains unimpressed — after listing the first-of-its-kind ETF, a parent suggested "maybe I should go back to Goldman Sachs and get a real job" — and her son's review of a Bloomberg TV appearance ("Mom, you look like an airline stewardess") earned only a cheerful "thank you. Keep me humble."

## Harris Kupperman — The Only Thing You Can Control is Profit
Harris Kupperman is the founder and CIO of Praetorian Capital, a long-time hedge fund manager and author of the *Adventures in Capitalism* blog, known for frontier-market adventures (Mongolia), activist firefighting (Aimia), and an aggressive inflection-and-trend style that nailed the March 2020 pivot. His episode with Maggie Lake is framed by his own philosophy: "the only thing you can control is profit" — and that you "have to fail epically a few times at this business."### Background: escaping Long Island, chess for dollars, and the 1997 crashKupperman grew up on Long Island — "probably the most boring place on earth" — and at 14, on his parents' offer to pay for any good boarding school that would take him, applied to every one and landed at **Phillips Andover**. He was a mediocre student ("I just didn't like school") but a fearsome chess player, paying his way through high school by taking the rich kids' lunch money — **$5 a game** — "until no one wanted to play me anymore." At **Tulane** in New Orleans, grades came easily. The career switch came in **1997**, watching the market crash on television: "I didn't know anything about stocks, but I felt it was very zero-sum, like chess. If all these guys in suits are losing a bunch of money, some other guy is taking it. Why am I wasting my time with chess? Let's go beat up these bigger guys — they have real money." His survey of the literature produced a lasting conviction: "The vast majority of books tell you the exact wrong thing to do... it's funny how many people who've been unsuccessful in finance write bestselling books." Twenty-five years on, he describes the market as "a piñata you wake up and whack — sometimes the piñata is empty, and sometimes the piñata swallows you." His stated apprenticeship requirement: **"You have to fail epically a few times at this business... really get hurt badly, question what your career looks like, and then show up the next morning and lose another 5 or 10%, over and over."** And his baseline: "Stocks are really easy — buy good companies at a fair price and go to the beach... what sinks most people is leverage."### Trade 1 (worst): Mongolia Growth Group — the liquidity that wasn'tIn the summer of 2010 a friend sent him to Mongolia, then the fastest-growing economy on earth: a $6 billion economy with some **$60 billion of megaproject capex** announced (the world's second-largest copper mine; a giant coal production-and-transshipment hub), and a stock exchange trading about **$10,000 a day**. He opened a personal account and issued the famous order: **"If it has a ticker symbol, buy it"** — waking to confirmations of seven shares here, three there ("what does this company do?" — "something with cashmere... something with sheep"). Recognizing that the other liquid frontier asset is real estate, he raised **$51 million**, listed **Mongolia Growth Group** in Canada (still publicly traded; he is chairman and CEO), bought the best commercial real estate in Ulaanbaatar, and built the country's only institutional property-management platform, plus the number-two sale-lease brokerage — "we did everything we told shareholders we would do. But unfortunately, it was pointless."A year in, the ruling party fractured — its prime minister and president ran against each other, letting **six minority parties form a freak ruling coalition** that squabbled, arrested each other, rotated ministers weekly ("I think the only thing they agreed on was stealing stuff"), banned foreign investment, and destroyed the economy: from **16% GDP growth to an IMF bailout**, a decade-long depression, and a currency down **70–80%**. The assets are "totally worthless right now," though he hasn't given up — "I just don't know if it's in my lifetime." The durable lesson is about **liquidity**: "I own 9.9% of a company and it's 100 trading days to get out — but call a broker and there's always a block available... When you own commercial real estate with a team of 20 on the ground, and you're chairman and CEO, there is no liquidity. When you get a trade wrong, all you can do is put your head down, cut costs, extend the runway, and hope to come out the other end." It also rewired his risk-reward calculus: "You can go far out on the risk curve for commensurate upside in frontier markets — or slightly out on the risk curve in the US. You don't need 20 or 50 times your money. Five times every couple of years with minimal downside is good enough. The equation is just better in developed markets." He closed his first hedge fund to try to salvage Mongolia — and the next trade happened with his own money.### Trade 2 (worst): Aimia — a one-man proxy warIn 2017 he bought a lot of **Aimia** (he jokes he owned it for years without knowing how to pronounce it), the Canadian loyalty company whose crown jewel, **Aeroplan**, was canceled by Air Canada — the stock collapsed from the high teens to **$2**. He saw value: the program was ending, but other divisions were worth far more than the price; what he missed was the people. Management "criminally insane" was his verdict after they **paid over $100 million for Sainsbury to take a profitable division** (~$40 million of EBITDA) off their hands: "Normally you either shut down a money-losing division, or you get paid. They paid someone to make a profitable one go away." He believed the stock was worth ~$20 before that transaction and ~$15 after — and that the rest would be parceled out to "drinking buddies." Without a fund (he had shut it down for Mongolia), he learned that **"when you don't have enough capital, you're going to be a victim sometimes."** A "coalition of the willing" of fellow shareholders collapsed into procedural bickering — "democracy doesn't work in real time" — so he found a quirk of Canadian law: **directors who fail to win 50% of votes at a shareholder meeting must resign.** He pulled the shareholder register off Bloomberg and, over two sleepless weeks and a couple hundred phone calls, ran a one-man no-confidence campaign — "not even vote for me, just vote against that guy" — and **fired two board members and the CEO**. Other hedge funds he barely knew then took the board, removed the corrupt actors, and salvaged value (a later recapitalization sold a division for hundreds of millions, then flipped a piece for roughly $2 billion). Kupperman paid $2 and sold at $5: "I made money on the trade, but I didn't deserve to. I relied on the kindness of strangers." The rule he extracted: either invest where you can sell by tomorrow, or own big positions only if you have **the strength to fight back** — and this trade was the direct motivation to relaunch Praetorian: "I don't ever want to be the victim again." His temperament note: "When a building's on fire, most people run away. I'm one of those guys that runs into the building — if everyone's running out, there's got to be some opportunity."### Trade 3 (best): the March 2020 pivot — "if you're going to panic, panic first"In Miami, in year two of the relaunched fund, he started tracking Wuhan in January 2020 — "this is going to be bad" — and sold aggressively in January and February, though "not enough" (he was busy stockpiling tuna and tomato sauce; 20% of his South Beach condo remains COVID food). By mid-March he'd concluded the virus was "a bad cold," but the shutdown and then the response were the signal: the Fed "printed a bajillion dollars," PPP money went out with no documentation — **"the economy was absolutely going to boom. When you throw that much money at the economy, where does it go? Into stocks and risk assets. It doesn't even matter if we sit home for two years."** He blogged it as **"Project Zimbabwe"**: "They will print enough money until everything goes straight up, and they won't stop until they've overshot."The portfolio surgery was brutal and fast. He owned **tankers** (winning pre-COVID, doomed once oil consumption collapsed) and his largest position, **Altisource** — the largest default mortgage servicer, a $20 stock already down to $6. The day the **foreclosure moratorium** was announced, "I sold every share I could in one day" — average exit around $11–12 (it still traded there two years later). His mantra: **"If you're going to panic, panic first."** Friends objected that the moratorium was unconstitutional and would lose in court — "Yeah, but are you going to sue them? That takes two years, and the company loses a fortune in the meantime." He rotated into **beneficiaries of money printing**: energy (when oil goes negative, production collapses — staying power gets paid), plus crypto, crypto brokers and crypto miners. The fund was down **half in Q1 2020**, flat by June, **up over 100% for the year**, and repeated the following year — while New York friends who "bought Peloton and shorted stocks" lost fortunes twice. The meta-lesson: "A younger version of me would have been frozen — it was the second year of my fund, we can't admit this loss. Instead, I pivoted... When something goes wrong, adjust as fast as possible."He rides trends with a cycle-tell discipline. In Bitcoin, bought at **$9,200** on a consolidation pattern with defined ~$500 risk, then watched it run — until the day **porn stars** (Mia Khalifa among them) were tweeting about crypto: "I sold a third that day, another third days later, the rest at about $58,000 average" — weeks before the top. "It's the 1920s showgirls thing... when porn stars start tweeting about energy, it's probably time to sell energy."### Trade 4 (best): building Praetorian into a real businessThe Aimia lesson demanded capital, so Kupperman relaunched as a friends-and-family fund — "me in my gym shorts with a CFO in the back office" — then, about a year before this conversation, converted it into an institution: capping the fund at **$250 million** (~$180 million at recording), hiring a client-facing partner, **three analysts**, and building **KEDM** (kedm.com), a data-analytics platform that systematizes his inflection hunting — "a cluster of insider buying means they think something's happening; go figure out what." The inflection thesis: **"When you show up in the first inning of an inflection, you're taking almost no risk — expectations are so low that if it doesn't inflect, you get your money back. By the third inning, the street has expectations and being wrong costs you money."** The team lets him carry a "shadow inventory" of themes (2023 ideas and 2024 ideas, holding four to eight at a time) instead of his old habit of committing capital first and learning the sector while it moved 5% a day — "pattern recognition is something I'm really good at, but that's not the right way to invest. You should have figured it out three months ago."### Principles and advice- **"If you get the trend right, it almost doesn't matter which stocks you buy. Get the trend wrong, and you're murdered."** His decade-trend call: an energy crisis — banning hydrocarbon exploration with no replacement for 20 years — "they'll succeed beyond their wildest dreams."- **Be super skeptical; assume everyone is lying to you; read a ton.** Three hundred years of financial history is "just the mistakes of others," mostly politicians optimizing election cycles: "I watch politicians make unforced errors and try to figure out who benefits and who loses."- **Fail epically, early, and repeatedly** — the tuition of the business — but "make new and more creative mistakes, not the same ones."- **Liquidity is situational:** listed shares always have a block at *some* price; operating businesses and control positions do not. Size accordingly.- **Never be the victim:** capital is what turns corporate-governance outrage into outcomes; without it, rely on the kindness of strangers and hope.- **Panic first, pivot hard:** sell impaired positions the day the fact changes, don't litigate, and redeploy into what the new regime rewards.- **Cycle tells work:** watch who just arrived at the party — "don't get off until the porn stars show up."- Motivation: "It's this big jigsaw puzzle and the pieces never fit... It's not really the money — I have nothing I want to buy. I just want the best numbers" — and bragging rights over beers with friends.

## Andrew Rosner — How To Recover from a Huge Loss
Andrew Rosner is the founder and CEO of MediaOptions, the world's largest domain-name brokerage, handling six- to eight-figure domain sales, and a decades-long digital-asset investor who was early into domains, Bitcoin, ICOs and NFTs. His April 2023 episode with Maggie Lake is built around the largest "money left on the table" figure in the series: $1.9 billion, on a single domain sale.### Background: from selling plants to seafood commoditiesRosner has been entrepreneurial "from day one" — selling plants (his mother was a florist), lemonade, lawn care and snow-shoveling from his corner lot, then a string of unglamorous jobs: meter reader, fishmonger, butcher. He graduated high school early, studied information systems in college (a business/computer-science hybrid), and started a software business in his senior year building inventory-management databases for blue-collar businesses — the largest landscaping company and largest pool company on Long Island. The venture's main yield was negative knowledge: "that was absolutely unequivocally what I didn't want to do with my life."The detour that taught him the most was **seafood** — not handling fish but **trading seafood commodities** by phone with Peru, China and Chile. He ran the company from roughly $8 million to $35 million in revenue, earning about $400,000 a year in his early twenties. "The seafood business taught me more than any other business I've ever been in" — a salt-of-the-earth trade where "anything that can be done is done in that business, from ways to get screwed to... very essential core business lessons." His mentor there supplied the mantra that anchors his whole investing philosophy: **"Nobody ever lost money taking a profit."**### Trade 1 (best): domains — the $5,000 ham that revealed a marketIn college, at the emergence of the consumer internet, a professor's aside — put up a website and anyone in the world can see it — landed as an aha moment, and Rosner began registering domain names as step one of any internet venture, the way a business needs a storefront ("all roads lead to domain names"). He insists he did **not** yet think of them as investments or internet real estate — only that a domain was the prerequisite.Around 2000–2001, just after the dot-com crash, he was in Mallorca with his now-wife, who introduced him to **jamón ibérico** (pata negra, the black-footed pig of northwest Spain and northern Portugal), then the most expensive meat in the world — and, he later learned, **illegal to import into the US** (the FDA classed it, like French raw cheese, unfit for import). He happened to own **jamoniberico.com**, registered with dreams of importing the ham. A few years later, driving across the Newport Bridge, he heard on NPR that the first legal pata negra shipment was arriving (for President George W. Bush). He tracked down the Virginia importer, called to congratulate him and buy a leg; the man laughed — sold out over a year ahead, ten containers, legs fetching **$7,000–$11,000 each**. Then the importer learned Rosner owned the domain, and his "proverbial jaw dropped." Rosner, knowing nothing of domain sales beyond business.com's reported $7 million, asked for one thing: **a ham from Bush's first container**. Deal: **$5,000 cash** — "done." Weeks later a coffin-shaped crate arrived with the pig leg, plus a case of Rioja, a carving knife and a ham holder.That sale was the tipping point: if a buyer in a tiny esoteric niche would pay the equivalent of ~$15,000, "everybody in every business in the world is going to want these domains." He went down the rabbit hole — "backing up the truck," though most great names were already gone — and discovered the **domain aftermarket**: no liquidity, broken auction systems, arbitrary reserves, nobody able to price anything, in a market still traumatized by the dot-com crash. He abandoned a $400,000-year seafood career, moved to Panama, and started trading domains full-time; his parents thought he was crazy, his cautious wife (his opposite, and co-founder of the business) eventually "took the brakes off."The lessons he carries from it: (1) **being early is extraordinarily lucrative** — he argues great domains bought in the 90s/early 2000s beat being early to Bitcoin, NFTs, Amazon or Apple as a directional bet on internet growth; (2) **the margin lives in illiquid markets that nobody can value** — real-estate comps sit within ±20% of each other, while even today only a handful of people can objectively value a domain; and (3) that obsession with valuing frontier assets led directly to his interest in Bitcoin and NFTs.### Trade 2 (best and worst in one): Bitcoin — thousands of coins for a backpack of goldIn **October 2011** a client — one of Bitcoin's early developers — bought a domain for about $250,000 and called weeks later to say he was putting everything into Bitcoin and mining equipment: "This is going to be the future of money... I really want you to get involved." Panama was then welcoming crypto exchanges, early Bitcoiners circulated through his neighborhood, and a friend who had worked for **Charlie Shrem** took him down the rabbit hole. In late 2011/early 2012 Rosner bought about **$5,000 worth — thousands of bitcoin — and essentially forgot about it**.Around late 2014/early 2015 the friend called: a client wanted to trade **gold bars for Bitcoin**. "Gold bars for magical internet money? That sounds great." The friend retrieved the coins (held in his wife's account, which Rosner no longer knew how to access) and Rosner swapped **all of the bitcoin for a backpack of gold bars** — with BTC around **$700**. "I stared at it for about an hour." Weeks later Bitcoin hit $1,000 and went mainstream; his friend's entire response was "buy more Bitcoin." Rosner re-entered at ~$1,200–1,300, then $1,500, $1,800, buying all the way up — **but never got back close to his original coin count**. The gold still sits in a safe; as Michael Saylor likes to say, Bitcoin was the better trade. His takeaway is not self-flagellation but epistemics: at the time it looked sage — the coins were, to him, "game tokens, totally experimental" — and "it's only hindsight that you know." (The sleepless decade over having his net worth in "an IP address" also taught him to get comfortable owning intangibles.)### Trade 3 (worst): Solana.com — "you should have taken the tokens"Through 2018 Rosner rode the full crypto arc: Bitcoin maximalist, then "I want to own everything" — roughly **200 ICOs** through the family investment vehicle, including Ethereum — then, after 99% went to zero and he lost millions, "don't ever talk to me about another altcoin ever again." Into that mood landed an email from **Raj Gokal**, co-founder of a startup called Solana, which had nothing to do with why Rosner owned **Solana.com** (a generic geographic name — Solana Beach, Florida; Solana Beach, California — held for years before the company existed). Gokal offered a **~50% premium if Rosner took the project's tokens**. The answer, at the bottom of the ICO wreckage: "I don't want your coin. Keep it. Just give me the cash. I want Uncle Sam bucks, not crypto bucks." Solana wired **$250,000**.In September 2021, at Solana's Breakpoint conference in Lisbon — the week after SOL's then all-time high near $280 — Rosner bought a VIP ticket and introduced himself at the founders' meet-and-greet. Gokal remembered him, gave him a bear hug, then pointed a finger: **"You should have taken the tokens."** They sat down on a sofa and ran the numbers: at Solana's token price, the cash deal had cost him **$1.9 billion**. Adjusting for the selling he would certainly have done on the way up, he puts the real figure at **$500 million to $1.5 billion** — "it will be on my tombstone as the worst decision I've ever made financially." He has made peace with it because his career is a museum of near-misses: he sold **NFT.com** for **$2 million** in January 2021, and a month later was offered **$20 million** — "six weeks" before the NFT market went parabolic. (He had been early into NFT *companies* from 2018 — his portfolio company Cryptograph did the first celebrity NFTs, introducing Paris Hilton, Ashton Kutcher, and the only NFT Vitalik Buterin ever did — but was too early.) The lesson he extracts: "this happens to me all the time, and I'm fine with it" — it is only hindsight that prices the road not taken.### Trade 4 (best): Bored Apes — buying IP, not jpegsDespite being burned by earliness, Rosner bought his **first Bored Ape in May 2021, a week or two after missing the mint** — first for ~$500, then a couple thousand each, ultimately accumulating **23**. The trigger was intellectual-property, not art: he read that Yuga Labs was giving buyers **100% of the commercial rights** to their individual apes. "That's what blew my mind... It was the equivalent of Stan Lee, before he launches the Marvel universe, coming to you and saying: I've designed all these characters... you can buy some of them." Owning characters inside a network where **every other owner holds the same rights** — so the whole community commercializes and brands in ways the founders couldn't imagine — read to him as "the making of Disney or Marvel at its earliest stages."His framework for NFTs generally: "the two most powerful forces on earth are **gravity and network effects**." A network with virality, utility and perceived value compounds exponentially — the telephone being the canonical case (one phone is useless; universal phones are a necessity), because each new member adds value to every existing member. An NFT, in his definition, is "a reference" to something **digitally scarce**, and permissionless community commercialization is the compounding engine: he points to the Bored Ape ecosystem's expansion (a Coinbase Hollywood-scale film project, restaurants, wine, beer, music) against a fixed supply and growing demand.### Principles and advice- **"Nobody ever lost money taking a profit"** — the seafood mentor's mantra, repeated before every buy/sell decision.- **Losing less beats winning more:** the fastest way to raise profits is cutting costs, not growing revenue; likewise, outperformance comes from losing less, then letting longevity and patience do the compounding.- **Schmuck insurance:** "never sell everything, keep a little bit" — 10%, in Jason Calacanis's phrase — because you never know.- **The smile indicator:** if your P&L makes you smile bigger than you ever expected, take some off — ideally pull your initial principal out and let winners ride.- **Take informed risk before certainty:** outperformance requires risk; "you cannot wait until you're certain about something to participate"; participate in what stokes your curiosity.- **Stay open-minded and revisable:** "if somebody can tell you something that you can't disprove, you need to be willing to say: maybe I was wrong."- **Failure discipline, via mantras:** "being early is the same as being wrong"; "the first wave usually takes the arrows in the back." "If you're not failing, you're not taking enough risk" — more failures, more lessons, more successes.

## Ben Harburg — Leaning Into Your Expertise
Ben Harburg is the founder and managing partner of **MSA Capital**, a firm that has operated for years at the intersection of the US, China and emerging markets, with local teams in China, India, the Middle East, North Africa and South America. Born in Colorado and genuinely nomadic — five US states and two other countries before college, an international school in Zurich, and no US residence since graduating — he originally trained for the Foreign Service (internships in diplomacy, a Fulbright) before taking the advice that "if you go to business first, you can always come back to government," but not vice versa. He first reached China in 2004 as a **Motorola intern**, returned on a near-monthly basis in 2009–2010 as a **commodities trader selling energy products and metal ores into China**, and in 2014–15 sold out of commodities entirely to bet on China technology. Hosted by Maggie Lake; published June 30, 2023.### Trade 1 (best) — Catching the China tech boom: the BOSS Zhipin investment**Context.** Harburg's macro conviction is that the **US–China relationship is the single bilateral relationship that will shape all of our futures** — business, military, economics, climate — and that fewer and fewer Americans actually understand China from the ground up, so being there made him both a better investor and "a steward of American interests down the road." As a commodity seller into China he watched "cute names like Tencent… and Alibaba with this funky logo" compound into a secular technology power, and saw the structural arbitrage: the US had a **40–50 year head start** in tech investing (the great US franchises date to the 1950s–80s) while **the first real Chinese VC funds only appeared around 2006–2010**. China was in its first or second generation of deals, full of US-precedent models not yet built locally. MSA's playbook: take the Silicon Valley playbook, identify the missing models, and **buy the sector winner in each vertical**. The 2015–2017 vintages were hugely successful, "pretty much right out of the gate."**The specific trade: BOSS Zhipin.** The online-recruitment incumbent, **Zhaopin**, was PC-oriented and never made the transition to mobile-first — his analog: Monster versus Indeed. MSA plucked out **Zhaopin's COO**, who knew exactly what was broken in the incumbent. The product became "**Tinder for hiring**": swipe-based, algorithmically matched, disintermediating recruiting agencies, letting high-growth companies' founders and recruiters engage candidates directly — with active users rather than dead links, high match velocity and very high success rates. (China doesn't track NPS-style engagement scores, he notes, but if it did, this would top them — every founder they talked to loved the product.)**The founder bet.** The founder was what Harburg calls a **"revenge founder"** — someone with everything to prove: no pedigree university, no classic VC-friendly profile, a heavy smoker ("he's got black teeth, almost — he turns off a lot of investors"). The entire VC community dismissed him: **passed over by 100+ VCs**. When the company **ran out of money**, MSA was effectively the only firm willing to step in at **Series B: $4 million for a 10% stake — a $40 million valuation**. Significantly, the founder himself was the **second-largest contributor to that round, all of his own money** — fully all-in.**Outcome.** The company IPO'd and has sat comfortably around a **$16 billion valuation** (off a bit with the broader Chinese market, which he expects to rebound). MSA invested in 2015 and, with a 10-year fund life plus a two-year extension, is still holding — "comfortably in the range where we don't need to exit today." One of the best trades the firm has ever made.**Lessons.**- **Believe in yourself and don't follow the crowd.** "Over and over and over the crowd has invested in the wrong models in China" — high-cash-burn consumer models, then whatever the fashion of the day is ("AI mirages"). MSA's job is to be **four or five years ahead of the trend** and invest with deep conviction in founders and verticals that will be big — accepting it may take four or five years for the market to appreciate it.- **China is a high-touch market.** What looks great on paper is often the wrong partner. His standing example: MSA were **Airbnb investors** and watched the founders repeatedly pick polished, English-speaking, US-educated, big-tech-pedigree leads for China over the "warrior in the trenches" type MSA recommended — and ultimately **essentially exit the market**, never solving regulatory localization. Western tech companies form "a graveyard" in China; if your value proposition isn't unique and moated enough, "you're going to get smoked immediately."- **Regulation is knowable if you listen.** The 2021 education-sector crackdown (companies forced to become nonprofits, "wiped off the map") surprised offshore investors but not MSA: they had heard for years from K-12 operators that they were about to be restricted, knew Xi Jinping had made education and curriculum politically sensitive, and judged on fundamentals that these were bad businesses anyway — "using investor money to buy users that weren't sticky." MSA **stayed entirely away** and was unaffected. The method: listen intently to regulators and decision-makers, and be a **beneficiary of regulatory shifts rather than a victim**.- On market access: certain sensitive sectors in China **cannot be invested in with US dollars** — you need local **RMB funds** — and the binding constraint today is geopolitics: Western restrictions on capital and core technology inputs (chips, equipment, software). MSA, mostly staffed by Chinese-national investment professionals, needs no local JV partner.- On the big picture, he pushes back on consensus: Western policymakers overstate China's territorial-displacement ambitions (Taiwan included); a historically non-expansionist China mainly wants **trade partners and food and economic security for 1.5 billion people**. And investors who try to "trade China from abroad… reading the tea leaves in New York" — "it's impossible. You have to be on the ground, deeply locally integrated."### Trade 2 (worst) — Greed: repeatedly not taking gains off the table**Context.** Harburg's worst "trades" are not single positions but **repeated behavioral themes** — this one is greed: not crystallizing gains at the top of the 2021 mania. At the literal **peak of the GameStop-era mania**, he spent the night working the phones nonstop with brokers, "selling everything I could get my hands on that was accessible" — but the firm's **biggest positions were still in lockup**, so the largest value creation couldn't be touched. A few months later, when prices had come off the highs and the lockups expired, **that was the moment to sell — and they didn't**, because of "this lingering thought that we might get back up to those highs," that the trough was just another step to a higher peak. What followed was a fairly **secular decline over the next 18 months**, especially in Chinese equities coming out of zero-COVID.**The lesson.** They knew what they were walking into — he calls it a **"slow-moving car crash"**: rising rates, the end of QE, financial levers all pointing to downward pressure — but acted as if the party might run a little longer. The mechanical failure: they kept **setting their bottom sale price slightly above the market** and so never crystallized. He is candid that this is largely a one-off in the firm's history — otherwise they've been disciplined profit-takers — which is precisely why the ones you miss sting enough to become a named "worst trade."### Trade 3 (best) — Backing Nio at a $1 billion valuation**Context.** **Nio** is one of China's largest EV makers; during the 2021 mania it touched roughly a **$98 billion market cap**. MSA put a significant ticket in at **a $1 billion valuation** — essentially the company's **first institutional round** (after a friends-and-family round). The founder is a **serial entrepreneur** with multiple multibillion-dollar builds, most notably **Mobike**, another MSA portfolio company, which MSA sold to **Meituan for almost $3 billion** — "definitively the best exit in the entire micro-mobility space" (contrast where names like Bird trade today).**Why the conviction.** The founder assembled the company "almost like he was constructing it with an instruction manual" — he knew who to hire for PR, design, manufacturing, and crucially **how to raise capital and extract funding, concessions and support from regional governments** to offset infrastructure costs, which gave Nio a calmer rise than most touch-and-go EV startups. The consensus bet against was deep: even after BYD, few believed the Chinese could build a **globally leading** EV — good manufacturing, the skeptics said, but not design. US PE funds and global growth funds passed because they simply **could not underwrite that China could produce a global EV leader**. MSA's case: China would be the majority of global EV demand; Tesla's China reception proved the appetite; and Nio's product was **on par with Tesla in design and specification at roughly a third of the price**. As an owner of both a Tesla and a Nio, Harburg testifies the Nio is "a better driver, far more comfortable," much cheaper.**Outcome.** At the valuation peak Nio became the **second- or third-most valuable car company in the world**, and it now sells not just in China but from showrooms in **Norway, Germany and Northern Europe**, with global demand — "the makings of a global electric vehicle company for China."**The broader thesis — China going global.** MSA was among the first firms to underwrite it systematically. For **hardware**, China offers the highest quality production at the lowest cost — smartphones, EVs, batteries, TVs, home electronics. For **consumer-facing software**, China is already building better applications for mobile-native users than the West, because technology adoption hit China at the exact moment people were leaping straight to mobile payments and e-commerce, skipping the intermediate rungs the West passed through.**Geopolitical risk management.** They discount geopolitical risk in valuation and actively manage it: steering portfolio companies toward **less sensitive sectors** (cross-border e-commerce, communications, entertainment — versus anything Huawei-like in national-security tech) and toward **receptive markets — Southeast Asia and the Middle East**. The Middle East corridor has opened materially: high-level delegations both ways since **Xi Jinping visited Saudi Arabia in December** (2022), with Gulf capital partially displacing departing Western capital — and countries like Saudi Arabia now able to weigh Chinese clouds (Alibaba Cloud, Baidu Cloud, Tencent Cloud) against Microsoft and AWS, a competitive dynamic MSA positions to benefit from. Bifurcation even creates deal flow the other way: as the US pushed out leading scientists of Chinese origin, many returned to China to build businesses "that otherwise wouldn't have been built in China" — and MSA invests in those founders coming home.### Trade 4 (worst) — Straying outside your zone of expertise**Context.** The second of Harburg's worst "trades" is a theme: **going outside the firm's zone of excellence — which is sectoral and geographic.** Investments in the US, or in any geography where MSA lacks a deeply embedded base of talent and insight, "usually don't end well — even if we're following what we think are the smart names," i.e., co-investing behind blue-chip Silicon Valley VCs.**The natural experiment.** When MSA entered the **Middle East about four years ago** (from mid-2023, so around 2019), they first **followed the blue-chip names — and those were some of their worst investments in the region**. As soon as they built their own local team, understood the market deeply, and made **uncorrelated** investments, they started **outperforming the market** — finding founders the regional market itself had dismissed who built the region's most valuable companies, and avoiding models that a myopic regional investor loves but that MSA's Asia experience says will never scale or turn profitable.**The cautionary example: cloud kitchens.** MSA invested in a cloud-kitchen business that has done well — up **~15x to unicorn valuation** since entry — yet Harburg lists it as a mistake: their China knowledge says the model doesn't survive (there are no standalone cloud-kitchen companies left in China; the category gets absorbed into the food aggregators' value chain), so they believe it will struggle globally. Good return, wrong process.**The anti-Tiger model.** Instead of the Tiger Global approach — pick a model you like and index it across every market by buying "the local one" everywhere — MSA evaluates **one business model across five or six markets** (Southeast Asia, Middle East, Africa, North America, etc.), compares **key KPIs country by country**, and funds only the **best value-for-dollar** implementation; if nothing clears the bar, they drop the whole opportunity.**People and process.** Local offices must have "the best minds in each geography," combining top local investors with full fluency in the **China playbook** — what he calls **"China IQ"** — so they can reject doomed imported models early. Hiring litmus test: **younger, hungry people with something to prove** (older, set-in-their-ways talent rarely works), locals who have **spent time inside a Chinese fund or corporation**, and people **unafraid of bold, contrarian positions**. Asked whether he's a contrarian, his answer: not exactly — "it's just being early," five or six years ahead geographically and four or five years ahead on regulation, with foresight and the patience to hold until the market catches up.### The Cadiz side-bet — the philosophy applied to footballHarburg's personal (not fund) investment in **Cadiz**, a small Spanish top-flight club, is the same framework transplanted: take a small, unknown team and outperform richer rivals through **systematic investment in scouting, data analysis and player evaluation** — evaluating footballers the way MSA evaluates companies, on spreadsheets across operational metrics, "a little bit of a Moneyball approach." The budget is about **€45 million against Real Madrid and Barcelona payrolls around €680 million each**, and over the last couple of years Cadiz has **beaten Real Madrid, Barcelona, Atletico Madrid, Betis, Roma and Manchester United**. He then globalized the asset: social-media content teams in the Middle East, Asia, Southeast Asia and China building localized content and new fan revenue from underserved audiences. (His jab at Ryan Reynolds, who "copied me at a much lower scale" with a fifth-tier English club: he plays "against teams you've never heard of… we're playing against Real Madrid.") Given the inbound since, he may expand sports as a strategy — other teams, other sports, and importing new US-pioneered sports into markets where they don't yet exist.

## Sa'ad Shah — Finding Joy and Success on Life's Serpentine Road
Sa'ad Shah is the managing partner and co-founder of the **Noetic Fund**, a venture fund focused on central nervous system and mental-health science. Interviewed by Maggie Lake (published August 4, 2023). His four "trades" are as much career-and-life decisions as market positions, running from FX trading desks to esoteric hedge fund strategies to psychedelic-medicine venture capital.### Background: a diplomat's son behind the Berlin WallShah is the son of a career Pakistani diplomat (his father served the government for close to 35 years) and grew up nomadically—Abu Dhabi, Ottawa, Yangon—with his formative years in **East Berlin in the mid-1980s**, just before Perestroika. He and his siblings studied in West Berlin, crossing the border daily on streetcars "that hadn't changed since World War II." Once, running to catch a bus, he crossed where you must never run: guards drew weapons and he was sent home. "It was a catalyst for me because it got me to really pay attention to my surroundings." The household was bugged; through his father's Afghan information exchanges, Shah says he met a KGB figure he later learned was Vladimir Putin, then based in Dresden. At 16–17 he was reading Nietzsche and Schopenhauer—an early pull toward the esoteric that recurs through his career.Money was visible early: the East–West mark rate reached double digits to one (garbled in the transcript, "12 or 15 to one") while buying power stayed similar, so cross-border arbitrage was everywhere. "I took my career initially into foreign exchange and commodity trading, which makes sense." He had wanted to be a diplomat or join the family steel-manufacturing business in Pakistan; instead he describes his youth as realizing he was "born wearing many different masks" and spending the years since "taking off the many masks." His credo: the greatest sin is not living up to your own potential—"the other close to 8 billion people are already taken" (a line he anchors in Kierkegaard).### Trade 1 (best): Walking away from Lehman Brothers for Toronto (mid-1990s)Shah graduated from **Columbia University** in economics and political science, still aiming at the diplomatic service. A family friend at the UN delivered the blocking fact: every country has a quota, Pakistan's was full for two years—"maybe banking." He talked his way into a **Columbia Business School** finance class in his final two semesters and discovered he loved the field.The job offer that followed was from **Lehman Brothers**, in the **World Trade Center North Tower**. His girlfriend—a Canadian MIT graduate he'd met in Boston at 18, now his wife of 27 years—was moving to Toronto (3M's Canadian headquarters, in London, Ontario). He gave up Lehman and followed.The sliding-door weight came on **9/11**: "There's not a week that goes by where I don't think about what could have potentially happened." A Columbia classmate—at the towers that day for a breakfast conference—perished. The lessons are humility and gratitude ("gratitude is such an important part of the equation—don't lose sight of it"), and it began the reckoning with death and meaning that resurfaces in trade 4.Toronto gave him nothing at first: no work permit for ~18 months (and his student visa barred him from returning to the US), so he tended bar and, for about nine months, worked as a **door-to-door salesman** in remote Ontario, selling $20 items to golf courses and restaurants. He treated it as paid training: building a relationship with a stranger in two or three minutes, conveying a narrative, qualifying the prospect, reading an economy's grievances first-hand. "There's absolutely no MBA course that can ever teach you that." It shattered his comfort zone and became a cornerstone of his career.### Interlude: the RBC yearsHe got into finance via real estate banking at the **Royal Bank of Canada**, then forced his way onto the trading floor: **debt syndication**, then the **FX and commodity desk**, ultimately **head of financial engineering**. RBC was the biggest player in everything CAD but myopic—a narrow set of Canada-relevant commodities and currencies. The core skill was **explaining complexity**: narratives showing institutions how complex M&A exposures would be hedged, plus work with sophisticated desks putting on risky **digital options** and **one-touch structures**. He did well young—"don't mess things up, you're on a fast track"—and got bored.### Trade 2 (best): Leaving RBC for DGAM (2006)—complexity as a productIn 2006 he left for a team building a diversified global asset manager, **DGAM**—again the Kierkegaard logic: "If I don't explore and push the limits in terms of who I am, I'll be doing myself a disservice." DGAM's pitch to institutions: *"You manage the traditional hedge fund strategies—long/short, macro, trend following. Let us manage complexity for you."* It seeded **strategies that didn't yet exist**, not just managers: **reinsurance**; **trading precipitation and temperature worldwide**; **commercial litigation finance**; **music royalties**; **pharmaceutical royalties**; **film finance**—among the biggest backers of Steven Mnuchin's operation across 77 films with 20th Century Fox.The payoff came in stress. Across **2007, 2008, 2009 and 2011** the complex strategies outperformed: "We were the first ones to do tail hedging—a very complex version: finding the cheapest form of volatility that would hedge our left tails." When everyone else was down 25–40% in 2008, DGAM was **flat to slightly up with capital available to deploy**—the trajectory that caught Carlyle's attention.By the sale DGAM was near **$7 billion** after almost ten years and needed permanent capital to reach $20–30 billion. **The Carlyle Group**—David Rubenstein, whom Shah respects deeply—already owned hedge fund platforms (Claren Road, Vermilion, Trafalgar, assets taken over from Goldman's Petershill fund), so "they understood hedge funds." In hindsight: "It wasn't the best trade for either of us." The acquirer spoke a different nomenclature—"no longer about multiple stores… all about alpha, beta, Sortino ratio, Sharpe ratios"—and Carlyle's entrenched sales team had to be brought up that curve from scratch. When Carlyle later shut its hedge fund operations, DGAM was a casualty; Shah had left about six months earlier, having "seen the writing on the wall." Lesson: **cultural and linguistic mismatch between buyer and acquired kills deals that look logical on paper.**### Trade 3 (worst): The AI quant equity fund, 2016–17Leaving Carlyle (Rubenstein: "I'm not going to stand in your way. But if it doesn't work out, come back"), he launched a quant equity fund (he calls it "Cube"; closed 2017) built on an **AI model using natural language processing** to decipher thousands of texts and infer market impact. Three legs: (1) **long-dated call options** with a lot of convexity when triggers fired; (2) a **ladder of long puts**; (3) **writing puts**. Launched early 2016 with the team's own capital—seven people, plan: trade own money for a year, then raise $20–50 million. **By February they were up 38%.**Then stress—the environment the fund was designed for—killed it. "Brexit happens, the AI could not compute Brexit… then Trump happens. And the AI says, I could not compute that." The fund ended the year **down 1%**—capital gone and a track record impossible to raise on. Lessons: **institutions are a box-checking exercise**—the fund was too small for them; family offices and ultra-high-net-worth investors are the natural market, and you must understand each party's utility function. **"Going into business with closest friends—friends first, business colleagues later—bad idea. I would not recommend it."** And business hygiene matters: what to bring in first, where to spend, how to hire.He describes himself as a **"manifesting generator"** in human-design terms: he only learns whether something is for him by being inside it. Risk credo: "If you don't take risks and break your comfort zone, you're never going to really progress… I've taken a lot of risks, and a lot of them have not worked out at all." Anti-paralysis tool: the **5-4-3-2-1 rule**—count down and just do it.### Trade 4 (best and worst): Brazil 2009 → the Noetic Fund (2020)The thread from Berlin: the near-death at the border, rekindled by 9/11, sent him after the perennial questions. He read philosophy, religion, quantum physics, and kept finding **plant medicines at the root of cultures**—the **blue lotus** in Egypt, the immortality plant in the **Epic of Gilgamesh**, **Soma** in the Rig Vedas, **Amanita muscaria** in Siberia, **ayahuasca** in the Amazon, **ibogaine** among West Africa's Dogon—and cave art 25,000–50,000 years old from three continents showing strikingly similar motifs. **Graham Hancock's** book on visionary experience "was a real turning point," and Shah befriended him.The trade itself: in **2009, aged 38–39**, he took two weeks off and went to Brazil with Hancock and professors **Michael Winkelman** and **Luiz Eduardo Luna** to study the **Shipibo tribe**, sitting **five ayahuasca ceremonies**, never having taken a psychedelic before. "A profound experience would be a hell of an understatement." His image: "like you've been looking at the world from a dirty window, and somebody takes a squeegee and cleans it." He came back **without his fear of death**: "This cycle never ends. Energy transfers, it doesn't die." He is explicit about the danger: "These are incredibly powerful molecules… they need to be approached with reverence"—a month of preparation, a taxing purge, and nothing to rush back into.The first person he told was **Warren Wright**, CIO at DGAM and then the Carlyle Liquid Markets Group—now his co-founder at Noetic. For the next five to eight years they researched mechanisms of action with academics at **Johns Hopkins, Harvard, Imperial College London, UCSD, UCLA and Basel University**. In **2018** a market appeared: private capital going into research and commercialization of plant-based entheogens—**Sage Therapeutics, Atai Life Sciences (Christian Angermayer), Compass Pathways**; friends running $60-billion credit shops were already investing. They put in their own money; friends and family asked in; a fund became necessary. Named **Noetic** (for the noetic qualities of intellect and lived experience), it opened in **February 2020**.Thesis and positioning: **"We are not a psychedelic fund. We're a fund focused on the central nervous system / mental health"**—science-backed opportunities with **strong IP** (which makes them scalable and accessible): psychedelic drug development plus **magnetic resonance technology, medical devices, digital therapeutics, vagus nerve stimulation**. "It's a renaissance period for neuroscience." **Fund I: $35 million raised, 24 investments from about 1,000 companies screened, six exits so far**—"done phenomenally well"—and Fund II is underway, though he credits "luck and timing."The "worst" half is the hype cycle. Cannabis investors arrived with FOMO—"apples and oranges"—and roughly **80 psychedelics stocks** listed across Canada, the US and Europe, with no revenue, a long regulatory path (the DEA still on the sidelines) and billion-dollar valuations. The crash came. Noetic, **private-only**, had exited the positions that went public on its own schedule and was spared the worst, but the last 18 months (2022–23) were the hardest fundraising stretch of his career: "Unless you're specifically doing credit, or you've got an AI bent, it's not that easy"—and good portfolio companies still need follow-on capital.Why he stays: the **zeitgeist is agency**—crypto, decentralization, wellness and psychedelics all express the same demand for taking control back, and medicine is heading to **personalization** ("Give 50,000 people the exact same dose and they will have 50,000 different experiences"). Oncology's R&D playbook is transferring; **AI** is collapsing molecule discovery; and "we've learned more about the human brain in the last five years than at any other point in history." The demand driver is grim: the **US Surgeon General** found **loneliness** is the biggest epidemic in America, and his daughters, 23 and 18, have known **five people** who died by suicide (he, at 51, has known one). "SSRIs have been a huge disappointment—all the big pharma companies know that," and FDA breakthrough designations keep accumulating. Maggie Lake's summary stands as the investment case: **"Where there's an epidemic, there's an opportunity."**### Overarching principles (Shah)- **Complicated vs complex**: "Complicated is getting an astronaut to the moon—follow the recipe. Complexity is raising a teenager." He builds careers and funds around complexity and the esoteric: "left of centre, on the fringe—before it becomes mainstream."- Alpha in complex strategies "is fleeting—it's all about **sizing, timing and tilting**."- Take risks and break the comfort zone; don't let analysis paralysis win.- Institutions buy checkboxes; family offices buy narratives and utility fit—know which you are selling to.- Sell (or be acquired) only into **cultural and linguistic compatibility**; the Carlyle mismatch destroyed a good business.- Gratitude and mortality awareness as the operating system: the Berlin border and 9/11 "all comes full circle."

## Anthony Scaramucci — Anthony Scaramucci on Failure, Authenticity, and the Future of Crypto
Anthony Scaramucci is the founder of SkyBridge Capital and of the SALT conference, a former Goldman Sachs private banker (joined 1989), and briefly White House Communications Director in 2017 (fired after 11 days). Speaking with Maggie Lake in November 2022 — with his Bitcoin position underwater and FTX having just taken a stake in SkyBridge — he frames his whole career around one early blow-up and the entrepreneurial philosophy it installed.### Background: blue-collar Long Island to Goldman SachsScaramucci grew up in Port Washington, Long Island, in a blue-collar Italian family; he now lives two miles from his parents, still in the house he grew up in. His grandfather emigrated from Italy to northeastern Pennsylvania, worked as a coal miner and later a butcher/storekeeper, and died young of black lung disease. His father answered a classified ad to leave coal country, moved to Long Island to mine sand instead, and spent 42 years at the same company — laborer, then crane operator, then truck dispatcher — in the union. His mother was a housewife who later worked a nail salon and became a bridal makeup artist. The family's income rose and fell with his father's overtime: "From ages 11 to 18 there was great financial uncertainty in my family." If his father only worked 40 hours, the family was squeezed; weekend work meant time-and-a-half. Young Scaramucci did every job imaginable — a Newsday paper route, stocking shelves at Key Food, selling mopeds at his uncle's motorcycle shop (which the uncle ran successfully for 50 years).His parents had no college education but demanded education for their kids. He went to Tufts (recruited for football, despite being short; a knee injury forced him to study), and chose law school with what he calls "superficial" reasoning: as a Tufts junior he saw in Time magazine that New York law firms paid starting salaries of $65,000 — double his father's roughly $33,000. He went to Harvard Law, hated it, and wanted to drop out over Thanksgiving 1986 (his mother's veto was operatic). The family's cluelessness about his world became family legend: his mother thought it was "Hartford Law School," and when he joined Goldman Sachs she cried on learning it was not a law firm — then spent about five years telling the neighbors it was one ("Mrs. Panetti: so, that law firm Goldman Sachs, how is it working out for you?").At his first Goldman interview he wore his best suit — 100% polyester, with a shiny polyester shirt ("fully flammable"). A partner took him outside and told him he was very smart but the worst-dressed person they had ever met. He bought two suits, shirts and rep ties at Brooks Brothers on a 20%-interest student credit card and got the job. He spent seven years at Goldman, mostly in private banking, and says he is "still acclimating" to corporate culture — the polymath's refusal to be a "corporate apparatchik" became a career theme.### Trade 1 (worst): Centocor biotech calls at 27 — "a great $50,000 tuition"On Goldman's desk, swinging, in his words, "from massive insecurity to overconfidence, borderline arrogance," Scaramucci followed the firm's biotech analyst into **Centocor**, a biotech developing a sepsis treatment expected to be a blockbuster. Despite zero science background, he decided he was "an all-knowing, omniscient genius" and bought **call options leading into the FDA approval**. The FDA approved the drug — the worst possible thing for his discipline — and the calls went from roughly **$6,000 to about $80,000**. Instead of paying down his ~$120,000 of student debt, he **rolled everything into the forward calls for the phase-two trials**, even flying down to the FDA hearing with the analyst. The agency rejected the phase-two filing, the calls imploded, and leverage/margin took his account from **plus $80,000 to minus $50,000**. Goldman's clearing group demanded a **$50,000 margin call by Friday**. He called his boss, who said, "You are an imbecile." ("Yes, I am an imbecile.") The firm fronted the money and docked his pay until it was repaid; his school debt grew to ~$170,000.He calls it his worst trade and, "in a weird way," his best: it was "a great $50,000 tuition" for what no textbook teaches — circle of competence, risk, overconfidence. It installed the question he says he has asked before **every** trade since (crypto, stocks, real estate): **"I'm 100% wrong. Now what?"** — as opposed to assuming the trade "will work out masterfully."### Trade 2 (best): Travelers stock and warrants, ~1995–1997After the savings-and-loan crisis, the **Resolution Trust Corporation** took over the failed banks' bad real-estate assets and unwound them (Blackstone and other private-equity buyers took portfolios), while the economy healed. Scaramucci's partner in Goldman private wealth management and he judged the financial-services sector to be structurally improving — and this time he was **inside his circle of competence**: he worked in financial services and understood the balance sheets, income statements and leverage of these companies. From about **1995 to 1997 he owned Travelers stock and, importantly, the warrants** — "a little bit of a levered kick to the stock" — while Sandy Weill ran the company toward its famous merger with Citicorp (bringing Jamie Dimon with him). It "made me a lot of money."What made it one of the best trades of his career was not just being right but the discipline around it: it was **sized** at a much smaller share of his net worth, with explicit recognition he could be wrong; and when it won, he used the proceeds well — paid off his school debt, bought a modest house in Manhasset, and banked a nest egg that funded his first business. He rejects hindsight regret ("I should have put more in") — "you don't know what's actually going to happen."### Leaving Goldman: the Gabelli verdict and firm #1Around age 30 he went to see **Mario Gabelli**, who told him: "You're not right for Goldman. Look at your personality. You tell people what you think. The only way you're going to be super successful at Goldman is to shave all the sharp points off your personality… or you could do what I did — leave and build your own business. And you're going to be 50 in 10 minutes… you'll be out to pasture at Goldman." In 1996, at 32, married with two kids, he left a job paying over $1 million a year to start a firm with one partner in a room smaller than his conference room — and made himself physically sick doing it (he believes he ran a 100-degree-plus fever his first day). His motivation traces to childhood: his father had a boss and no control, so "I am going to do everything in my power not to have a boss" — invoking Jack Welch's "control your own destiny or somebody else will." Clients and regulators are still bosses, he concedes; at SkyBridge "performance is a close second to regulatory compliance," because a small firm cannot pay its way past a mistake the way JPMorgan absorbed ~$4 billion of crisis-era fines.The first firm grew to **$1 billion under management** and was sold to **Neuberger Berman about six weeks after 9/11** in a nine-figure sale, split among four or five partners. He paid his taxes and parked the proceeds in municipal bonds. Neuberger was then bought by Lehman Brothers (a bump on his stock), and when his contract ran out he went to **Dick Fuld**, who gave him **$10 million of balance-sheet money to start SkyBridge**.### Trade 3 (his best): buying Citigroup's alternatives business, 2009The global financial crisis hit SkyBridge hard ("we were becoming Lowbridge, the bridge to nowhere"). He made two contrarian moves. First, SkyBridge took a room block at the Encore (Wynn) in Las Vegas and launched what became the **SALT conference** — precisely when every major bank was abandoning Las Vegas and the conference business — to "create something out of the ashes." Second, at lunch with **Jane Fraser** (today Citigroup's CEO, then running the wind-down of non-core assets in what he calls Citi Private Holdings with Mike Corbat), he learned that after Citi took **$25 billion** from the government, the government wanted it to be a commercial bank and to shed non-core assets. He wanted to buy her hedge-fund seeding business; she countered: take the **whole fund-of-funds complex**. "I gulped" — and bought it, **liquidating his entire municipal-bond portfolio, his life savings, and borrowing money on top**.His reasoning was cyclical, not heroic: markets may drop 40–50% in a bear (Jamie Dimon was out calling for another 20%), "but I don't think it's going down 100%. I don't think JPMorgan or Disney is going to trade to zero. They have assets and cash flow." If the crisis ended, the Citi assets plus SkyBridge would be a great story. He names it **the best trade of his career** — risky to an outsider, but inside his competence, and necessary for the firm's survival. On how such decisions get made: mentors and a "personal board of advisors" help, but ultimately "you're making the decision in a vacuum of your own loneliness… you either believe you're a Swiss Army knife" who can "jump off a cliff and build the airplane on the way down, or not." His model of the fall-and-rebuild entrepreneur is **Elon Musk**, who was "virtually bankrupt" in 2008, levered in both the rocket and the car company, needed several things to break his way — and planned to rebuild either way. Hence his mantra: entrepreneurs are not capitalists, they are "**capital artists**" painting on the canvas of a business; if the canvas is smudged, "they'll start on another canvas." He keeps his own fallback explicit: "If I've got to go back to a white T-shirt and a six-pack of Schlitz and a rabbit-ear TV to watch my beloved Mets, I'm doing that."### The politics detour (a cautionary tale of ego)He built his network through political giving because, as a crane operator's son, he had no other route to successful people: a **$250 check at 25** to Young Republicans for Rudolph Giuliani; Giuliani's 1989 loss to Dinkins made him a friend of Rudy, and the 1993 win put him inside the network. He worked for Giuliani, Pataki and D'Amato; in July 2007 he fundraised for a law-school contemporary he barely remembered — **Barack Obama** — with the famous exchange in which Obama offered, for a doubled check, to pretend they'd been close in law school ("take it all the way back to Hawaii"). Scaramucci ripped up the first check and handed over the doubled one.He calls accepting the White House communications director job "a mistake of pride and ego" — his wife begged him not to do it. After 11 days, a flippant on-the-record remark about Steve Bannon to a journalist whose family had known his for 50 years ran on CNN, and General John Kelly fired him. His read: a well-known figure phoned him in the White House basement to warn "this town is allergic to the truth," and the firing was deserved. His rule from it: **be accountable — don't blame anybody else** ("He had the right to fire me. I deserve to have been fired"). He later split with Trump when, in his view, the president began attacking the system's checks and balances: "he's the wrong messenger for your movement."### Trade 4: structured credit — worked twice, broke oncePost-crisis, in 2009–10, SkyBridge bought **structured credit** — packaged housing, credit-card and auto loans that had been "blown to pieces," down 40–60% — at roughly **30 cents on the dollar, expecting 70, never 100**. It worked; the firm sold ("shedded") the assets and rotated into activism. Then, returning to SkyBridge in 2017–18 after the political adventure, the firm **went back into structured credit** as a defensive position: volatility was low and the beta was **0.3** — "70% less risk than the overall market" — in a market they thought top-heavy. In **March 2020** COVID annihilated the position. The beta numbers, he says, were "garbage in, garbage out"; nobody models the economy going from ~2% growth to a standstill. His distilled lesson: **"Anything that you think cannot happen, will happen."** And the sizing sin: "we had too much on… we were picking up pennies in front of a steamroller" — the yield was the penny; the principal got run over. They recovered, rotated out, and moved toward crypto and "growthier" assets. Humility anchors the point: even Buffett's Berkshire has bad years — "you get things wrong… stay humble and stay open-minded."### Crypto and the futureAt recording (November 2022) he was visibly underwater on Bitcoin — bought in the **$16,000–17,000s**, then trading near $19,000, down from $60,000-plus — immortalized by the New York Post front-page composite of him "sinking in the SS Mooch." He sized it as a **five-year** bet and refuses to call it: "this could either be a great trade or a horrific one." FTX had taken a stake in SkyBridge weeks earlier. His conviction is technological: after three decades in financial services he sees blockchain as "a wonderful **de-layering mechanism**" — peer-to-peer transfers via a phone smart-wallet and stablecoins that cut the ~3.5% payments toll out of the economy, which spread economy-wide is "very disinflationary." Every claim, he repeats, is one he "could be 100% wrong on."### Principles and advice- **Pre-trade checklist:** "I'm 100% wrong. Now what?" — run the failure case before entry; size so the failure case is survivable.- **Circle of competence:** the Centocor loss was the tuition; the Travelers and Citi wins were the same industry knowledge applied with discipline.- **Cyclicality:** assets blown up in a crisis recover if the assets have cash flow; buy when others are forced sellers (RTC 1990s, structured credit 2009, SALT 2009).- **"Anything you think cannot happen will happen"** — respect the tails, especially when earning yield.- **Accountability:** own failures ("I deserve to have been fired"); never dishonor his father's work ethic — "you can call me an imbecile, but you can't call me dishonest."- **Regret management:** "Leave yesterday behind… take the millstone of regret off your neck." With too many decisions to ever be right always — "most of the time you're guessing" — self-torture over mistakes only destroys today.- **Ignore the noise:** Kellyanne Conway deleted his Twitter notifications on day one at the White House; his grandmother's rule — "what other people think of you is none of your business."- **Authenticity over fit:** he never made a TV appearance from 1989 to 2009; media only became part of the job when SALT needed promoting. He'll take the "Mooch" caricature over a sanded-down personality.

## David Rubenstein — David Rubenstein on Luck, Leadership, and Investing
David Rubenstein is the co-founder and co-chairman of the Carlyle Group, one of the world's largest private equity firms (roughly $400 billion under management at the time of this January 2023 conversation), and a well-known interviewer of business and political leaders. His episode is less about trading positions than about two of the greatest "trades not taken" in modern investing history — Amazon and Facebook — bracketed by the founding and globalization of Carlyle.### Background: Baltimore, the White House, and a mediocre law careerRubenstein grew up in a small Jewish neighborhood in Baltimore. His father dropped out of high school to serve in World War II, came back, never finished high school or college, and worked for the post office his entire career; his mother was also blue-collar, and the family lived paycheck to paycheck. He is insistently self-deprecating about his early years: "I wasn't very good at anything. I wasn't brilliant. I wasn't a great athlete. I wasn't particularly handsome. Nobody thought I was charming." He went to Baltimore public schools, then Duke University and the University of Chicago Law School.His leadership framework (from his book on the subject): divide life into thirds, and note that the first-third stars — Rhodes Scholars, Supreme Court clerks, valedictorians, Heisman winners — rarely become presidents or Chief Justices. "I was very much the tortoise. And I caught up to some of the hares and bypassed them. But I got lucky in life... it's better to be lucky in the second and third third than in the first third." Money was never the motivator: he had none growing up and "didn't aspire to have any." In that era a Jewish kid from Baltimore went into the family business or a profession — his mother wanted him to be a dentist ("you could be called a doctor and you don't have weekend hours"). His real love was government and politics; law was the vehicle.He practiced law in New York for two years, choosing the firm because he could work with **Ted Sorensen**, John F. Kennedy's top adviser ("I thought maybe some of his glitter would rub off on me. That really didn't happen"). He moved to Washington as chief counsel to Senator **Birch Bayh's** presidential campaign; Bayh dropped out a month later. Someone then asked if he wanted to work for another candidate — "Jimmy Carter." His response: "Well, that's the peanut farmer. He's never going to be elected president." He joined anyway, working the general election in Georgia; when he arrived Carter led Ford by 34 points, and by Election Day the margin was one — Carter liked to joke about "your contribution." Because campaign staff become White House staff, Rubenstein became **deputy domestic policy adviser to the president at 27**: "I wasn't qualified. He wasn't qualified. None of us were qualified. But that's how the system works." The White House deflated the mystique — "the people working there were not superstars. They were average humans who happened to get lucky. We made a lot of mistakes... We were criticized almost every day." His advice to the young: do government service when you're young, before family and financial obligations.### Trade 1 (best): founding Carlyle in 1987 — with $5 million and no experienceAfter Carter lost in 1980, the "call me anytime" promises of the White House years evaporated — "nobody wants a Carter White House aide when Reagan's going to be president." It took months to get a law-firm job, and once back in practice he understood why: "I wasn't that good at it. I didn't have any clients. I didn't have any specialty." With time to think, he read that entrepreneurs typically start businesses **between ages 28 and 37** — "I read that when I was 37." And **Bill Simon**, the former Treasury Secretary, had just done the famous **Gibson Greeting Cards** buyout, turning roughly **$1 million into $80 million in about 18 months**. "Whatever that is, it's better than what I'm doing now."So he decided to build **the first buyout firm in Washington, D.C.** "Everybody laughed because I had no experience." He recruited people who had investing experience (if not buyout experience), and in **1987 cobbled together $5 million** from four institutional investors and a friend he "probably strong-armed." The plan was a boutique that would never exceed ten people; instead it became one of the largest private equity firms in the world. His reflections on entrepreneurship come from that leap: founders are young, "don't know what they don't know," are self-confident, and want to do something not done before — "had I been 10 years older... had I realized how challenging it was... how little I knew about the business, I wouldn't have done it." In his high school or college class, "most people would not know I was even in the class," and a most-likely-to-succeed vote would have put him "near the bottom." Hence his signature theme: **"Luck is something you make for yourself"** — sit in your office, meet nobody, take no risks, and you'll have none.The name: after rejecting a consultant's Greek and Roman options and their own "Washington International Finance and Investment Company" ("doesn't exactly roll off the tongue"), a partner proposed **Carlyle** — after the Carlyle Hotel, where a friend who co-ran Lazard Frères lived. It worked because it was two syllables, easy to pronounce, and "sounded upper class, British, and maybe gave us more credibility than we deserved." Staying in Washington was itself a positioning trade, guided by Everett Dirksen's line, "when you're getting kicked out of town, get out in front and pretend you're leading a parade": they marketed themselves as the firm that understands heavily regulated, government-affected industries (telecom, healthcare, communications) better than New York rivals — "it may have been true. It may not have been true. But we began to use that as a marketing device." He also lacked the New York investment-banker credentials PE required there. Context he supplies: when Carlyle started there were perhaps **150 private equity/venture firms in the world; today roughly 10,000**, and PE-type assets are probably 30%-plus of institutional portfolios.### Trade 2 (worst): Amazon — 20% offered, $100,000 a year takenCarlyle owned **Baker & Taylor**, then the second-largest book distributor in the United States. **Jeff Bezos**, launching an internet bookseller, needed a bibliography of titles and came to Carlyle offering **20 to 30 percent of the startup** in exchange. Carlyle's salesman answered: "We don't take stock in startup companies. We want cash." The negotiated result: **$100,000 a year for five years**. Rubenstein hadn't known of the offer at the time; when he found out, he flew out to Bezos and asked for the 20–30% retroactively. Bezos: "David, that was a couple of years ago. I don't need you quite as much now. But let's do this — I'll give you 1% of the company." Which he did. And when Amazon went public, **Carlyle sold the stock immediately** — "we had no confidence that his company was going to survive."He absolves them slightly with context: Amazon listed alongside a wave of dot-coms of which "almost every single one went under"; Bezos was essentially the only one who survived with consequence, and the stock at one point fell to about **$6 a share** and went sideways for years. The eventual profits, he notes, came not from selling books but from **AWS**, the cloud business built by **Andy Jassy** — "that wasn't even on the horizon when I was talking to Jeff." Why does the story still sting? He tells it deliberately, as self-deprecation: "I'm not a fisherman, but fishermen like to talk about the ones that got away... it's a way of saying, see, I'm not that smart. Look at the mistakes I made" — the alternative to bragging. The behavioral fix: he set up a **family investment office** that does take early-stage risk, on the theory that "every young company isn't going to be Amazon or Apple or Google, but hope springs eternal," and venture stakes need little capital — "prayer kind of companies." Bezos's own family office has, he notes, invested modestly in some Carlyle funds; they have kidded about it.### Trade 3 (best): globalizing and multi-productizing CarlyleThe deliberate build that made Carlyle huge had two novel moves. First, **multiple funds at once** — a buyout fund plus growth capital/venture plus real estate — which no large PE firm had done; the model was Fidelity and Vanguard's many-product approach applied to private equity. Second, **globalization**: no PE firm had built a global presence, so Carlyle set up funds in Europe, Asia, Japan, Latin America, Africa and the Middle East. His division of labor: "I was reasonably good at recruiting people and raising money; my partners were good at overseeing the investments." Results were mixed by geography — big winners in European, Japanese and Asian buyouts; failures in **Africa** (too few deals), the **Middle East** (few opportunities) and **Latin America** (currencies moved against them). The fund structure itself was the risk-management device: each strategy sits in its own dedicated fund, so "if it doesn't work out, it doesn't necessarily ruin the rest of the business, though your reputation can be hurt."Leadership lessons from the build-out: success bred permission for bigger risks ("if you build out a successful European business, you think you can build out a successful Asian one"), sharper articulation, and better recruiting. His criteria for the people who run funds: **hungry, smart, hardworking, share the credit, take the blame**, deep knowledge of their area, a thirst for more knowledge, and the ability to work with others — "investing in the private equity world is a team sport. There are no solitary geniuses."### Trade 4 (worst): Facebook — the college dating serviceThe channel was family: his daughter, a Harvard sophomore, was dating a young man (now her husband) whose Phillips Exeter and Harvard classmate "had really good computer skills" and was starting a company — pitched to Rubenstein as **a dating service to help Harvard students get dates, with the big expansion plan being... other universities**. Having "seen many college dating services come and go," he passed when the chance came through his son-in-law. He dates his realization to **May 2012**, when Carlyle went public the same week Facebook did and Facebook's market cap was "many, many times higher than ours." He has since met Zuckerberg — they sit together on the advisory board of Tsinghua University's School of Economics and Management — and the questions he'd ask him: when did you realize this was not going to be a college dating service; how do you live with the fame and wealth; what about the metaverse billions with no revenue yet.### Principles and advice- **Nobody can reliably pick winners:** "If I knew the answer" to distinguishing a Bezos or Zuckerberg from a Sam Bankman-Fried, "I would be very, very happy." Even **Sequoia** put roughly **$150 million into FTX's final round** — "they were fooled as well." The toolkit is experience, gut, and due diligence. "If you say you haven't made mistakes in investing, then you really haven't been doing much investing."- **Make your own luck:** meet people, listen to opportunities, take risks; his co-founders were all recruited "by happenstance."- **Age and entrepreneurship:** the 28–37 window is real enough that he acts on it; ignorance of difficulty is a founder's asset.- **Hard work remains "the coin of the realm":** hard workers outdo non-workers, smart outdoes not-smart, and the combination wins; nobody raises money admitting they work nine-to-five and plan to cut to three days a week.- **Reputation as balance sheet:** responsibility to clients (do what you said), employees, suppliers and portfolio companies; run a firm people can be proud to associate with — the credibility has been "helpful to us."- **Motivation at 73:** be useful — "justify your presence on the face of the earth"; first his parents' pride, now his children's and grandchildren's. He is "sprinting to the finish line," living each day as his last, reading the obituary pages daily, and joking that his exercise program is osmosis from walking past the gym equipment.
