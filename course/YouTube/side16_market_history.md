## Part 2: YouTube Script

**VIDEO TITLE:** A Hundred Years of Market History — Every Bear, Every Recovery, in Sixteen Minutes | Side Lesson 16

**RUNTIME TARGET:** ~16 minutes

**HOSTS:**
- **Horace** (teacher): Career investor, lived through 1987, 2000, 2008, 2020, 2022.
- **Stella** (student): Risk-aware retail investor, has only invested in a bull market.

---

**[INTRO SEQUENCE]**

[VISUAL: Animated logo "Side Lesson 16 — A Hundred Years of Market History"]

[VISUAL: image/side16_century_drawdowns.png — full screen.]

**Horace:** This is the chart that runs the lesson. S&P 500 drawdown — the percentage below the prior peak — every day since 1928 to last month. The big trench on the left is 1929. The other trenches, in order, are 1937, 1968, 1973, 1987, 2000, 2007, 2020, 2022. Eight bears of twenty per cent or worse in ninety-eight years.

**Stella:** And the line always comes back to zero.

**Horace:** Always. That is the most important sentence in the entire tutorial — and also the most misleading, because the *speed* with which it comes back is the actual variable, and the speed varies by an order of magnitude. 1929 took twenty-five years on a total-return basis. 2020 took five months. Same word, "recovery," doing very different work.

---

**[SEGMENT 1: THE EIGHT BEARS]**

[VISUAL: image/side16_century_drawdowns.png with each bear annotated.]

**Horace:** Let me walk through the eight bears one by one. Each one is a different *kind* of stress test, and the four-tranche portfolio is supposed to handle each one differently.

**Stella:** Start with the worst.

**Horace:** Nineteen-twenty-nine. The Dow peaked September third at three-eighty-one. It bottomed July eighth, nineteen-thirty-two at forty-one. Down eighty-nine per cent on the Dow. The S&P composite of the time was down eighty-six. The Dow did not retake nineteen-twenty-nine highs until November nineteen-fifty-four. Twenty-five years.

**Stella:** What broke?

**Horace:** Margin debt. Investors were borrowing up to ninety per cent of their stock purchases. When the market went down, the margin calls cascaded, the banks failed, deposits disappeared because there was no FDIC, the Fed *tightened* into the deflation — that one is the canonical Fed mistake — Smoot-Hawley collapsed international trade, and the whole real economy went with it. Unemployment hit twenty-five per cent.

**Stella:** OK and the next one?

**Horace:** Nineteen-thirty-seven. The Fed prematurely tightened, doubled the reserve requirements, and the recovery rolled over into a second leg. Markets fell forty-nine per cent. Recovered in four years. Nineteen-thirty-seven is the *forgotten* bear, and it is forgotten for a reason — the Fed of 2008 read about it. The reason Bernanke held rates at zero for seven years after the Lehman crisis was that he had read this chart.

[VISUAL: drawdown chart with 1973-74 trough highlighted.]

**Horace:** Nineteen-seventy-three to nineteen-seventy-four. OPEC quadrupled the oil price, Bretton Woods had ended in 1971, inflation was already running, Vietnam was ending, Watergate was on television. The market fell forty-eight per cent. The recovery looks fast — twenty-one months back to the prior peak — but in *real* terms, you didn't recover until 1992. Eighteen years of negative real returns inside a flat nominal index. We covered this in side lesson six on inflation.

**Stella:** So the chart hides what the inflation does to your purchasing power.

**Horace:** Always. Nominal recoveries flatter to deceive in inflation regimes.

[VISUAL: drawdown chart zoomed to October 1987.]

**Horace:** October nineteen-eighty-seven. Down twenty-two-point-six per cent in a single day — the largest one-day percentage decline ever. Computerised portfolio insurance was the trigger. The market sold itself, automated. The next day, Greenspan — two months into his chair — flooded the system with liquidity. The Fed's discount window was open at any size at the lowest rate. The market bottomed within two months and was back to pre-crash levels within twenty months.

**Stella:** That's the Fed put.

**Horace:** First instance of it, yes. Every bear since has had a similar response, scaling up in size. That is the regime.

[VISUAL: drawdown chart with dot-com trough.]

**Horace:** Two-thousand to two-thousand-two. The dot-com bubble unwinding, plus the 2001 recession, plus 9/11. Forty-nine per cent peak to trough on the S&P, eighty per cent on the Nasdaq. Twenty-eight months from peak to bottom — the *slow* bear. The dot-com peak was not retaken until October 2007, and then briefly. This is the bear that taught us that bears are sometimes erosion, not events.

**Stella:** And 2008?

**Horace:** Two-thousand-seven to two-thousand-nine. Fifty-seven per cent peak to trough. The mortgage-credit crisis, Lehman, AIG, the entire shadow-banking system. The Fed responded with ZIRP, QE — buying bonds with newly created money — and TARP. That response is the entire post-2008 monetary regime. Stocks bottomed in March 2009 and did not retake their highs until March 2013 — four years.

[VISUAL: drawdown chart zoomed to March 2020.]

**Horace:** March 2020. The fastest crash in history — thirty-four per cent in twenty-three trading days. And then the *fastest* recovery in history. Five months from the bottom to a new all-time high. The Fed cut rates to zero in two weeks, started buying corporate bonds — they had never done that before — and Congress passed fiscal stimulus of about twelve per cent of GDP. The virus didn't go anywhere; the policy response made it irrelevant for the market.

**Stella:** And 2022?

**Horace:** Twenty-twenty-two is the most interesting bear of the modern era. Down twenty-five per cent on the S&P, recovered in twenty-one months. *But* — and this is the part nobody talks about — long Treasuries fell thirty-one per cent at the same time. The bond sleeve of the 60/40 portfolio fell *with* the equity sleeve, for the first time since the 1970s. A traditional 60/40 had its worst real-return year on record. The Fed was *causing* the bear, not stopping it — they were hiking aggressively to break the post-COVID inflation.

---

**[SEGMENT 2: THE RECOVERY-TIME CHART]**

[VISUAL: image/side16_recovery_times.png — bar chart of months to recovery.]

**Horace:** Now look at the recovery-time chart. Y-axis: months from the bottom to the new high. The 1929 bar is the tallest by a factor of three. 2020 is the shortest — barely visible on the chart. The post-1987 cluster is *much* shorter than the pre-1987 cluster.

**Stella:** Why?

**Horace:** The Greenspan put. The implicit promise that the Fed will respond to severe drawdowns. That promise has been kept across Greenspan, Bernanke, Yellen, and Powell. It is not a law of nature. It is a thirty-eight-year-long policy regime.

**Stella:** And if the regime changes?

**Horace:** Then recovery times revert to their pre-1987 distribution and the standard "stocks for the long run" math takes a haircut. Regimes change — that is the framing. The young investor whose plan assumes 9% per year compounding is implicitly betting that the Fed put continues. Most of the time, that bet pays. Sometimes — Japan from 1989, the US from 1929 — it does not.

---

**[SEGMENT 3: WHAT ENDED EACH BEAR]**

**Horace:** Here is the pattern across all eight bears. The thing that *triggered* the bear is almost never the thing that *ended* it.

**Stella:** Like what?

**Horace:** 1929 was triggered by margin debt. It ended by Roosevelt, the gold devaluation, FDIC, and the 1934 Securities Exchange Act — *fiscal-monetary regime change.* 1973 was triggered by oil. It ended by Volcker breaking inflation in 1981 — eight years later. 1987 was triggered by portfolio insurance and ended by a single Fed liquidity injection. 2008 was triggered by mortgages and ended by ZIRP and QE. 2020 was triggered by a virus and ended by the Fed cutting to zero plus fiscal stimulus. 2022 was triggered *by* the Fed and ended when the market priced in the peak of the hiking cycle.

**Stella:** So the answer is always policy.

**Horace:** A bear ends when *policy stops getting worse*. Not when the cause is fixed; not when valuations are cheap; not when sentiment is washed out. When policy stops getting worse. That is the only signal that has worked across all eight bears.

---

**[SEGMENT 4: WHAT YOU SHOULD HAVE DONE]**

**Stella:** And what should an investor have done in each of these?

**Horace:** Almost always — *nothing*. Or specifically: keep dollar-cost-averaging, don't sell, don't lever up, rebalance opportunistically when the drawdown crosses obvious psychological thresholds.

**Stella:** That feels like cheating.

**Horace:** It feels like cheating because it is so hard to do. Look at JP Morgan's analysis of the 1990 to 2024 window. An investor who stayed fully invested compounded at about ten per cent per year. An investor who missed just the *ten best days* of the entire window compounded at half that. And six of those ten best days happened *within fifteen trading days of the worst ten days*. You cannot have one without the other.

**Stella:** So the cost of trying to dodge the bear is missing the recovery.

**Horace:** Exactly. The *moment* you believe you have correctly identified the bottom, you have already sold near the bottom. The arithmetic of compounding is unforgiving on missed days.

**[SEGMENT 5: THE INTERACTIVE]**

[VISUAL: cut to interactive panel `interactive/side16_history_explorer.html`.]

The chart up top tells you where you are in the historical distribution. The interactive tells you what the playbook is for the analogous era. Neither one tells you when the bottom is. Nothing does.

---

**[SEGMENT 6: THE JAPAN ASTERISK]**

**Stella:** What about Japan?

**Horace:** Japan is the live counter-example to everything I've said for the last fourteen minutes. The Nikkei peaked at thirty-eight thousand nine-hundred-fifteen in December 1989. It did not retake that level until February twenty-twenty-four. Thirty-five years. An entire investing career, peak to peak.

**Stella:** Why didn't the playbook work?

**Horace:** Three reasons. Banking crisis that was not resolved promptly — zombie banks left functioning into the late 1990s. Demographic decline — the working-age population peaked in 1995 and has fallen ever since. And a deflationary regime, where the Bank of Japan tightened too soon repeatedly, very 1937-style. Combine the three and you get thirty-five years of stuck.

**Stella:** Could the US become Japan?

**Horace:** Mechanically, yes. Probabilistically, no — the US does not currently have a frozen banking system or a falling working-age population. But "this time is different" runs both ways. The US-only-investable thesis is a *bet* on the continuation of the conditions that have made the US recovery story hold. It is not a guarantee. Knowing the difference is what this lesson is for.

---

**[OUTRO]**

[VISUAL: drawdown chart and recovery-time chart side by side.]

**Horace:** Three takeaways.

One: every US bear of the past hundred years has fully recovered, in dollars, on a total-return basis. The depths varied; the durations varied by an order of magnitude. The pattern held.

Two: the *speed* of the recovery is the variable that maps to your retirement plan. Pre-1987, recoveries took years to decades. Post-1987, they take months to a couple of years. The dividing line is the Fed put, which is itself a regime, not a law.

Three: a bear ends when policy stops getting worse. Not when the cause is fixed; not when valuations are cheap. Watch the policy second derivative.

**Stella:** And what do I actually *do* during a bear?

**Horace:** Keep contributing on schedule. Rebalance opportunistically at the obvious thresholds — minus twenty, minus thirty, minus forty. Have two years of cash so you do not have to sell at the bottom. Do not try to time the bottom; nobody does it twice. And every single time the market is down ten per cent and you feel scared, look at the drawdown chart and remind yourself that ten per cent happens roughly once a year, twenty per cent every three to five, forty per cent every fifteen. You are inside a known distribution. Calibrate.
