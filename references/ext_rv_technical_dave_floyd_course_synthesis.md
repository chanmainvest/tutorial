## About the Instructor and the Course

Dave Floyd is a short-term technical trader and the founder of Aspen Trading Group. He has traded for over two decades — he left a bank job with a good salary and benefits in 1995 to trade his own account — and describes his style as sitting "at the opposite end of the investment world": he focuses on a handful of markets only (S&P 500 futures, 10-year note futures, and a handful of currency pairs), and his trades last anywhere from five minutes to five hours to five days to a week or two. He started as a day trader for roughly ten years, migrated into swing trading, and in recent years runs a combination of day trading (mainly S&P futures) and swing trading (mainly FX and 10-year notes). He is explicit that he does not trade the big macro picture: technical factors drive everything.

The course is a sequential build of a complete short-term trading method across nine modules: (1) series intro, (2) "Meet Dave" career/psychology, (3) what type of trader you are, (4) trend identification, (5) stochastics as a momentum filter, (6) support and resistance levels, (7) a chart walkthrough applying those levels, (8) trade setups and scenario analysis, and (9) position sizing. Floyd's own framing of the arc: *"Until we do the last module, which is on position sizing, none of the other stuff really matters... Without it, you don't have a prayer of becoming a successful trader."*

The method stack, in the order he teaches it, is:

1. **Trader type / time frame** — pick the style that matches your temperament.
2. **Trend identification** — always trade with the trend in your time frame, or don't trade.
3. **Stochastics** — a momentum filter that stops you entering at the worst moment.
4. **Support and resistance levels** — objectively calculated price levels where the market has left "footprints."
5. **Setups / scenario analysis** — combining all three into 4–6 specific setups looked for each day.
6. **Position sizing** — sizing every trade off the stop loss and a fixed portfolio risk percentage.

All chart examples in the course are from February 2022 (a volatile period around Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which began February 23, 2022); Floyd recorded the setup module on Friday, February 25, 2022.

## Part 1 — What Type of Trader Are You?

### 1.1 The baseball analogy

Deciding to "become a trader" is like saying "I'd love to become a baseball player." The real question is *what position*: catcher, center fielder, third baseman? Each has different roles, different practice routines, and different pre- and post-game analysis. Trading is the same — before anything else you must decide **what type of trader you want to be**.

### 1.2 The three trader types and their time-frame stacks

| Type | Holding period | Framing chart | Execution charts | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Day trader** | In and out within the day; normally flat (in cash) each night | 15-minute | 5-minute, ideally 1-minute | A full-time endeavor — you cannot duck in and out during your workday. Must be far more precise with entries/exits because the moves are smaller; being late or early has an outsized adverse effect. |
| **Swing trader** | Several days to a week or two | Daily | 240-minute (4-hour), 60-minute | Can be done part-time: do the analysis in the evening, place orders during the workday. Fewer positions at any given time. |
| **Position trader / investor** | Many weeks, months, even years | Quarterly / monthly / weekly | Daily | The classic investor — e.g., buying large-cap growth and letting the P/E multiple expand, selling when deemed necessary. |

The **universal logic of the time-frame stack**: use the highest time frame to frame *where you are contextually*, then work back down the stack to find the optimal entry point within current price action.

### 1.3 Temperament decides — the core self-assessment

Trader types differ mainly on **temperament and risk tolerance**. Floyd's rule: *if there is a mismatch between your temperament and the time frame you trade, it will create problems and sabotage your success.* Before choosing, ask: what time frame suits my temperament? If you like being in and out and lack patience to let ideas play out, go where your temperament is.

His self-portrait as an example: he has "the patience of a saint" *waiting for trades to set up*, but is impatient *waiting for trade outcomes* — he likes to get in, get validated, and get out fairly quickly. That is why he landed on short time frames.

You are not imprisoned by one style — as you become proficient you can run shorter-term and medium-term trades together and adjust with what the market requires. But the default must fit who you are. Two extra tests from his "Meet Dave" material:

- **Time horizon must be explicit.** *"If you don't know the time horizon that you're going to be implementing a trade, you're ultimately going to screw that trade up."* When someone asks "what do you think of XYZ?", that is a lazy question — the real question is "what do you think of XYZ over the next three months?" Only then do you have something to work with.
- **Match time horizon to personality and risk tolerance, then don't deviate.** Floyd likes immediate-gratification trades and is uncomfortable saying "I think the dollar will be at X in five years." Wherever your range is, stay in it.

### 1.4 Specialize in a handful of markets

Regardless of the time frame chosen: **pick a few markets and trade those exclusively.** Floyd caps it at about five instruments — *"anything more than five instruments or so, basically there's a diminishing rate of return on my time spent."* The objection "a chart is a chart, a head and shoulders is a head and shoulders" is only partly right: the **edge is in the nuance and minutiae** — the repeatable patterns specific to one market that may not work in another. Become an expert in a handful of securities and trade them religiously day to day. You must "get intimate with the price action and get into the swing and the groove of how that instrument trades."

His market-chemistry observation: FX and 10-year notes are more trending and slower minute-to-minute; a 1-minute chart of S&P futures is constant price action — nimble in-and-out trading suits it.

### 1.5 Floyd's own path

1995: left the bank, day-traded for ten years. Then migrated to swing trading. In the last few years, dipped back into day trading. Current mix: day trading S&P futures plus a fair amount of swing trading in FX and 10-year notes.

## Part 2 — Trend Identification

### 2.1 The foundational rule

**It is imperative that you always trade in the direction of the trend in the time frame you are trading** — buying pullbacks within an uptrend, selling rallies within a downtrend, or *not trading at all* if there is no trend. Aligning with the trend gives the higher-probability setup because you are "in harmony with the market."

Counter-trend trading exists but is an advanced concept, deliberately not taught here. The win-rate math he gives:

- A good **with-trend** trader is right about **5 to 6, maybe 6.5 times out of 10**.
- A **counter-trend** trader is right about **1 to 2, maybe 2.5 times out of 10** — because they are trying to call turning points.

### 2.2 "Observe, not perceive"

Floyd's recurring mantra: **observe, not perceive**. Calling turning points means *perceiving* what might happen; trading with the trend means *observing* what is happening and concluding "it seems logical that this continues." When price charts are put in front of a group, people disagree about the trend — because they are perceiving what they want to happen. Show a simple line to a kindergartner and they always get the direction right. *"Show up and leave the ego at the door."*

He illustrates with Annie Duke's book *Thinking in Bets* (about poker, but a perfect analogy for traders): at some point at the table you have to say, "I've got all the information I can possibly have. I think I have an edge. Let's see how it plays out." In trading you can never check every box — you can only say "this looks like a pretty darn good setup," place the trade, and see how it plays out.

### 2.3 The three trend states

1. **Uptrend** — prices moving upward and to the right. Be a buyer of pullbacks.
2. **Downtrend** — prices moving downward and to the right. Be a seller of rallies.
3. **No trend / non-trending (sideways)** — **avoid it like the plague.** There is no built-in edge: the market is in indecision, participants cannot agree on direction. Sideways price action is a clear signal to do one simple thing: *not trade*. Fading the range looks easy in retrospect and never is in real time.

### 2.4 The 20-period moving average (training wheels)

Floyd's trend filter for anyone who cannot read trend at a glance: draw a **20-period moving average** (the average of the previous 20 bars, whatever the bar size).

- **Uptrend:** the MA slopes upward and price is above it.
- **Downtrend:** the MA slopes downward and price is below it.
- Simple vs. exponential "won't make any difference whatsoever."

He no longer personally uses moving averages — after 23+ years his eye is trained to see trend directly — but he recommends keeping the 20-period MA until you can classify trend instantly.

### 2.5 Be second on board

Most of the time you must **wait for the trend to establish itself and then latch on**. Traders feel they "missed out" if they don't catch the ultimate low or high — that is simply false. Give yourself permission to be second: when a trend changes there is enough travel left that you can board on the first stall/pullback and still get the higher-probability trades. Two mental-game corollaries:

- A **higher win-loss ratio puts you in a better mental framework**. If you get chopped up in a sideways mess, when the real break finally comes you are so beaten down you don't take it — "and of course, that's the one that goes."
- **The head game separates winners and losers.** You must understand the technical foundations, but you also have to show up in a good headspace — and trading with the trend is how you get there.

### 2.6 What trends look like on real charts (his examples)

- **NZD/USD, 2-hour chart (uptrend):** clearly higher after a period of sideways chop. Price whipsawed above/below the MA during the chop — "you're just getting yourself chopped up." Sometimes you won't know you were in chop until a couple of failed attempts; then step back and wait for definitive price action.
- **NZD/USD, 2-hour chart (sideways chop):** MA sloping up in places and down in others, price oscillating on both sides — the market to avoid.
- **S&P 500, 5-minute chart (down-to-up transition):** from about 3,730 the market switched to an uptrend toward almost 3,800. Very few traders catch the exact bottom; realistically you get your "aha" once prices have turned and the MA turns up. The "blue shaded" zones on his chart mark where people *perceive* "this move has gone too far, the market looks tired, I'll short it" — perception, not observation. Every pause on the way up was minor and choppy; every with-trend pullback buy led to flowing, stair-stepping advances where each bar built on the previous high with no overlap. Even the correct counter-trend scalps weren't worth the effort.
- **NZD/USD (uptrend to downtrend transition):** he marked the break with a red arrow in advance of the move. Let the market "whack you over the head" and tell you the trend changed — then you have *permission to sell rallies*. In his example: short around **66.75** (i.e., 0.6675), smooth decline to **65.25** (0.6525) — a **150-pip sell-off over two days** where price never came whipping back to the entry. Trend-aligned trades "seem easier to manage versus ones where you're fighting the market. The market always wins in those cases."

### 2.7 Recap rules

1. What's the trend — up or down? That is the first question, every time.
2. Uptrend → wait for pullbacks toward the upward-sloping MA and buy in and around that area.
3. Downtrend → wait for rallies toward the MA and sell into that area, expecting downtrend resumption.
4. Sideways → do not trade; it is a low-probability situation.

## Part 3 — Stochastics: The Momentum Filter

### 3.1 The problem it solves

The scenario: you've done the analysis, placed the trade, got your fill — and price immediately moves against you ("I just top-ticked or bottom-ticked that market"). It happens to everyone, but one indicator greatly reduces poorly-timed entries: **stochastics**, which Floyd has used for over 23 years.

A stochastic is an **oscillator that measures the momentum of price action** over a period of bars (15 daily bars, 15 one-minute bars, etc.), plotting a value from **0 to 100**. It is not infallible — no indicator is — but its best use is telling you **when you should *not* buy and when you should *not* sell**.

### 3.2 His setting, and why the setting barely matters

Floyd uses a **slow stochastic, 15,3 setting** — a setting he chose years ago and has grown accustomed to. His honest advice: at the end of the day the setting doesn't matter; different settings only change the oscillator's sensitivity (faster/slower). A 12, 14, or 16 would "probably be just fine" as long as it isn't outlandish — but since he continues with students across modules and live sessions, he asks everyone to use **15,3** so the charts match.

### 3.3 Overbought, oversold, and the filtering rule

- **Overbought: reading above 80.** Suggests likelihood of mean reversion is high.
- **Oversold: reading below 20.** Same logic downward.

The filter rules:

- **Do not buy anything when the stochastic is above 80.**
- **Do not go short anything when the stochastic is below 20.**

Walking into the office and shorting a "great downtrend" while the stochastic is pegged oversold is picking the worst spot — the usual next event is a correction higher (or at minimum a stall). Same for buying a breakout with the oscillator pinned overbought: you may be right eventually, but on that time frame you're probably topped out for the time being.

### 3.4 The three benefits of stochastics

1. **Filter (keep you out of low-probability entries).** Never enter at the end of a rally or the end of a sell-off. The oscillator gives a quick visual of where you most likely are in momentum.
2. **Entry timing (when to get back in).** Once the stochastic comes *off* the extreme — it does **not** have to travel all the way to the opposite extreme — momentum has "reset," energy has rebuilt, and you get a window to re-enter with the trend. Buying the pullback when the stochastic resets beats buying when it's pinned: entering and being immediately underwater makes you doubt yourself ("oh crap, am I right? Did I make a mistake?"); entering after the reset means price usually doesn't go far against you.
3. **Divergence (heads-up on trend changes + trade management).** See below.

### 3.5 Stochastic divergence

- **Bullish divergence:** price makes a **new low**, but the stochastic makes a **higher low** (e.g., prior reading 25, next low only ~12–15). Downside momentum is waning — the first hint a labored decline may reverse.
- **Bearish divergence:** price makes a **new high**, but the stochastic reading is lower (e.g., 70 on the prior peak, 60–65 on the new high). Upward momentum is flagging.

Divergence does *not* mean the trend will reverse — it changes probabilities and behavior:

- If you are short and a bullish divergence develops while you're in profit: get **nimble** — tighten stops, take profits, don't get greedy and go for everything.
- If you see a bearish divergence: be a little less eager to buy the next pullback expecting trend continuation.
- Divergence is an advanced entry tool; at the start, use it for **trade management**.

His S&P 500 30-minute example: clear downtrend, price low from an oversold reading (~8); price rallies to a new low later, but the stochastic reads only ~12–15 — bullish divergence. A "decent-sized rally" followed (roughly 3,780 up to 3,840), price fell to fresh lows (a short from ~3,800 down toward 3,720 had worked), but with each successive new price low the stochastic printed higher — energy dissipating — and the market eventually meandered higher. Lesson: if you're short and divergences keep appearing at lower prices, "you better start really, really tightening up your stop losses or taking profits."

### 3.6 The known failure mode

**Oscillators can stay pegged.** His NZD example: the stochastic remained oversold for **over two days while prices kept falling**. If you refused to short the whole way down because "it's oversold," you missed the entire move. Overbought can become more overbought; oversold more oversold. His (admittedly un-rigorous) estimate: about **8 times out of 10** an extreme resets rather than staying pegged — but the 2-out-of-10 cases will happen and you must be prepared for them.

## Part 4 — Support and Resistance Levels: The Objective Framework

### 4.1 Why generic support/resistance isn't enough

Everyone knows support and resistance: previous highs and lows, yearly highs and lows, yesterday's low. Floyd's critique: those are **far too generic** — an obvious reference point at best. Worse, they are **subjective**: "that was a significant swing high" is what *you* (or someone) thinks matters. An all-time high/low is undisputed; but "was yesterday's low at 10:00 AM really that key? I don't know. It depends what the rest of the market thinks about it."

His deeper critique of classical technical analysis (which he otherwise believes in): even excellent TA "didn't matter very much unless the market, in general, saw that same pattern... and decided to act upon it." Even trendlines drawn strictly "by the rules" are subjective — *which* swing highs and lows did you pick? The question that matters: **does everybody else also look at that same level?**

### 4.2 The footprint concept

His alternative: levels that represent a **footprint** — price levels where there was a lot of trading activity (volume, trade frequency, time frequency) at a particular price, whether an hour, a day, a week, or a year ago. Price levels are **the most objective measure in the marketplace** — "like a breadcrumb trail": this is where prices traded, this is the volume, this is the trade frequency, this is the time frequency. Those are objective data points that can be mapped — and the levels the market, by and large, recognizes are very often where **inflection points** form.

His levels are **mathematically calculated from volume, price, frequency, and time** (the precise formula is proprietary/not taught in this course; he has them pre-calculated and plotted on his charts). What the course teaches is how to *use* them.

### 4.3 Level mechanics

- **Defended levels:** participants who traded at a level tend to *protect* it on retest. His $10 example: buyers bought at $10, price went to $12, price returns to $10 — those buyers defend their thesis and the level; the defense is often the **catalyst for resumption** of the prior trend.
- **Broken levels signal trend change.** When a level breaks — and levels inevitably break — that indicates a change of tone/trend and can be a very powerful observation.
- **Role reversal:** broken support becomes resistance and vice versa. His client shorthand: **"a support level is support until proven otherwise."** Which role a line plays depends on which side of it price currently sits.

### 4.4 The four scenarios with one support/resistance channel

With a single channel (upper resistance, lower support) there are exactly four scenarios to prepare for:

1. **Prices rally into resistance** → short there.
2. **Prices sell off into support (then rally)** → long there.
3. **Prices break above, retest the underside, then move higher** → buy the retest; expect upside expansion.
4. **Prices break below, retest the top side, then move lower** → sell the retest; expect downside expansion.

The trades he finds most appealing are **the fringes, not the middle**: *"I'm not necessarily interested in trading the channel. I'm interested in trading the fringes of the channel"* — where price accelerates after a break, because once a range breaks, prices get **more expansionary** (the tight cycle is broken).

### 4.5 Levels complete the stack — but never stand alone

Three tools now stack: **trend identification + stochastics + objective levels**. Buying merely because "the stochastic has pulled back and price dropped $0.30" is perceiving — a forecast with at best 50-50 odds. But pullback + stochastic reset **at an objective support level** puts you in the observation business. Conversely: never go short just because price touches resistance, or long just because it touches support — context (where we are relative to where we've been, and the trend) decides which levels are *key right now*. The same level that matters at 2 PM may have had no relevance at 10 AM. Using levels as an arbitrary standalone trigger "will end up getting you really chopped up."

Removing subjectivity matters because **subjectivity is skewed by emotion** — an objective framework keeps you executing at your highest level.

### 4.6 The four color-coded channel sets

Floyd runs **four sets of channels simultaneously**, each built on a different look-back magnitude, so the same system serves an intraday scalper through a long-term portfolio manager. All are significant, but they are ranked in a hierarchy and differ in how often price interacts with them:

| Band | Look-back | Who leans on it | Interaction frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Green** | Shortest | Day traders | Constant throughout the day, until the period readjusts |
| **Orange** | Next | Swing traders | Regularly, week in / week out |
| **Red** | Longer (wider range) | Swing/position traders | Less often; price must travel into its vicinity |
| **Purple** | Longest (big chunk of data) | Long-term investors / portfolio managers | Rare; marks long-term inflection points |

Portfolio-manager use case: if your valuation work says buy a stock, *still* check where price sits relative to the purple band — if price is arriving at a purple support level, "you can feel pretty confident that prices are going to respect that level and probably move higher from there."

## Part 5 — The Levels in Action: Chart Walkthroughs

(All examples are real price action from February 2022, shown on charts where the levels were drawn *in advance* — he stresses: no magic "draw the levels after the fact because they conveniently fit.")

### 5.1 S&P 500, 15-minute chart, mid-February 2022 — green band 4,367 / 4,453

- **Fade at the key level (against the trend, deliberately):** a sharp sell-off into the green lower bound at **4,367** with the stochastic oversold — while the trend was down. His answer to "Dave, why get long when the trend is down?": *"There are rules and then there are times to break your rules."* An objective level the whole market recognizes is where you get the most robust reaction — you're catching the crowd leaning too far one side. Price bottomed at **4,354** (12–13 points through the band, not a magic exact tag), chopped, then ripped from ~**4,380** to ~**4,410** in about half an hour.
- **Trade management lesson:** you could hold for the traverse to the upper band, but letting 20–30 points of S&P profit evaporate back to break-even is **bad trade management** — "I don't know too many... shorter term traders who grabbed 20 points in the S&Ps and let that 20 points evaporate... pick your spots, take a piece of the move, and then move on," and re-engage at the next level.
- The upper band was respected repeatedly; a few attempts below/above it stopped people out before a "picture-perfect" run: a buyer at ~**4,370** was rewarded "easily, no sweat" up to ~**4,410** — about **40 S&P points**. If you were short and knew the level, you knew to start reeling the trade in as price approached it.
- **Later in February — the orange band:** price tagged the lower orange band with the stochastic oversold ("oversold, oversold, oversold") after an almost 100-point drop from 4,354 to ~4,260. His "**speed of the tape**" reasoning: the market just moved 100 points — does it really have another 100 in the tank? Usually not; the market **ebbs and flows** and the levels get you in rhythm with that. The orange level was tested five times, finally broke, was retested from below ("squirted below, ripped back above"), and by the Friday Feb 25 close, **4,263** was the key level price ripped from — "you buy that pullback" and price gravitated right up to the upper resistance band.

### 5.2 ARKK — all four bands, and the view from the portfolio manager's chair

The bands on ARKK at the time: **purple 131 → 103; red 115 → ~89 (89.60); orange 89 → 70; green 74 → 67.7** — and on Friday, February 25, 2022, ARKK closed **right on the lower green band** (~67.6–67.8; he cites the close at 67.82 against a 67.74 band).

- **The January 2022 call:** *"We get below $89.60 — it's lights out. We're going to $70."* It took about a month, and it went to $70, because the level kept being tested and once it broke, "we're done."
- **The purple-band verdict:** if you're a portfolio manager questioning why you're still long ARKK given its holdings — "there was no reason at all for you to be in this stock below $103." And once ARKK **failed to get back above $131** (it broke above in January 2021, rallied to the all-time high, fell back below, and its best attempt failed), "bloom's off the rose."
- **The philosophy:** your analysis of the underlying companies may be spot on — but that's *your* view, not the market's. *"Price action is how you get paid in the market... I don't believe in what I think... I want to know what the market thinks, and the only way to know what the market thinks is to know the price levels where the market's going to be focusing on. And how it reacts to those levels tells you all you need to know."*
- **The Monday playbook (his Feb 28 plan):** short-term bullish. **Above 67.77 → long. Fails at 67.77 → short.** If price can reclaim and hold the ~67.7 green band, the likely path is **$74** (a ~7-point move — "that's a great swing trade"), and if the market turns "uber-bullish" and $74 is reclaimed and held, the next stop is a **retest of $89**. "That's just the way these support and resistance levels work."
- **For the investor:** a reclaim and hold of the green band might be the *first sign* the selling is over — "maybe you're starting to nibble there... prices continue to move higher, you start building your position. But why go all in right here because you, quote unquote, think it's a low? None of us know." Levels respected and held are the market telling you something — then you deploy capital with confidence.

### 5.3 USD/JPY — trading the levels, not the opinion

From his Friday Feb 25, 2022 screens: he had been long USD/JPY from **109** and exited around the purple level at **112.07** — "how did I know to get out there? Because it was the level at the time" (price pulled back there, later pushed through). USD/JPY had held the red level at **114.43**, and price was coiled below the upper green band at **115.62** — "on the verge of breaking higher." His scenarios: **above 115.62 with a retest → buy, breakout mode**; or the range persists and price moves back lower. "I'm a bull above here. I'm a bear if we can't break below it," leaning bullish because the overall trend is higher. No Bank of Japan opinion, no fundamentals — "it's all based on hard technical levels."

### 5.4 10-year notes — the Ukraine invasion trade (February 23, 2022)

Floyd was at his screens at 5–6 PM West Coast time, fresh off a client call to Australia, watching invasion headlines cross his Bloomberg terminal. Reasoning: S&P selling off = risk-off = **10-year notes are the safe-haven play**. Conviction added by the levels: price was **holding right above the known level at 126.12**, with stochastics pushing higher (not overbought). He got long around **126.13**. His risk was defined and tiny — a stop at 126.10, "I lose six ticks, whoopie-do" — and he knew his upside target *before entry* because **127 was a known level when he put the trade on**. The market ran almost to 127.10; he exited at **127.10** — "a hell of a move" — after which notes gave the entire move back over the rest of the week.

- **Weekend insurance:** he went long into Friday's close at 126.12 as an insurance trade — "if things go squirrelly over the weekend, 10-year notes will open higher."
- **Monday scenarios:** price was back *below* 126.12 (a less-bullish checkmark — the breakout fully failed). Two scenarios only: **(a)** bad Ukraine developments → notes rip; lean on 126.12 heavily and be long; **(b)** neutral/good news, can't get above 126.12 → *"I'm shorting that level, no doubt about it."*

### 5.5 The 20-second morning routine

*"I come in the morning. I'm like: where are the levels? Is support holding? Is resistance holding? Where are we relative to the current price action? And what has happened with the longer-term and shorter-term price levels?"* — about 20 seconds of orientation, replacing hours of technical analysis that "the market doesn't care about." The supplement is always the **tape**: is it heavy, bullish, sluggish, fast? Plus the cross-market context (S&P, NASDAQ, bonds, geopolitics).

## Part 6 — Trade Setups Worth Waiting For: Daily Scenario Analysis

### 6.1 The discipline

On any given day Floyd looks for **four to six specific trade setups**, all built from the stack: trend + stochastics + levels (he also says Fibonacci is "introduced into this particular module," though the module itself works entirely from the levels — no Fib ratios are actually taught in the source material). Not every day produces them: some days zero, some days one or two, some days four to six. **If there is no setup, there is nothing to do.**

The method he demonstrates is **pre-market scenario analysis**: on a Friday afternoon (markets closed, no live price action to bias him), he writes out every scenario for Sunday night's futures open / Monday morning, so that whatever unfolds, "you're ready for them."

### 6.2 S&P 500 scenario board (recorded Friday, Feb 25, 2022)

Context: S&P closed Friday at **4,380** after a very strong day; it had wrestled with the **4,367** green-band level all day (a known level, heavy historical activity) and had rallied from **4,263** (also a known level). His scenarios for Monday:

1. **Base case, long:** trend is clearly up and price is back above the lower green band (4,367) — expect a traverse toward the upper band. But his entry style: **he does not buy breakouts.** *"If we open and prices immediately start to go higher, I probably won't be on board."* He wants: prices to pull back, the stochastic to come off overbought (not necessarily to 20), and **4,367 to hold** with the tape feeling firm — then he's long. Stop: **not much below 4,367** — "I don't need to give it a whole lot of room."
2. **Failed level, short:** prices open lower and break back below 4,367 → **sell the retest, get short**, targeting **4,263**. This is the level taking **precedence over the trend** — and precisely because he'd be fighting the trend, he won't give it room: "if I get short and we really start to rally hard, I'm gone."
3. **Mid-range pullback long (acceptable, less robust):** prices rip, pull back mid-channel, stochastic resets and turns up — yes, he'd consider it, but "you're just in the middle of nothing. There are no levels that I would deem critical." Any pullback in an uptrend is "fair game," just not the favored outcome. He wants entries **into levels** and exits **into levels**.
4. **Short the upper band:** prices rally ~100 points into the upper green band (**4,453**) → *"Am I getting short here? You're damn right I am... even though it's scary as hell, you have to be a seller there."* Stop-and-out quickly if the market keeps going.
5. **Failed short → flip the view (not chase):** short at the band, price farts around and pushes through → instantly flip your *view* to bullish — the market is stronger than the level. But **never chase**: buy only the pullback, and expect the pullback to fall short of the exact level ("maybe it only comes back to 4,460... trade the areas, not the levels").
6. **Capitulation long:** price crashes through 4,263, extends, stalls → **"You're damn right, I'm getting long"** — the tone has changed after the break; the move back may not reach the whole range but the trade is there, with the retest-failure scenario (break, retest, go lower) as its mirror.

The scenarios are the same for any instrument; the market reacts to the levels and **how it reacts determines what you do**. Default stance: **buy support, sell resistance, until proven otherwise.**

### 6.3 ARKK scenario board (same date)

ARKK is in a downtrend, but: the stochastic is oversold after a strong bullish move, and Friday closed right on the lower green band (67.74; close 67.82). Two scenarios:

- **Favored — counter-trend long at the level:** open Monday, rally toward **$70** (lower orange band), pull back toward **67.74** → **long**, with oversold-stochastic momentum at his back, targeting **$74**. Risk definition: "if we start to move back below 67.74, guess what? I'm long, I'm out" — risking **1–2 points to make 15, 20, maybe more**. He takes this even though the trend is down, because **the levels take precedence**, recent price action is bullish, and after an extended trend the market mean-reverts — *"like a rubber band. You can only stretch it so far and then eventually it has to snap back."*
- **Lesser — short the rally:** the mechanical downtrend play (short any rally). He downweights it given S&P/NASDAQ strength and the strength of Friday's move, but if it plays out, he knows how to play it.

### 6.4 USD/JPY and 10-year notes

As walked through in Part 5: USD/JPY — buy the retest above 115.62, or range-persist lower; bull above / bear if it can't break. 10-year notes — long on bad-news strength through 126.12, or short the failure at 126.12.

### 6.5 The discipline rules

- **Don't chase.** "One of the biggest mistakes that traders can make. You'll get caught in a trap, and inevitably they're going to be really poor trading choices."
- **If you miss a trade, move on.** You'll miss them — you weren't at the screens, or the setup didn't feel right. You'll develop a feel; sometimes a setup that looked good on paper "really wasn't good in reality."

## Part 7 — Position Sizing

### 7.1 Why it trumps everything

Even with robust setups and a high win rate you can underperform if you skip this. **Position sizing = adjusting trade size on each trade based on your stop loss and the risk profile you set in advance.** Two payoffs: (1) winning trades earn robust returns because exposure per trade is adequate; (2) you avoid a deep drawdown that is brutally hard to dig out of — financially *and* psychologically. Floyd repeats: this is the most overlooked concept in trading, and without it the rest of the course is worthless.

The fixed-lot habit is the target of his critique: "I'm just going to trade 1,000 shares every time... three or four contracts... three or four standard lots" — with no thought given to why.

### 7.2 The drawdown table (why depth matters)

His table is the standard loss-to-recovery arithmetic. The anchor he works through: a **10% loss needs an 11.11% gain** to break even; a 12% loss needs roughly 12–13%+. The deeper the hole, the worse it gets — his "66% or 100%" recovery references correspond to the classic 40%-loss/50%-loss rows (a 50% drawdown needs a 100% gain). Beyond arithmetic, psychology compounds it: down 50%, you're "in a pretty crappy place" and gun shy — you then trade the *next* setup (often the big winner) at half size, guaranteeing you don't recover. **Your job is to manage drawdowns so they are minimal and don't change your behavior.**

### 7.3 Size against your portfolio, not your trading account

His Trader A example: you open a $25,000 FX account (Oanda, forex.com, FxPro — "an adequate starting amount") — but you have a **$500,000 total portfolio** (savings, 401(k)s, retirement accounts, other trading accounts). **The portfolio is the reference point.** Making $10,000 in the $25k account is a tremendous 40% account return — but it doesn't move the needle on $500k. Corollaries: make sure the trading account's size is commensurate with the portfolio, and size risk in portfolio terms — otherwise you shortchange your overall financial picture by focusing on a sliver of your capital.

### 7.4 The risk rule

- Best traders run roughly a **55–65% win rate**; assume ~4 losses out of every 10, and **four losses in a row is entirely possible**.
- At 3% risk per trade, four straight losses = −12%, needing ~12–13%+ to recover. Hence his personal rule: **no single trade should risk more than 2% of the entire investment portfolio — 1% to 2%, your discretion; newer traders maybe 0.5%.** Whatever number you choose, **use it consistently**.
- On the $500k portfolio: $5,000–$10,000 maximum loss per trade. Losing $10,000 on $500k is recoverable; losing $10,000 on a $25,000 account is a disaster.

### 7.5 The arithmetic: size = risk dollars ÷ stop distance

The question that precedes every entry: **"Where's my stop loss?"** Then work backwards. Entering a trade without a stop is a **major no-no**, for two reasons: you need a clear point where the thesis is invalid, and you *cannot size correctly without it*.

- **Microsoft example:** buy at $100, stop at $90 → $10 risk per share. Risk budget $5,000 (1%) → **500 shares**. Risk budget $10,000 (2%) → **1,000 shares**.
- **Tesla example (shorting a failed level):** downtrend, support broken, price retests the old support as resistance at **$666** (actual 666.40). Next resistance above sits at **$671**, above the downward-sloping MA — if price gets back above $671 the short thesis is wrong. So: short at **$666**, stop **$671**, $5 risk/share. At 2% of $500k = $10,000 risk → **maximum 2,000 shares** ($10,000 loss if stopped). If the stop distance were 10 points → only **1,000 shares**.
- **NZD/USD example (FX):** intraday resistance tested at **0.7174** (he says "7174"), stochastics overbought and turning down, downtrend in a pause/consolidation → short at **0.7150** ("7150"), stop back above resistance at **0.7175** ("7175") — a **25-pip stop**. Tight, and deliberately so: "if it goes back above 7175, we know we're wrong." With a $10,000 (2%) risk budget and a 25-pip stop, the position is **$4 million notional** NZD — "not a crazy amount" in FX, and exactly what the math says.

### 7.6 The inverse law and the classic mistake

**Narrow stop → large size; wide stop → small size — identical dollar risk.** The classic error: using the same lot size whether the stop is narrow or wide. "That makes no sense whatsoever." Every trade has a *unique* position size relative to its stop. Conversely, don't arbitrarily de-risk: *"Don't start scaling back your trades because your confidence level is shaken. Maybe just take the day off. But don't place trades if you're not going to be placing them at the maximum position size you've allotted yourself."* Your max loss was decided in advance, when you were calm — honor it.

## Part 8 — Psychology, Process, and Career Lessons

### 8.1 Know your weaknesses, and design around them

*"As a trader, you have to be aware of your weaknesses because we all have them. If you don't know what they are or you're ignoring them, you're going to do it at your own peril."* Floyd's own: **he hates to lose** — "probably like most people but I think I might have it to the nth degree" (he half-jokes that a trader-psychologist like Denise Shull would trace it to childhood). The consequences he manages:

- He **hesitates on entries** waiting for the *perfect* setup, "which of course never exists" — you only ever have so much information, and sometimes you must say "close enough, I'm in." He misses plenty of good trades by overthinking.
- He cuts losses without problem and waits for setups with discipline. His real gap is the other end: **staying in winning trades long enough** — extracting the most from a trade before the thesis is actually invalid. "If I could work on staying in my winning trades longer, that would be wonderful."

### 8.2 The edge decays when you do

Short-term trading produces "death by a thousand cuts" — an accumulation of small wins rather than a few dramatic outcomes — so its danger is quiet: **losing sight of your edge after a good run.** After a hot streak Floyd catches himself letting his guard down, "being a little too cocky." His fix is the professional-athlete analogy: an athlete doing well who stops going to the gym stops performing. **Maintain the daily rigorous process; if you can't maintain that intensity, you shouldn't be trading that day.**

### 8.3 Build a network — his single biggest regret

The one thing he wishes he'd known earlier: **have a deeper network of fellow traders as a sounding board.** Markets always change — "the way you're trading right now is not going to be the way you're trading in three, four, maybe five years." After his first career phase (the early 2000s, transitioning from pure scalping to longer time frames) he had no built-in peer group to ask "what am I doing wrong?" — and estimates the solo trial-and-error cost him **two to three years**. Stick to what you know, but keep mentors and sounding boards around you.

### 8.4 Process temperament

His operating mode after 20+ years: **"rinse and repeat"** — do what works, make adjustments as the market requires, repeat. He is consistent by nature, pragmatic, and willing to take risks *he has a good handle on* (leaving the bank to start the business was one such bet). For decompression: rigorous outdoor activity (mountain biking with his wife, skate skiing, backcountry snowshoeing) and playing music in a band — and he notes traders are "a pretty creative bunch... it's not all science."

### 8.5 The mental game across the modules

- **Headspace decides outcomes:** "At the end of the day, it's what's going on up here that ultimately separates the winners and the losers... you've got to show up in a good headspace" — and trading with the trend is what keeps you there.
- **Observe, not perceive** (Part 2) is a psychological rule as much as a technical one: the ego wants tops and bottoms; the observer takes the second boarding.
- **Entering after a stochastic reset** (Part 3) is partly psychological engineering: a trade that doesn't immediately go against you keeps you rational; an immediate drawdown makes you question the trade.
- **Drawdown psychology** (Part 7): deep drawdowns break people behaviorally — they skip or shrink the next trade, which is statistically the one that pays them back.
- **Scary trades are sometimes required:** shorting a 100-point extended rally into the upper band ("it's scary as hell... you have to be a seller there") — predefined scenarios make that possible without courage-in-the-moment.

## Appendix A — Key Rules and Parameters (Quick Reference)

| # | Rule / parameter | Floyd's specification |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trader types | Day (flat nightly), swing (days–2 weeks), position (weeks–years); choose by **temperament & risk tolerance**; never deviate outside your range |
| 2 | Time-frame stacks | Day: 15m frame → 5m/1m execute. Swing: daily frame → 240m/60m execute. Position: quarterly/monthly/weekly frame → daily execute. Highest frame = context, lowest = execution |
| 3 | Market focus | Max ~5 instruments; know one market's nuances; trade them religiously |
| 4 | Trend filter | Trade only with trend: buy pullbacks / sell rallies. Sideways = **no trade**. With-trend win rate 5–6.5/10; counter-trend 1–2.5/10 |
| 5 | Trend training wheels | **20-period MA** (simple or exponential): slope + price side defines trend |
| 6 | Boarding rule | Wait for trend to establish; be **second on board**; never demand the exact top/bottom |
| 7 | Stochastic | **Slow stochastic 15,3**; scale 0–100; overbought > **80**, oversold < **20** |
| 8 | Stochastic filter | Never buy above 80; never short below 20; wait for reset (need not reach the opposite extreme); ~8/10 extremes reset |
| 9 | Divergence | Price new low + higher stochastic low = bullish; price new high + lower stochastic = bearish → manage trades (tighten stops, take profits), temper trend-continuation enthusiasm |
| 10 | S/R levels | Objective levels from **volume, price, frequency, time** (his pre-calculated sets); support holds *until proven otherwise*; broken level = trend-change signal; broken support = resistance |
| 11 | Four scenarios per channel | Short resistance; long support; buy break+retest up; sell break+retest down. Trade the **fringes/expansion**, not the middle |
| 12 | Band hierarchy | **Green** (short look-back, day trading) → **orange** (swing) → **red** (longer) → **purple** (longest; PM inflections) |
| 13 | Level precedence | A key level **takes precedence over the trend** — fading a major level is allowed ("rules, and times to break your rules") |
| 14 | Entry style | **Do not buy breakouts**; buy pullbacks into levels with stochastic reset and firm tape; **trade the areas, not the levels**; never chase |
| 15 | Stops | Known before entry; placed just beyond the level that proves you wrong (S&P: just under 4,367; Tesla: 5 pts; NZD: 25 pips; ZN: 3 ticks to 126.10) |
| 16 | Position sizing | Size = risk dollars ÷ stop distance; risk **1–2% of total portfolio** per trade (max 2%; new traders ~0.5%); size against the **whole portfolio**, not the trading account; narrow stop → bigger size, wide stop → smaller |
| 17 | Workload | 4–6 setups per day; some days zero — then do nothing; missed trades → move on |
| 18 | Routine | Morning: where are the levels, is support/resistance holding, where are we — ~20 seconds; plus tape feel (heavy/bullish/sluggish/fast) and cross-market context |
